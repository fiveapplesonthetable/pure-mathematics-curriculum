\section{Negation: How to Disagree Precisely}
\chapterline

In Chapter~4, we saw that quantifiers transform mathematical statements into an interactive game. A universal claim ($\forall x$) invites a skeptical adversary to choose the most challenging test case, while an existential claim ($\exists y$) requires you to produce a winning response. But what happens when someone else makes a claim, and \emph{you} are the skeptic? When an author or your own intuition asserts a general statement, what must you establish to prove it wrong?

In ordinary conversation, disagreement can be casual and vague. If someone announces ``Everyone loves summer,'' an everyday response might be ``I don't'' or ``Winter is better.'' In daily life, the intent is clear enough. But everyday language permits subtle ambiguities: does ``everyone doesn't love summer'' mean nobody loves it, or merely that at least one person does not? Figure~\ref{05:fig-summer} separates the two readings. Only the second is the negation of ``Everyone loves summer''; the first is a much stronger claim.

In mathematics, negation is an exact logical operation. The \textbf{negation} of a statement $P$, written $\neg P$ (read ``not $P$''), is the precise statement that is true whenever $P$ is false, and false whenever $P$ is true. A \textbf{counterexample} is a single, concrete object showing that a general claim fails.

You need exact negations constantly. A \emph{proof by contradiction} assumes that the statement you want is false and then derives something impossible; to start it, you must write down exactly what ``false'' means, which is the negation. And to decide whether a proposed counterexample refutes a claim, you must know exactly what the negation of the claim requires.

\begin{historybox}
In the 1910s and 1920s, the Dutch mathematician L.E.J. Brouwer---a pioneer of topology, the mathematical study of shapes that can be stretched or deformed without tearing---triggered one of the fiercest debates in modern mathematics by questioning the meaning of negation on infinite sets. In classical logic (the standard logic used in most of mathematics), every well-formed statement is assumed to be either true or false. This principle is called the \emph{Law of the Excluded Middle}, written $P \lor \neg P$ and read ``$P$ or not $P$.'' Under this rule, proving that a statement cannot possibly be false automatically proves that it is true. In symbols, $\neg \neg P \implies P$: ``not not $P$'' implies $P$.

Brouwer pointed out a problem. On a finite list of numbers, you can in principle inspect every item, so ``no counterexample exists'' can be checked directly. But on an infinite set, showing that ``it is impossible for no solution to exist'' does not hand you the solution. Brouwer founded \emph{intuitionism}, also called constructive mathematics, which holds that you may claim an object exists only if you give an explicit recipe to compute or build it. A proof by double negation, which gives no such recipe, does not count.

David Hilbert, champion of formal axiomatic proofs, objected strongly. To Hilbert, outlawing proof by contradiction and double negation was like ``forbidding the telescope to the astronomer or the use of his fists to the boxer.'' Hilbert declared: ``No one shall expel us from the paradise that Cantor created,'' defending the infinite sets studied by Georg Cantor, the founder of set theory. Hermann Weyl, one of Hilbert's students, briefly joined Brouwer, warning that classical analysis was a house built on sand.

The controversy had lasting results. In 1933, Kurt Gödel used a translation that inserts double negations to show that if constructive arithmetic is free of contradictions, then so is classical arithmetic. Today, Brouwer's constructive ideas are built into computer proof assistants such as Lean, Coq, and Agda, where a constructive proof that something exists is a computer program that returns a witness. A related open question remains in computer science: when classical logic guarantees that some object exists (such as a stable strategy in a game, meaning a choice of moves that no player can improve by changing only their own moves), can a computer always find it quickly, or are some objects guaranteed to exist yet too hard to compute in any reasonable time?
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Negating quantifiers: crossing the boundary}

The central rule of quantifier negation is that \textbf{negation swaps the quantifier and moves inside}:
\begin{align*}
\neg \bigl(\forall x \in D, \, P(x)\bigr) &\iff \exists x \in D \text{ such that } \neg P(x), \\
\neg \bigl(\exists x \in D \text{ such that } P(x)\bigr) &\iff \forall x \in D, \, \neg P(x).
\end{align*}
Here $D$ is the domain of discourse (the set of all objects under discussion, as in Chapter~2), $P(x)$ is a property of $x$, and $\iff$ (``if and only if'', from Chapter~4) means the two sides are true in exactly the same situations.

Why does this flip occur? Picture the domain $D$ as a region. Let $S = \{x \in D : P(x)\}$ be the set of points where property $P$ holds, and let its complement $S^c = \{x \in D : \neg P(x)\}$ be the set of points of $D$ where $P$ fails. Figure~\ref{05:fig-quantifier-negation} shows the two regions.

You must demonstrate that \emph{every} point in $D$ fails to enter $S$, so that $S$ is empty. Figure~\ref{05:fig-existential-negation} shows this situation.

\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose someone claims: ``Every student in the seminar arrived on time.'' What is the exact mathematical negation? Does it say that every student was late? No. It requires only that at least one student was not on time.

\subsection{Negating implications: the broken contract}

Beginners frequently stumble when negating an if-then statement. When asked to negate:
\begin{quote}
``If it rains, then the ground is wet'' ($P \implies Q$),
\end{quote}
an intuitive first guess is often ``If it rains, then the ground is dry.'' But that is a different if-then claim, not the negation. On a day with no rain, both if-then claims count as true (we will see why below), while a statement and its negation can never be true together.

An implication $P \implies Q$ (read ``$P$ implies $Q$'' or ``if $P$, then $Q$'') is a contract between a \textbf{premise} (or hypothesis) $P$ and a \textbf{conclusion} $Q$. It does not claim that $P$ causes $Q$; it only promises:
\begin{center}
\itshape ``Whenever condition $P$ is fulfilled, I promise outcome $Q$ will occur.''
\end{center}
Under what exact circumstance is this contract broken? 
\begin{itemize}
\item If it does not rain, the contract was never tested. No promise has been broken. In logic, this is called \textbf{vacuous truth}: an implication is considered true by default whenever its premise fails, because no breach could possibly have occurred.
\item If it rains and the ground is wet, the contract was honored.
\item The contract is broken \textbf{if and only if} it rains, and yet the ground remains dry.
\end{itemize}

We can visualize the four possible combinations of true and false for $P$ and $Q$ as a contract (Figure~\ref{05:fig-implication-contract}). In the figure, $P \land \neg Q$ means ``$P$ and not $Q$.''

Therefore, the negation of an implication is never an if-then statement. It is a \textbf{conjunction}: an ``and'' statement, written with the symbol $\land$, which is true only when both parts are true:
\[
\neg(P \implies Q) \iff P \land \neg Q.
\]
To disprove $P \implies Q$, you must produce an instance where the hypothesis $P$ is fully satisfied, while the conclusion $Q$ fails.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose you make the pledge: ``If I win the lottery, I will buy you a car.'' In which scenario have you broken your word? If you never win the lottery, you never had an obligation to buy a car. The promise is broken in exactly one scenario: you win the lottery, and you do not buy the car ($P \land \neg Q$).
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{De Morgan's laws: the duality of AND and OR}

When negating compound statements joined by ``and'' (a conjunction, $P \land Q$, true when both statements are true) or by ``or'' (a \textbf{disjunction}, $P \lor Q$, true when at least one statement is true), the nineteenth-century British logician Augustus De Morgan observed that \textbf{negation swaps ``and'' with ``or'' and moves inside}. Words such as ``and'', ``or'', ``not'' and ``if \ldots\ then'' that build a statement out of smaller statements are called \emph{logical connectives}. This exchange of the two connectives ``and'' and ``or'' is what ``duality'' means here.
\begin{align*}
\neg(P \land Q) &\iff \neg P \lor \neg Q, \\
\neg(P \lor Q) &\iff \neg P \land \neg Q.
\end{align*}

Since $P$ and $Q$ can each be true or false, there are only four cases to check. Figure~\ref{05:fig-demorgan-table} goes through all four: in every row, the two sides of each law have the same truth value, so the laws hold.

The same rule shows up in daily life. If a club requires you to be ``over 21 \textbf{and} a resident of California,'' what does it take to be turned away at the door? You do not need to fail both rules. You are turned away if you are under 21, \textbf{or} if you are not a resident of California.

Similarly, if an airline offers free luggage to ``passengers with gold status \textbf{or} a first-class ticket,'' what does it take to be charged for a bag? You must lack gold status \textbf{and} lack a first-class ticket.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Negate the statement: ``The number $x$ is positive and rational.'' Does its negation claim that $x$ is negative and irrational? No: it asserts that $x$ is non-positive (zero or negative) \textbf{or} $x$ is irrational. A single failure breaks an ``and''.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Negating a multi-quantifier statement: step-by-step}

In real analysis (the branch of mathematics that makes limits and calculus rigorous), an infinite list of numbers $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$ is said to converge to a real number $L$ (written $a_n \to L$ as $n \to \infty$) if the terms eventually get and stay arbitrarily close to $L$. Formally (as in Chapter~4, $\eps$ is a tolerance, $N$ is a cutoff index, and $|a_n - L|$ is the distance from the term $a_n$ to $L$):
\[
\forall \eps > 0, \, \exists N \in \N, \, \forall n \in \N, \, \bigl(n \ge N \implies |a_n - L| < \eps\bigr).
\]
Mathematicians often abbreviate the tail condition as $\forall n \ge N, \, |a_n - L| < \eps$. Writing the implication explicitly makes it easier to negate the definition without mistakes.

To negate sequence convergence, we push the negation symbol through the quantifiers one by one from left to right. Each time it passes a quantifier, that quantifier flips; when it reaches the implication, the rule $\neg(P \implies Q) \iff P \land \neg Q$ finishes the job. Figure~\ref{05:fig-negation-march} shows the negation sign moving to the right, and the derivation below writes out each step with its reason:
\begin{align*}
&\neg \bigl(\forall \eps > 0, \, \exists N \in \N, \, \forall n \in \N, \, (n \ge N \implies |a_n - L| < \eps)\bigr) \\
&\iff \exists \eps > 0, \, \neg \bigl(\exists N \in \N, \, \forall n \in \N, \, (n \ge N \implies |a_n - L| < \eps)\bigr) && \text{($\neg\forall$ becomes $\exists\neg$)} \\
&\iff \exists \eps > 0, \, \forall N \in \N, \, \neg \bigl(\forall n \in \N, \, (n \ge N \implies |a_n - L| < \eps)\bigr) && \text{($\neg\exists$ becomes $\forall\neg$)} \\
&\iff \exists \eps > 0, \, \forall N \in \N, \, \exists n \in \N, \, \neg \bigl(n \ge N \implies |a_n - L| < \eps\bigr) && \text{($\neg\forall$ becomes $\exists\neg$)} \\
&\iff \exists \eps > 0, \, \forall N \in \N, \, \exists n \in \N, \, \bigl(n \ge N \land |a_n - L| \ge \eps\bigr). && \text{($\neg(P \implies Q)$ is $P \land \neg Q$)}
\end{align*}
In the last step, $P$ is $n \ge N$ and $Q$ is $|a_n - L| < \eps$, so $\neg Q$ is $|a_n - L| \ge \eps$.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The index flip trap:} Why did $n \ge N$ remain $n \ge N$ in the negation, rather than flipping to $n < N$? Beginners often reverse every inequality sign on sight. But $n \ge N$ is the premise of the implication, the condition under which the guarantee was made, and the negation $P \land \neg Q$ keeps the premise $P$ unchanged. A sequence fails to converge if there is one fixed tolerance $\eps$ such that, beyond any cutoff index ($n \ge N$), there is still a term that breaks out ($|a_n - L| \ge \eps$). Early terms ($n < N$) are always allowed to wander freely.
\end{trapbox}

Call the fixed tolerance in the negation $\eps_0$; the subscript $0$ marks it as one particular value that stays fixed for the rest of the argument. Figure~\ref{05:fig-non-convergence} shows what the negation looks like: the band from $L - \eps_0$ to $L + \eps_0$, a cutoff $N$, and terms after $N$ that land outside the band.

\subsection{Using formal negation and unpacking definitions}

The rules of this chapter give a procedure for refuting claims. By pushing negations past the quantifiers ($\neg \forall$ becomes $\exists \neg$) and rewriting negated implications ($\neg(P \implies Q) \iff P \land \neg Q$), we reduce the refutation of a universal implication to finding a single concrete counterexample: an object satisfying the premise while violating the conclusion. Figure~\ref{05:fig-rules-summary} collects the rules of this chapter, with what you must show to refute each kind of claim.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Claim} & \textbf{Its negation} & \textbf{To refute the claim, show that} \\
\midrule
$\forall x \in D,\, P(x)$ & $\exists x \in D,\, \neg P(x)$ & one object $x$ in $D$ fails $P$ \\
$\exists x \in D,\, P(x)$ & $\forall x \in D,\, \neg P(x)$ & every object $x$ in $D$ fails $P$ \\
$P \land Q$ & $\neg P \lor \neg Q$ & at least one of $P$, $Q$ is false \\
$P \lor Q$ & $\neg P \land \neg Q$ & both $P$ and $Q$ are false \\
$P \implies Q$ & $P \land \neg Q$ & $P$ is true and $Q$ is false \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The negation rules of this chapter in one place. The right-hand column says what a refutation must contain.}
\label{05:fig-rules-summary}
\end{figure}

However, applying these rules requires unpacking definitions that are expressed in words. For example, to evaluate the claim that every continuous function is differentiable, formal negation asks for a function that is continuous and not differentiable. (Roughly, a continuous function has a graph with no jumps or breaks; a differentiable one also has a well-defined slope at every point, so no sharp corners. The function $|x|$ is continuous but has a corner at $0$, as Figure~\ref{05:fig-abs-corner} shows.) But verifying whether a candidate function qualifies requires inspecting the formal quantifiers inside the definitions of continuity and differentiability.

\subsection{Exercises}

\textbf{Warm-up mental checks (test your instincts in ten seconds):}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Negate: ``Every student in the seminar brought a textbook.'' (Does refutation require an empty room, or one student without a book?)
\item Negate: ``If an integer $n$ is divisible by $6$, then $n$ is divisible by $3$.'' (What condition must an integer satisfy to break this promise?)
\item Negate: ``Every prime number is odd.'' (Identify the single number in $\N$ that serves as a witness.)
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Quantifier and connective mechanics (push all negations fully inside):}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every real number has a real cube root: $\forall x \in \R, \, \exists y \in \R, \, y^3 = x$.
\item There is an integer whose square is negative: $\exists n \in \Z, \, n^2 < 0$.
\item For every positive real number $x$, there exists a positive real number $y$ such that $xy = 1$:
\[
\forall x \in (0, \infty), \, \exists y \in (0, \infty), \, xy = 1.
\]
\item If $n$ is prime, then $n = 2$ or $n$ is odd.
\item $\forall x \in A, \, \bigl(P(x) \implies \exists y \in B, \, Q(x, y)\bigr)$.
\item The function $f : \R \to \R$ is bounded: $\exists M > 0, \, \forall x \in \R, \, |f(x)| \le M$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Finding witnesses and refuting claims:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Consider the assertion: ``For every real number $x$, if $x^2 > 4$, then $x > 2$.''
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Formulate the exact mathematical negation using quantifiers and connectives.
    \item Provide an explicit numerical witness $x \in \R$ showing that the negation is true (and hence the original assertion is false).
\end{enumerate}
\item A function $f : \R \to \R$ is called \emph{periodic} if there exists a non-zero real number $T$ such that for all $x \in \R$, $f(x + T) = f(x)$. In symbols:
\[
\exists T \in \R \text{ with } T \ne 0, \, \forall x \in \R, \, f(x + T) = f(x).
\]
Formulate the exact formal negation: what does it mean for a function to be \emph{aperiodic} (not periodic)?
\end{enumerate}

\begin{walkbox}
Whenever you read a news headline asserting that ``Nobody wants to do X anymore,'' practice formulating the minimal mathematical negation. Does it require an enthusiastic crowd, or merely one person?
\end{walkbox}
