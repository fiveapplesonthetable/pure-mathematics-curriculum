\section{Real Analysis Preview: Epsilon as a Contract}
\chapterline

In introductory calculus, we are told that a sequence approaches a limit:
\[
\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L,
\]
or that a function approaches a value:
\[
\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L.
\]
Textbooks describe this dynamically: ``As $x$ gets closer and closer to $c$, $f(x)$ gets closer and closer to $L$.''

While that mental picture is intuitive, it lacks logical precision. What does ``closer and closer'' actually mean? Does it mean the sequence can never drift backward? Does it mean the distance decreases at every single step? 

In the nineteenth century, mathematicians like Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Karl Weierstrass replaced dynamic intuition with precise logic, framing the limit as a verifiable contract.

\begin{historybox}
The rigor of real analysis was born out of a philosophical challenge. In 1734, Bishop George Berkeley published \emph{The Analyst: A Discourse Addressed to an Infidel Mathematician}. Berkeley criticized Isaac Newton's ``fluxions'' and Gottfried Leibniz's ``differentials,'' pointing out a logical gap in early calculus: to compute a derivative, one divided by an increment $\Delta x$ (requiring $\Delta x \neq 0$), only to set $\Delta x = 0$ a moment later to eliminate unwanted terms. Berkeley famously scoffed:
\begin{quote}
\itshape
``And what are these same evanescent increments? They are neither finite quantities, nor quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the ghosts of departed quantities?''
\end{quote}
For nearly a century, mathematicians calculated results using infinitesimals without a formal foundation. The resolution came when Augustin-Louis Cauchy (\emph{Cours d'Analyse}, 1821) and Karl Weierstrass in his 1860s Berlin lectures established analysis on real inequalities. Weierstrass demonstrated that calculus does not require infinitely small quantities; instead, limits are defined using ordinary positive real numbers ($\eps$ and $\delta$) governed by nested quantifiers.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The Sequence Contract: Epsilon and Index Cutoffs}

A sequence of real numbers $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ is an infinite, ordered list of numbers:
\[
a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4, \dots
\]
What does it mean for this list to settle down toward a target limit $L$?

\begin{definition}[Limit of a Sequence]
A sequence of real numbers $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ \textbf{converges} to a real number $L$, written $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$, if:
\[
(\forall \eps > 0)(\exists N \in \N)(\forall n \in \N)(n \ge N \implies |a_n - L| < \eps).
\]
\end{definition}

\subsubsection{Understanding the Quantifiers}

In plain words: no matter how small a positive tolerance $\eps$ is chosen around the limit $L$, there exists a cutoff index $N$ such that every term from $a_N$ onward lies within distance $\eps$ of $L$.

To understand the quantifier structure, we can view convergence as a dialogue between a given tolerance and an index response:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{The Given Tolerance ($\forall \eps > 0$):} An error tolerance $\eps > 0$ is specified, which may be arbitrarily small (such as $\eps = 0.0001$ or $\eps = 10^{-12}$).
\item \textbf{The Index Cutoff ($\exists N \in \N$):} For the given $\eps$, we determine an index cutoff $N \in \N$. Because $N$ is chosen after $\eps$, it may depend on $\eps$.
\item \textbf{The Verification ($\forall n \ge N$):} We check every index $n \ge N$ at or past the cutoff. The condition:
\[
|a_n - L| < \eps \iff L - \eps < a_n < L + \eps
\]
must hold for every $n \ge N$.
\end{enumerate}

If a valid cutoff $N$ can be found for every positive tolerance $\eps$, the sequence converges to $L$.

\subsection{Worked Proof: The Contract in Action for \texorpdfstring{$1/n \to 0$}{1/n -> 0}}

To see how an official $\eps$-$N$ proof is assembled, let us prove that the sequence $a_n = 1/n$ converges to $0$.

Every analysis proof proceeds in two stages: an informal scratchpad calculation (working backward from what you want to what you must choose), followed by the official forward proof.

\begin{theorem}
\[
\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\eps > 0$ be arbitrary. 

\emph{Scratchpad Search (backward reasoning):} We need to ensure that $|a_n - L| < \eps$. Substituting our sequence and candidate limit:
\[
\left|\frac{1}{n} - 0\right| = \frac{1}{n} < \eps \iff n > \frac{1}{\eps}.
\]
This inequality tells us exactly how far out in the sequence we must travel. For instance, if the challenger names $\eps = 0.005 = 1/200$, the scratchpad demands $n > 200$, so any integer cutoff $N \ge 201$ wins the round.

\emph{Official Written Proof (forward reasoning):}
The \textbf{Archimedean Property} of real numbers guarantees that the set of natural numbers $\N$ is not bounded above in $\R$---meaning no matter how large a real number $x$ is, there always exists an integer $N$ with $N > x$. Applying this property to the positive real number $1/\eps$, there exists a positive integer $N \in \N$ such that:
\[
N > \frac{1}{\eps}.
\]
Now let $n \in \N$ be an arbitrary index and assume $n \ge N$.
Because $n$ and $N$ are positive integers with $n \ge N$, taking reciprocals reverses the inequality:
\[
\frac{1}{n} \le \frac{1}{N}.
\]
Since $N > 1/\eps$, we know $1/N < \eps$. Combining these inequalities yields:
\[
\left|\frac{1}{n} - 0\right| = \frac{1}{n} \le \frac{1}{N} < \eps.
\]
Thus for all $n \ge N$, we have $|1/n - 0| < \eps$. Because $\eps > 0$ was arbitrary, $\lim_{n \to \infty} 1/n = 0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{When the Contract Breaks: Proving Divergence}

To see how the definition detects non-convergence, consider the alternating sequence $a_n = (-1)^n$:
\[
-1, \; +1, \; -1, \; +1, \; -1, \; +1, \dots
\]
Could this sequence converge to the candidate limit $L = 1$?

Recall from Chapter~5 how to negate a quantified statement. The claim $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$ states:
\[
(\forall \eps > 0)(\exists N \in \N)(\forall n \ge N)(|a_n - L| < \eps).
\]
Its logical negation flips every quantifier:
\[
(\exists \eps > 0)(\forall N \in \N)(\exists n \ge N)(|a_n - L| \ge \eps).
\]
To prove that the sequence does \emph{not} converge to $1$, we specify a tolerance $\eps > 0$ that cannot be satisfied.

Let the challenger choose $\eps = 1/2$. The horizontal corridor of tolerance around $L = 1$ is the interval $(1 - 1/2, 1 + 1/2) = (0.5, 1.5)$. This corridor contains $+1$, but it completely excludes $-1$.

For any proposed integer cutoff $N \in \N$, we can choose an odd index $n \ge N$ (such as $n = 2N + 1$). Because $n$ is odd, $a_n = -1$, and the distance to the proposed limit is:
\[
|a_n - L| = |-1 - 1| = |-2| = 2.
\]
Since $2 \ge 1/2$, this term lies outside the corridor. Because this holds for every cutoff $N$, terms escape indefinitely, and $\lim_{n \to \infty} (-1)^n \neq 1$. (A similar check shows that no candidate $L \in \R$ can trap both $+1$ and $-1$ once $\eps \le 1$.)

\subsection{Continuity: Epsilon and Delta}

In a sequence $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$, the index $n \in \N$ takes discrete values ($n \to \infty$). When studying a function $f: \R \to \R$, the input $x \in \R$ varies continuously, approaching a specific target point $c \in \R$ ($x \to c$).

The logical structure is analogous: instead of choosing an integer cutoff $N$, we find a radius $\delta > 0$ for an interval around $c$.

\begin{definition}[Continuity at a Point]
A function $f: \R \to \R$ is \textbf{continuous at a point $c \in \R$} if:
\[
(\forall \eps > 0)(\exists \delta > 0)(\forall x \in \R)(|x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \eps).
\]
\end{definition}

Geometrically, an error tolerance $\eps > 0$ defines a horizontal strip $(f(c) - \eps, f(c) + \eps)$ around $y = f(c)$. Continuity asserts that there exists a neighborhood $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$ around $x = c$ whose image under $f$ lies entirely inside that strip.

\begin{theorem}
The linear function $f(x) = 3x + 2$ is continuous at every $c \in \R$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $c \in \R$ and $\eps > 0$ be arbitrary.

\emph{Scratchpad Search:} Because $f(x) = 3x + 2$ has a constant slope of $3$, any horizontal input deviation $|x - c|$ is scaled vertically by a factor of $3$:
\[
|f(x) - f(c)| = |(3x + 2) - (3c + 2)| = |3(x - c)| = 3|x - c|.
\]
We want $3|x - c| < \eps$, which is equivalent to $|x - c| < \eps/3$. This tells us to choose $\delta = \eps / 3$.

\emph{Official Proof:}
Given $\eps > 0$, choose $\delta = \frac{\eps}{3} > 0$. Suppose $x \in \R$ satisfies $|x - c| < \delta$. Then:
\[
|f(x) - f(c)| = |(3x + 2) - (3c + 2)| = 3|x - c| < 3\delta = 3\left(\frac{\eps}{3}\right) = \eps.
\]
Thus for all $x \in \R$ with $|x - c| < \delta$, we have $|f(x) - f(c)| < \eps$. Since $c \in \R$ and $\eps > 0$ were arbitrary, $f$ is continuous at every $c \in \R$.
\end{proof}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose $f(x) = -5x + 4$. If a skeptic sets an error tolerance $\eps = 0.05$ at the point $c = 1$, what input radius $\delta$ should you choose to ensure $|f(x) - f(1)| < \eps$?
\\[0.4em]
\emph{Thought process:} Compute the output gap:
\[
|f(x) - f(1)| = |(-5x + 4) - (-1)| = |-5x + 5| = |-5(x - 1)| = 5|x - 1|.
\]
We require $5|x - 1| < \eps = 0.05$, which means $|x - 1| < 0.01$. Choosing $\delta = \eps/5 = 0.01$ guarantees the required bound. Notice that the minus sign in the slope $-5$ was absorbed by the absolute value: $\delta$ is always a positive geometric distance!
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Non-Linear Functions and Provisional Bounds}

For the linear function $f(x) = 3x + 2$, the slope is $3$ everywhere, so dividing $\eps$ by $3$ gave a value of $\delta$ directly. For non-linear functions, the rate of change varies, so additional care is needed.

Consider $f(x) = x^2$ near $c = 3$. We want to control:
\[
|f(x) - f(3)| = |x^2 - 9| = |x - 3| \cdot |x + 3|.
\]
We can make $|x - 3| < \delta$ small, but the factor $|x + 3|$ grows if $x$ is far from $3$.

To control this factor, we apply a standard technique: \textbf{impose a provisional bound on $\delta$}.

We first require that $\delta \le 1$. If $|x - 3| < 1$, then $x \in (2, 4)$, which bounds the factor:
\[
|x + 3| \le |x| + 3 < 4 + 3 = 7.
\]
With $|x + 3| < 7$ secured, our error calculation becomes:
\[
|x^2 - 9| = |x - 3| \cdot |x + 3| < 7|x - 3|.
\]
Now we know how to finish: to make $7|x - 3| < \eps$, we simply need $|x - 3| < \eps / 7$.

To guarantee that \emph{both} conditions hold simultaneously ($|x - 3| < 1$ to control the factor, and $|x - 3| < \eps/7$ to hit the tolerance), we set:
\[
\delta = \min\left\{1, \; \frac{\eps}{7}\right\}.
\]
If $\eps = 14$, then $\eps/7 = 2$, so we pick $\delta = 1$. If $\eps = 0.07$, then $\eps/7 = 0.01$, so we pick $\delta = 0.01$. The minimum function $\min\{1, \dots\}$ is the standard method in analysis for bounding terms that depend on $x$ while simultaneously satisfying the error tolerance.

\subsection{Quantifier Order and Variable Scoping in Analysis}

The logical structure of analysis depends centrally on \textbf{quantifier order} and tracking variable dependencies. 

Recall the strict dependency chain in the definition of a limit:
\[
\forall \eps > 0 \quad \longrightarrow \quad \exists N \in \N \quad \longrightarrow \quad \forall n \ge N.
\]
Because the quantifiers are read from left to right, your choice of $N$ is permitted to depend on $\eps$. It is \emph{not} permitted to depend on $n$, because the index $n$ has not been introduced yet!

To see why this ordering is essential, consider what happens if you swap the first two quantifiers:
\[
\exists N \in \N \quad \forall \eps > 0 \quad \forall n \ge N \quad (|a_n - L| < \eps).
\]
This swapped statement claims that there exists a single, fixed cutoff $N$ that works for \emph{every positive tolerance $\eps$ simultaneously}. If $|a_n - L| < \eps$ for every $\eps > 0$, the only real number whose distance to $L$ is strictly less than every positive real number is $0$ itself. Therefore, every term past index $N$ must equal $L$ exactly:
\[
a_N = L, \quad a_{N+1} = L, \quad a_{N+2} = L, \dots
\]
Swapping the quantifiers fundamentally alters the statement: rather than asserting that terms eventually become arbitrarily close to $L$, it requires the sequence to be eventually constant at $L$.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} What goes wrong with the claim $\lim_{n \to \infty} 1/n = 0$ if a student writes: ``Given $\eps > 0$, let $n \ge N$ and choose $N = n + 1$''?
\\[0.4em]
\emph{Thought process:} This is circular variable scoping. In the definition, the cutoff $N$ must be chosen before the arbitrary index $n \ge N$ is specified. Defining $N$ in terms of $n$ violates the quantifier scoping.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Cauchy's Quantifier Error (1821):}
Even Cauchy encountered subtleties with quantifier order. In his 1821 textbook \emph{Cours d'Analyse}, Cauchy claimed that if an infinite series of continuous functions converges, its sum must also be continuous. 

Five years later, Niels Henrik Abel and others showed this was false: a series of continuous trigonometric functions can converge to a discontinuous step function.

What had gone wrong? Cauchy had assumed that because each individual point $x$ settles down ($\forall x \, \forall \eps > 0 \, \exists N$), the whole family must converge at a single shared speed ($\forall \eps > 0 \, \exists N \, \forall x$), inadvertently swapping the order of ``for all $x$'' and ``there exists $N$''. It took over two decades for Philipp Ludwig von Seidel (1847) and George Gabriel Stokes to untangle this, establishing the modern distinction between \textbf{pointwise convergence} (each point has its own cutoff) and \textbf{uniform convergence} (one shared cutoff works across the whole domain).
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Scope, Limitations, and Looking Ahead}

The $\eps$-$\delta$ framework established rigorous foundations for analysis:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Foundations for calculus:} Limits, derivatives, and integrals are defined by inequalities rather than infinitesimals.
\item \textbf{Convergence criteria:} It provided tools to determine when sequences, power series, and function families converge.
\item \textbf{Logical precision:} It formalized quantifier scoping and adversarial arguments in analysis.
\end{itemize}

The $\eps$-$\delta$ definition relies on measuring distances with absolute values on the real line. In more abstract settings, numerical distance is not always available:
\begin{itemize}
\item Spaces in differential geometry and general relativity lack a global Cartesian coordinate system.
\item In topology, spaces are studied up to continuous deformation, where distances change while topological continuity is preserved.
\item In algebra, spaces are defined by symmetries and group actions rather than coordinates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exercises}

\subsubsection*{Part I: Mental Checks}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Oscillation vs. Convergence:} Does a sequence need to approach its limit monotonically (decreasing at every step) to converge? Consider the alternating sequence $a_n = \frac{(-1)^n}{n}$. For a given $\eps > 0$, verify mentally what cutoff $N$ ensures $|a_n - 0| < \eps$ for all $n \ge N$.
\item \textbf{The Constant Function Case:} Suppose an author mistakenly defines continuity at $c$ by swapping quantifiers:
\[
(\exists \delta > 0)(\forall \eps > 0)(\forall x \in \R)(|x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \eps).
\]
Explain in plain words what this condition forces upon the function $f(x)$ inside the interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part II: Limit and Continuity Proofs}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Rational Sequence Limit:} Use the formal $\eps$-$N$ definition of a limit to prove:
\[
\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{2n + 1}{n + 3} = 2.
\]
\emph{Strategy:} Show that $|a_n - 2| = \frac{5}{n + 3} < \frac{5}{n}$. Use this simpler upper bound to select $N$, and then write the official forward proof.
\item \textbf{Proving Divergence:} Prove that the alternating sequence $a_n = (-1)^n$ does not converge to \emph{any} real number $L \in \R$.
\emph{Hint:} Let $L \in \R$ be arbitrary. Choose $\eps = 1/2$. Show that no single open interval of radius $1/2$ can contain both $+1$ and $-1$, ensuring that terms escape for every cutoff $N$.
\item \textbf{Linear Continuity:} Use the $\eps$-$\delta$ definition to prove that $f(x) = -4x + 7$ is continuous at $c = 2$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part III: Deeper Explorations}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Non-Linear Continuity:} Use the provisional bound strategy ($\delta \le 1$) to prove that $f(x) = x^2$ is continuous at $c = 3$. Write out both the scratchpad derivation and the official proof choosing $\delta = \min\{1, \eps/7\}$.
\item \textbf{The Sum Rule (Splitting $\eps$):} Suppose $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ converges to $A$ and $(b_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ converges to $B$. Prove that the sequence $(a_n + b_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ converges to $A + B$.
\emph{Hint:} Apply the triangle inequality:
\[
|(a_n + b_n) - (A + B)| = |(a_n - A) + (b_n - B)| \le |a_n - A| + |b_n - B|.
\]
Allocate an error tolerance of $\eps/2$ to each sequence, find their respective cutoffs $N_1$ and $N_2$, and let $N = \max\{N_1, N_2\}$.
\end{enumerate}
