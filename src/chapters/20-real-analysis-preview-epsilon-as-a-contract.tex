\section{Real Analysis Preview: Epsilon as a Contract}
\chapterline

In introductory calculus, we are told that a sequence approaches a limit:
\[
\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L,
\]
or that a function approaches a value:
\[
\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L.
\]
Here $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$ is a list of numbers, $f$ is a function, $c$ is an input point, and $L$ is the target value, called the \emph{limit}. Textbooks describe this as motion: ``As $x$ gets closer and closer to $c$, $f(x)$ gets closer and closer to $L$.''

That picture helps, but it is not precise. What does ``closer and closer'' mean? Does it mean the sequence can never drift backward? Does it mean the distance decreases at every single step? 

\textbf{Real analysis} is the branch of mathematics that answers such questions: it gives exact definitions of limits, continuity, derivatives and integrals, and proves the facts that calculus uses. In the nineteenth century, Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Karl Weierstrass replaced the picture of motion with a precise statement built from the nested quantifiers of Chapter~4. The limit becomes a contract whose terms can be checked.

\begin{historybox}
The rigor of real analysis was born out of a philosophical challenge. In 1734, Bishop George Berkeley published \emph{The Analyst: A Discourse Addressed to an Infidel Mathematician}. Berkeley criticized Isaac Newton's ``fluxions'' and Gottfried Leibniz's ``differentials'' (their names for rates of change and for vanishingly small changes). He pointed out a logical gap in early calculus. To compute a derivative (the slope of a graph at a point), one divided by a small change $\Delta x$ in the input (which requires $\Delta x \neq 0$), only to set $\Delta x = 0$ a moment later to eliminate unwanted terms. Berkeley famously scoffed:
\begin{quote}
\itshape
``And what are these same evanescent increments? They are neither finite quantities, nor quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the ghosts of departed quantities?''
\end{quote}
For nearly a century, mathematicians calculated with \emph{infinitesimals} (quantities supposed to be smaller than every positive number yet not zero) without a logical foundation for them. The resolution came when Augustin-Louis Cauchy (\emph{Cours d'Analyse}, 1821) and Karl Weierstrass in his 1860s Berlin lectures rebuilt analysis on ordinary inequalities between real numbers. Weierstrass demonstrated that calculus does not require infinitely small quantities; instead, limits are defined using ordinary positive real numbers ($\eps$ and $\delta$) governed by nested quantifiers.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The Sequence Contract: Epsilon and Index Cutoffs}

A sequence of real numbers $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ is an infinite, ordered list of numbers:
\[
a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4, \dots.
\]
The notation $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ names the whole list; $a_n$ is its $n$-th term, and $n$ is called the \emph{index}.
What does it mean for this list to settle down toward a target limit $L$?

\begin{definition}[Limit of a Sequence]
A sequence of real numbers $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ \textbf{converges} to a real number $L$, written $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$, if:
\[
(\forall \eps > 0)(\exists N \in \N)(\forall n \in \N)(n \ge N \implies |a_n - L| < \eps).
\]
\end{definition}

\subsubsection{Understanding the Quantifiers}

In plain words: no matter how small a positive tolerance $\eps$ is chosen around the limit $L$, there exists a cutoff index $N$ such that every term from $a_N$ onward lies within distance $\eps$ of $L$.

As in the quantifier game of Chapter~4, read the definition as three moves between a Challenger and a Prover:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{The Given Tolerance ($\forall \eps > 0$):} The Challenger names an error tolerance $\eps > 0$: how far from $L$ a term is allowed to be. It may be as small as they like (such as $\eps = 0.0001$ or $\eps = 10^{-12}$).
\item \textbf{The Index Cutoff ($\exists N \in \N$):} For that $\eps$, the Prover answers with an index cutoff $N \in \N$. Because $N$ is chosen after $\eps$, it may depend on $\eps$.
\item \textbf{The Verification ($\forall n \ge N$):} The Challenger may test any index $n \ge N$ at or past the cutoff. The condition
\[
|a_n - L| < \eps \iff L - \eps < a_n < L + \eps
\]
must hold for every such $n$.
\end{enumerate}
The right-hand form says that $a_n$ lies in the \emph{open interval} $(L - \eps, L + \eps)$, the set of real numbers strictly between $L - \eps$ and $L + \eps$. (The same notation $(a, b)$ is also used for an ordered pair; the context tells you which is meant.) Figure~\ref{20:fig-open-interval} draws this on the number line: $|a_n - L|$ is the distance from $a_n$ to $L$, and the condition says that distance is less than $\eps$.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} To verify that $a_n = 1/n^2$ converges to $0$ for the tolerance $\eps = 0.01 = 1/100$, what is the smallest index cutoff $N \in \N$ that guarantees $|1/n^2 - 0| < 0.01$ for every $n \ge N$?
\\[0.4em]
\emph{Thought process:} For a positive integer $n$,
\[
\frac{1}{n^2} < \frac{1}{100} \iff n^2 > 100 \iff n > 10.
\]
The smallest integer strictly greater than $10$ is $N = 11$. For any $n \ge 11$, we have $1/n^2 \le 1/121 < 1/100$, so the contract holds.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Worked Proof: The Contract in Action for \texorpdfstring{$1/n \to 0$}{1/n -> 0}}

We now write a complete $\eps$-$N$ proof that the sequence $a_n = 1/n$ converges to $0$.

Such proofs are usually built in two stages: an informal scratchpad calculation (working backward from what you want to what you must choose), followed by the official forward proof.

\begin{theorem}
\[
\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\eps > 0$ be arbitrary. 

\emph{Scratchpad Search (backward reasoning):} We need to ensure that $|a_n - L| < \eps$. Substituting our sequence and candidate limit:
\[
\left|\frac{1}{n} - 0\right| = \frac{1}{n} < \eps \iff n > \frac{1}{\eps}.
\]
This inequality tells us exactly how far out in the sequence we must travel. For instance, if the Challenger names $\eps = 0.005 = 1/200$, the scratchpad demands $n > 200$, so any integer cutoff $N \ge 201$ wins the round.

\emph{Official Written Proof (forward reasoning):}
The \textbf{Archimedean Property} of real numbers guarantees that the set of natural numbers $\N$ is not bounded above in $\R$: no matter how large a real number $x$ is, there always exists an integer $N$ with $N > x$. Applying this property to the positive real number $1/\eps$, there exists a positive integer $N \in \N$ such that:
\[
N > \frac{1}{\eps}.
\]
Now let $n \in \N$ be an arbitrary index and assume $n \ge N$.
Because $n$ and $N$ are positive integers with $n \ge N$, taking reciprocals reverses the inequality:
\[
\frac{1}{n} \le \frac{1}{N}.
\]
Since $N > 1/\eps$, we know $1/N < \eps$. Chaining these steps:
\begin{align*}
\left|\frac{1}{n} - 0\right| &= \frac{1}{n} && \text{(since $n > 0$)}\\
&\le \frac{1}{N} && \text{(since $n \ge N$)}\\
&< \eps. && \text{(since $N > 1/\eps$)}
\end{align*}
Thus for all $n \ge N$, we have $|1/n - 0| < \eps$. Because $\eps > 0$ was arbitrary, $\lim_{n \to \infty} 1/n = 0$.
\end{proof}

Figure~\ref{20:fig-one-over-n} plays one round of this game with $\eps = 0.15$. Then $1/\eps \approx 6.7$, so the proof picks $N = 7$, and from $a_7 = 1/7 \approx 0.143$ onward every term lies inside the band.

\subsection{When the Contract Breaks: Proving Divergence}

A sequence that does not converge to any number is said to \emph{diverge}. Consider the alternating sequence $a_n = (-1)^n$, whose terms switch sign at every step:
\[
-1, \; +1, \; -1, \; +1, \; -1, \; +1, \dots.
\]
Could this sequence converge to the candidate limit $L = 1$?

Recall from Chapter~5 how to negate a quantified statement. The claim $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$ states:
\[
(\forall \eps > 0)(\exists N \in \N)(\forall n \ge N)(|a_n - L| < \eps).
\]
Its negation turns each $\forall$ into $\exists$ and each $\exists$ into $\forall$, while the condition $n \ge N$ stays attached to $n$ (it does not flip to $n < N$; Chapter~5 explains why):
\[
(\exists \eps > 0)(\forall N \in \N)(\exists n \ge N)(|a_n - L| \ge \eps).
\]
Figure~\ref{20:fig-negation-flip} lines the two statements up piece by piece.

To prove that the sequence does \emph{not} converge to $1$, we must exhibit one tolerance $\eps > 0$ for which no cutoff works.

Take the Challenger's side and choose $\eps = 1/2$. The horizontal corridor of tolerance around $L = 1$ is the interval $(1 - 1/2, 1 + 1/2) = (0.5, 1.5)$. This corridor contains $+1$, but it excludes $-1$.

For any proposed integer cutoff $N \in \N$, we can choose an odd index $n \ge N$ (such as $n = 2N + 1$). Because $n$ is odd, $a_n = -1$, and the distance to the proposed limit is:
\[
|a_n - L| = \lvert -1 - 1 \rvert = \lvert -2 \rvert = 2.
\]
Since $2 \ge 1/2$, this term lies outside the corridor. Because this holds for every cutoff $N$, no cutoff works, and $\lim_{n \to \infty} (-1)^n \neq 1$. Figure~\ref{20:fig-divergence-witness} shows the argument. (A similar check shows that no candidate $L \in \R$ can trap both $+1$ and $-1$ once $\eps \le 1$.)

\subsection{Continuity: Epsilon and Delta}

In a sequence $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$, the index $n$ runs through the whole numbers $1, 2, 3, \dots$, and we ask what happens as $n$ grows without bound ($n \to \infty$). For a function $f: \R \to \R$, the input $x$ can be any real number, and we ask what happens as $x$ approaches a target point $c \in \R$ ($x \to c$). A function is \emph{continuous} at $c$ when $f(x)$ approaches $f(c)$ itself as $x \to c$: the limit $L$ is the actual value of the function at $c$.

The logical structure is analogous: instead of choosing an integer cutoff $N$, we find a radius $\delta > 0$ for an interval around $c$.

\begin{definition}[Continuity at a Point]
A function $f: \R \to \R$ is \textbf{continuous at a point $c \in \R$} if:
\[
(\forall \eps > 0)(\exists \delta > 0)(\forall x \in \R)(|x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \eps).
\]
\end{definition}

Geometrically, the Challenger's tolerance $\eps > 0$ defines a horizontal strip $(f(c) - \eps, f(c) + \eps)$ around $y = f(c)$. Continuity asserts that the Prover can find an interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$ around $x = c$ whose image under $f$ (the set of all outputs $f(x)$ for $x$ in that interval; see Chapter~14) lies entirely inside that strip. \begin{theorem}
The linear function $f(x) = 3x + 2$ is continuous at every $c \in \R$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $c \in \R$ and $\eps > 0$ be arbitrary.

\emph{Scratchpad Search:} Because $f(x) = 3x + 2$ has a constant slope of $3$, a change of $|x - c|$ in the input changes the output by exactly $3|x - c|$:
\begin{align*}
|f(x) - f(c)| &= |(3x + 2) - (3c + 2)|\\
&= |3(x - c)|\\
&= 3|x - c|.
\end{align*}
We want $3|x - c| < \eps$, which is equivalent to $|x - c| < \eps/3$. This tells us to choose $\delta = \eps / 3$.

\emph{Official Proof:}
Given $\eps > 0$, choose $\delta = \eps/3$, which is positive. Suppose $x \in \R$ satisfies $|x - c| < \delta$. Then
\begin{align*}
|f(x) - f(c)| &= |(3x + 2) - (3c + 2)| = 3|x - c| && \text{(as in the scratchpad)}\\
&< 3\delta && \text{(since $|x - c| < \delta$)}\\
&= 3 \cdot \frac{\eps}{3} = \eps.
\end{align*}
Thus for all $x \in \R$ with $|x - c| < \delta$, we have $|f(x) - f(c)| < \eps$. Since $c \in \R$ and $\eps > 0$ were arbitrary, $f$ is continuous at every $c \in \R$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose $f(x) = -5x + 4$. If the Challenger sets an error tolerance $\eps = 0.05$ at the point $c = 1$, what input radius $\delta$ should you choose to ensure $|f(x) - f(1)| < \eps$?
\\[0.4em]
\emph{Thought process:} Since $f(1) = -1$, the output gap is
\begin{align*}
|f(x) - f(1)| &= |(-5x + 4) - (-1)|\\
&= \lvert -5x + 5 \rvert\\
&= \lvert -5(x - 1) \rvert\\
&= 5|x - 1|.
\end{align*}
We require $5|x - 1| < \eps = 0.05$, which means $|x - 1| < 0.01$. Choosing $\delta = \eps/5 = 0.01$ guarantees the required bound. The minus sign in the slope $-5$ disappears inside the absolute value: $\delta$ is a distance, so it is always positive.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Non-Linear Functions and Provisional Bounds}

For the linear function $f(x) = 3x + 2$, the slope is $3$ everywhere, so dividing $\eps$ by $3$ gave a value of $\delta$ directly. For a non-linear function the graph is steeper in some places than in others, so no single slope tells us how to scale $\eps$.

Consider $f(x) = x^2$ near $c = 3$. We want to control:
\[
|f(x) - f(3)| = |x^2 - 9| = |x - 3| \cdot |x + 3|.
\]
We can make $|x - 3| < \delta$ small, but the factor $|x + 3|$ grows if $x$ is far from $3$.

To control this factor, we \textbf{impose a provisional bound on $\delta$}: a temporary extra restriction that keeps $x$ close enough to $3$ for $|x + 3|$ to stay small.

We first require that $\delta \le 1$. If $|x - 3| < 1$, then $x \in (2, 4)$, so $x + 3$ is positive and the factor is bounded:
\[
|x + 3| = x + 3 < 4 + 3 = 7.
\]
With $|x + 3| < 7$ secured, our error calculation becomes:
\[
|x^2 - 9| = |x - 3| \cdot |x + 3| < 7|x - 3|.
\]
Now we know how to finish: to make $7|x - 3| < \eps$, we need $|x - 3| < \eps / 7$.

To guarantee that \emph{both} conditions hold simultaneously ($|x - 3| < 1$ to control the factor, and $|x - 3| < \eps/7$ to hit the tolerance), we set
\[
\delta = \min\left\{1, \; \frac{\eps}{7}\right\},
\]
where $\min\{a, b\}$ means the smaller of the two numbers $a$ and $b$.
If $\eps = 14$, then $\eps/7 = 2$, so we pick $\delta = 1$. If $\eps = 0.07$, then $\eps/7 = 0.01$, so we pick $\delta = 0.01$. Figure~\ref{20:fig-delta-min} plots this rule for every $\eps$.

In the forward verification, suppose $|x - 3| < \delta = \min\{1, \eps/7\}$. Then both bounds hold at once: $|x - 3| < 1$ forces $|x + 3| < 7$, and $|x - 3| < \eps/7$. Therefore
\begin{align*}
|x^2 - 9| &= |x - 3| \cdot |x + 3|\\
&< \frac{\eps}{7} \cdot 7 && \text{(since $|x - 3| < \eps/7$ and $|x + 3| < 7$)}\\
&= \eps.
\end{align*}
Taking a minimum like this is the usual way to make one $\delta$ meet several requirements at once. Figure~\ref{20:fig-nonlinear-provisional} shows the two stages.

\subsection{Quantifier Order and Variable Scoping in Analysis}

In analysis, the \textbf{quantifier order} decides which choices are allowed to depend on which.

In the definition of a limit, the quantifiers come in this order:
\[
\forall \eps > 0 \quad \longrightarrow \quad \exists N \in \N \quad \longrightarrow \quad \forall n \ge N.
\]
Because the quantifiers are read from left to right, your choice of $N$ is permitted to depend on $\eps$. It is \emph{not} permitted to depend on $n$, because the index $n$ has not been introduced yet.

Consider what happens if you swap the first two quantifiers:
\[
\exists N \in \N \quad \forall \eps > 0 \quad \forall n \ge N \quad (|a_n - L| < \eps).
\]
This swapped statement claims that there exists a single, fixed cutoff $N$ that works for \emph{every positive tolerance $\eps$ simultaneously}. So for each $n \ge N$, the distance $|a_n - L|$ is less than every positive number. The only non-negative real number with that property is $0$: if $|a_n - L|$ were positive, the tolerance $\eps = |a_n - L|/2$ would violate $|a_n - L| < \eps$. So $|a_n - L| = 0$. Therefore, every term past index $N$ must equal $L$ exactly:
\[
a_N = L, \quad a_{N+1} = L, \quad a_{N+2} = L, \quad \dots.
\]
Swapping the quantifiers changes the meaning. The original says the terms eventually come as close to $L$ as anyone asks; the swapped version says that from some point on, every term equals $L$ (the sequence is \emph{eventually constant}). Figure~\ref{20:fig-quantifier-order} compares the two.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Cauchy's Quantifier Error (1821):}
Even Cauchy encountered subtleties with quantifier order. In his 1821 textbook \emph{Cours d'Analyse}, Cauchy claimed that if an infinite series of continuous functions converges, its sum must also be continuous. (An \emph{infinite series of functions} is a sum $f_1(x) + f_2(x) + f_3(x) + \cdots$ with infinitely many terms; its value at $x$ is the limit of the finite partial sums $f_1(x) + \cdots + f_n(x)$ as $n \to \infty$.)

Five years later, Niels Henrik Abel and others showed this was false: a series built from continuous sine functions can converge to a function whose graph has sudden jumps, so the sum is not continuous.

What had gone wrong? Cauchy had assumed that because each individual point $x$ settles down ($\forall x \, \forall \eps > 0 \, \exists N$), the whole family must converge at a single shared speed ($\forall \eps > 0 \, \exists N \, \forall x$), inadvertently swapping the order of ``for all $x$'' and ``there exists $N$''. It took over two decades for Philipp Ludwig von Seidel (1847) and George Gabriel Stokes to untangle this, establishing the modern distinction between \textbf{pointwise convergence} (each point has its own cutoff) and \textbf{uniform convergence} (one shared cutoff works across the whole domain).

Figure~\ref{20:fig-pointwise-xn} shows the same effect in a simpler setting, a sequence of functions $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $0 \le x \le 1$ instead of a series. Each $f_n$ is continuous. For each fixed $x < 1$ the values $x^n$ tend to $0$, while at $x = 1$ they all equal $1$, so the limit function jumps from $0$ to $1$ at $x = 1$. The convergence is pointwise but not uniform: with $\eps = 1/2$, points closer to $1$ need later cutoffs, and no single $N$ makes $x^N < 1/2$ for every $x < 1$.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Scope, Limitations, and Looking Ahead}

The $\eps$-$\delta$ framework established rigorous foundations for analysis:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Foundations for calculus:} Limits, derivatives, and integrals are defined by inequalities rather than infinitesimals.
\item \textbf{Convergence criteria:} It provided tools to determine when sequences, infinite sums, and families of functions converge.
\item \textbf{Logical precision:} It made the order of quantifiers, and the Challenger--Prover style of argument, an explicit part of analysis.
\end{itemize}

The $\eps$-$\delta$ definition measures closeness by distance on the real line, using absolute values such as $|x - c|$. Other parts of mathematics need closeness or structure without such a ruler:
\begin{itemize}
\item On a curved surface such as the Earth, no single flat grid of coordinates covers everything; differential geometry (and Einstein's general relativity, which is built on it) must work with curved spaces of this kind.
\item In topology, two shapes count as the same if one can be stretched into the other without tearing. Stretching changes distances, so distance cannot be the basic notion, yet continuity still makes sense.
\item In algebra, objects are described by their symmetries (the ways they can be moved onto themselves) rather than by coordinates.
\end{itemize}

In the chapters ahead:
\begin{itemize}
\item In Chapter~21, \textbf{Abstract Algebra Preview: Structure and Symmetry}, we study systems of objects with rules for combining them (groups, rings and fields, defined there) and their symmetries.
\item In Chapter~22, \textbf{Topology Preview: Continuity Without Rulers}, we define continuity without any notion of distance, using open sets (defined there) and the inverse images of Chapter~14.
\item In Chapters~28 and 35, we return to real analysis for a second, more detailed pass, including uniform convergence.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exercises}

\subsubsection*{Part I: Mental Checks}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Oscillation vs. Convergence:} Does a sequence need to approach its limit from one side only, always decreasing (or always increasing), to converge? Such a one-directional sequence is called \emph{monotone}. Consider the alternating sequence $a_n = (-1)^n/n$. For a given $\eps > 0$, verify mentally what cutoff $N$ ensures $|a_n - 0| < \eps$ for all $n \ge N$.
\item \textbf{The Constant Function Case:} Suppose an author mistakenly defines continuity at $c$ by swapping quantifiers:
\[
(\exists \delta > 0)(\forall \eps > 0)(\forall x \in \R)(|x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \eps).
\]
Explain in plain words what this condition forces upon the function $f(x)$ inside the interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part II: Limit and Continuity Proofs}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Rational Sequence Limit:} Use the formal $\eps$-$N$ definition of a limit to prove:
\[
\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{2n + 1}{n + 3} = 2.
\]
\emph{Strategy:} Show that
\[
|a_n - 2| = \left|\frac{2n + 1}{n + 3} - 2\right| = \frac{5}{n + 3} < \frac{5}{n}.
\]
Use this simpler upper bound to select $N$, and then write the official forward proof.
\item \textbf{Proving Divergence:} Prove that the alternating sequence $a_n = (-1)^n$ does not converge to \emph{any} real number $L \in \R$.
\emph{Hint:} Let $L \in \R$ be arbitrary. Choose $\eps = 1/2$. Show that no single open interval of radius $1/2$ can contain both $+1$ and $-1$, ensuring that terms escape for every cutoff $N$.
\item \textbf{Linear Continuity:} Use the $\eps$-$\delta$ definition to prove that $f(x) = -4x + 7$ is continuous at $c = 2$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part III: Deeper Explorations}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Non-Linear Continuity:} Use the provisional bound strategy ($\delta \le 1$) to prove that $f(x) = x^2$ is continuous at $c = 3$. Write out both the scratchpad derivation and the official proof choosing $\delta = \min\{1, \eps/7\}$.
\item \textbf{The Sum Rule (Splitting $\eps$):} Suppose $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ converges to $A$ and $(b_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ converges to $B$. Prove that the sequence $(a_n + b_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ converges to $A + B$.
\emph{Hint:} Apply the \emph{triangle inequality} $|u + v| \le |u| + |v|$, which holds for all real numbers $u$ and $v$ (the size of a sum is at most the sum of the sizes):
\[
|(a_n + b_n) - (A + B)| = |(a_n - A) + (b_n - B)| \le |a_n - A| + |b_n - B|.
\]
Allocate an error tolerance of $\eps/2$ to each sequence, find their respective cutoffs $N_1$ and $N_2$, and let $N = \max\{N_1, N_2\}$, the larger of the two.
\end{enumerate}
