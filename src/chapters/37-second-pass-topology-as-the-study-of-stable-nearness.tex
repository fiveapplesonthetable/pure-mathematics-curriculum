\section{Second Pass: Topology as the Study of Stable Nearness}
\chapterline

In Chapter~36, we discovered how abstract algebra strips away concrete numbers to uncover the lawful grammar of combination and symmetry. By replacing calculation with structural axioms, we learned to manipulate operations without ever relying on numerical arithmetic.

Now we turn to the twin question for geometry and analysis: \emph{how can we describe space and continuity without relying on numerical distance measurements?}

In elementary geometry, everything is measured: lengths, angles, perimeters, and areas. In calculus, limits and derivatives depend on distances: you measure $|x - p|$, compare it to $\delta$, and demand that $|f(x) - f(p)|$ be smaller than $\eps$. But what if the space you are studying has no natural coordinate grid and no ruler? How can you say that two points are close to each other, that a shape is connected in one piece, or that a function does not tear?

The answer is the \textbf{open set}. By replacing numerical distances with qualitative neighborhoods, topology studies properties that are preserved under homeomorphisms (continuous bijections with continuous inverses).

\begin{historybox}
At the turn of the twentieth century, mathematicians collided with a crisis of scale. In classical physics, calculus took place in three-dimensional space $\R^3$, where Euclidean distance was obvious. But in the calculus of variations---the branch of mathematics that seeks paths or surfaces minimizing physical quantities like energy, travel time, or soap-film area---the ``points'' under study were no longer numbers or vectors. Each point was an entire function, curve, or physical field.

In 1906, the French mathematician Maurice Fr\'echet generalized Euclidean distance by introducing \emph{metric spaces}, where any two elements have an assigned numerical distance satisfying the triangle inequality (such as measuring the distance between two functions by integrating the difference between their graphs). Yet metrics still leaned heavily on numerical measurements, and many natural notions of convergence resisted being captured by a single distance function.

The decisive breakthrough came in 1914, when Felix Hausdorff published \emph{Grundz\"uge der Mengenlehre} (\emph{Foundations of Set Theory}). Hausdorff discarded numerical rulers entirely, defining a space purely through abstract open neighborhoods: regions enclosing each point with room to spare. In 1922, the Polish mathematician Kazimierz Kuratowski provided the modern structural closure axioms (rules governing which points lie on the brink of a set).

Topology unlocked modern functional analysis, quantum mechanics (where physical states inhabit infinite-dimensional function spaces), and algebraic topology. In 1930, the Soviet mathematician Andrey Tychonoff proved that the product of \emph{any} collection of compact spaces is compact, providing the universal engine for existence proofs in differential equations and probability theory. Open questions remain: while Grigori Perelman proved the 3-dimensional Poincar\'e conjecture in 2003, the smooth 4-dimensional Poincar\'e conjecture---whether exotic smooth 4-spheres exist that are homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to the standard 4-sphere---remains open.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Open Sets as Robust Properties}

On the real line $\R$, an open interval like $(2, 5)$ gives every point wiggle room. If $p = 2.001$, it is close to the edge, but you can still draw a small interval $(2.0005, 2.0015)$ around it that remains entirely inside $(2, 5)$.

In contrast, the closed interval $[2, 5]$ includes the boundary point $2$. No matter how tiny an open interval you place around $2$, its left half spills outside $[2, 5]$ into numbers less than $2$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of an open set as describing a \textbf{stable property}. If a state satisfies $x \in U$, where $U$ is open, small perturbations of $x$ remain inside $U$. A closed set contains its boundary points, whereas every point of an open set is an interior point.
\end{intuitionbox}

A \textbf{topological space} $(X, \mathcal{T})$ abstracts this intuition. A collection $\mathcal{T}$ of subsets of $X$ is declared to be ``open,'' subject to three axioms:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The entire space $X$ and the empty set $\emptyset$ are open.
  \item The union of \emph{any} collection of open sets is open.
  \item The intersection of \emph{any finite} collection of open sets is open.
\end{enumerate}

Why does the third axiom restrict itself to \emph{finite} intersections? Consider what happens on the real line if you intersect infinitely many open intervals centered at $0$:
\[
U_n = \left(-\frac{1}{n}, \frac{1}{n}\right) \quad \text{for } n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
\]
Each individual $U_n$ is open: every point inside has an open neighborhood contained in $U_n$. But their intersection across all $n \in \N$ leaves only a single number:
\[
\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \left(-\frac{1}{n}, \frac{1}{n}\right) = \{0\}.
\]
The singleton set $\{0\}$ is not open in $\R$: every interval $(-\eps, \eps)$ around $0$ contains non-zero numbers. For any finite collection of open sets $U_1, \dots, U_k$ containing $p$, each set provides a positive radius $\eps_i > 0$. The minimum $\eps = \min\{\eps_1, \dots, \eps_k\}$ is strictly positive, so $(p - \eps, p + \eps)$ lies inside the intersection. With infinitely many sets, the infimum of the radii can collapse to zero.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If an open set contains an open neighborhood around every one of its points, what is a \emph{closed set}?
A subset $C \subseteq X$ is defined to be closed if its complement $X \setminus C$ is open---meaning that every point outside $C$ has an open neighborhood completely disjoint from $C$. Consequently, $C$ already contains all of its boundary and accumulation points.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Competing Axioms and the Heine--Borel Illusion}

Topology stripped away familiar metric coordinates, creating subtle questions and pitfalls:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Axiomatic Debate on ``What Is a Space?''}: In the 1910s, Fr\'echet, Riesz, and Hausdorff considered competing foundations for abstract spaces. Fr\'echet used sequences ($L$-spaces, taking sequence convergence as primitive). Riesz focused on accumulation points. Hausdorff championed open neighborhoods, originally requiring that distinct points have disjoint neighborhoods (the $T_2$ or Hausdorff condition). In the 1950s, algebraic geometry showed that Hausdorff's separation axiom was not universal: the Zariski topology on algebraic varieties (where closed sets are zero-sets of polynomials) has open sets so large that any two non-empty open sets intersect. Even without the Hausdorff property, the Zariski topology is mathematically consistent and central to modern algebraic geometry.
  \item \textbf{The Heine--Borel Illusion in Infinite Dimensions}: In Euclidean space $\R^n$, the Heine--Borel theorem states that a set is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded. It is tempting to assume that ``closed and bounded'' implies compactness in every metric space. This fails in infinite dimensions. Consider the Hilbert space $\ell^2$ of infinite square-summable sequences $(x_1, x_2, \dots)$ satisfying $\sum_{n=1}^\infty x_n^2 < \infty$, with metric $\|x - y\| = \sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^\infty (x_n - y_n)^2}$. Look at the sequence of standard unit basis vectors:
  \[
  e_1 = (1, 0, 0, \dots), \quad e_2 = (0, 1, 0, \dots), \quad e_3 = (0, 0, 1, \dots), \quad \dots
  \]
  The set $S = \{e_1, e_2, e_3, \dots\}$ is bounded (each vector has $\|e_n\| = 1$) and closed (the distance between any two distinct basis vectors is $\|e_n - e_m\| = \sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{2}$, so $S$ has no accumulation points). Yet $S$ is not compact: if we place an open ball of radius $1/2$ around each $e_n$, the balls are pairwise disjoint because $\|e_n - e_m\| = \sqrt{2} > 1$. The union of these open balls covers $S$, but removing even one ball leaves that basis vector uncovered. Thus no finite subcover exists. In infinite dimensions, closed and bounded sets need not be compact; compactness must be verified via open covers.
  \item \textbf{The Forward-Open Fallacy}: A common novice intuition is that if $f: X \to Y$ is continuous and $U \subseteq X$ is open, then the image $f(U)$ should be open. It is not! Consider the constant function $f: \R \to \R$ defined by $f(x) = 0$. The open interval $(-1, 1)$ pushes forward to the single point $\{0\}$, which has no wiggle room in $\R$ and is closed, not open. Continuity is strictly a backward guarantee: preimages of open sets are open ($f^{-1}(V)$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Continuity Without Rulers: The Pullback Architecture}

In metric calculus, you were taught that a function $f: X \to Y$ is continuous at $x$ if
\[
\forall \eps > 0, \; \exists \delta > 0, \; \forall t \in X, \quad |t - x| < \delta \implies |f(t) - f(x)| < \eps.
\]
Notice that distances appear in that formula only to carve out open balls $B_\delta(x)$ and $B_\eps(f(x))$.

Once we express those balls directly as open sets, continuity no longer needs a metric at all:
\begin{quote}
\emph{A function $f: X \to Y$ is continuous if and only if the preimage of every open set in $Y$ is open in $X$:}
\[
V \in \mathcal{T}_Y \implies f^{-1}(V) \in \mathcal{T}_X.
\]
\end{quote}

Why does continuity look backwards? Why do open sets pull back, rather than push forward?

Imagine an engineer adjusting input dials $x \in X$ to hit a target outcome $y = f(x) \in Y$. The observer at the output specifies an allowable margin of tolerance: an open set $V \subseteq Y$ around the desired result. The engineer needs to know: what range of dial settings guarantees that the outcome lands safely inside $V$? That safe tolerance region is precisely the preimage:
\[
f^{-1}(V) = \{x \in X : f(x) \in V\}.
\]
Continuity promises that whenever the target margin $V$ is open in $Y$, the allowable dial settings $f^{-1}(V)$ form an open set in $X$. Small variations around an input $x$ remain within the allowable tolerance. Discontinuity, by contrast, means that an arbitrarily narrow tolerance at the output can require infinite precision at the input.

Forward images of open sets can fail to be open even for simple functions. For example, if $f: \R \to \R$ is the constant function $f(x) = 5$, the open interval $(1, 3)$ pushes forward to the single point $\{5\}$, which is closed, not open. But preimages never misbehave: the preimage of $(4, 6)$ is $f^{-1}((4, 6)) = \R$ (open), and the preimage of $(6, 7)$ is $\emptyset$ (open). Preimages preserve unions, intersections, and complements without exception.

\paragraph{Connecting Back to Calculus: How Open Sets Recover $\eps$--$\delta$.}
To see that the topological definition is not a departure from analysis but its purification, let us derive the classic $\eps$--$\delta$ definition directly from it on the real line $\R$.

Suppose $f: \R \to \R$ satisfies the topological condition: preimages of open sets are open. Fix $x \in \R$ and let $\eps > 0$ be any positive tolerance. The interval $V = (f(x) - \eps, f(x) + \eps)$ is an open set in $\R$ containing $f(x)$. By hypothesis, its preimage $f^{-1}(V)$ is an open set in $\R$. Since $f(x) \in V$, the point $x$ belongs to $f^{-1}(V)$.

Now invoke the definition of openness in $\R$: because $x \in f^{-1}(V)$ and $f^{-1}(V)$ is open, there must exist an open buffer $(x - \delta, x + \delta) \subseteq f^{-1}(V)$ for some $\delta > 0$. What does this inclusion say? It means:
\[
|t - x| < \delta \implies t \in f^{-1}(V) \implies f(t) \in V \implies |f(t) - f(x)| < \eps.
\]
The classical $\eps$--$\delta$ definition is simply the pullback of an open interval expressed in metric notation.

\paragraph{The Power of the Pullback: Composition in One Line.}
In calculus, proving that the composition of two continuous functions $g \circ f$ is continuous requires juggling two nested layers of $\eps$ and $\delta$. In topology, the proof is an immediate one-line consequence of set algebra.

If $f: X \to Y$ and $g: Y \to Z$ are continuous, let $W \subseteq Z$ be open. Because $g$ is continuous, $g^{-1}(W)$ is open in $Y$. Because $f$ is continuous, $f^{-1}(g^{-1}(W))$ is open in $X$. Since $(g \circ f)^{-1}(W) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(W))$, the preimage $(g \circ f)^{-1}(W)$ is open in $X$. This establishes continuity directly from set preimages.

\subsection{Connectedness and Compactness}

Two topological properties dominate modern geometry: \textbf{connectedness} and \textbf{compactness}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Connectedness (No Tearing)}: A topological space $X$ is \textbf{connected} if it cannot be partitioned into two non-empty, disjoint open sets: there do not exist open sets $U, V \subseteq X$ such that
  \[
  X = U \cup V, \quad U \cap V = \emptyset, \quad U \neq \emptyset, \quad V \neq \emptyset.
  \]
  Equivalently, the only subsets of $X$ that are simultaneously open and closed (``clopen'') are the empty set $\emptyset$ and the whole space $X$. If such a pair $U, V$ existed, the space would be partitioned into two disjoint open pieces. For instance, the subspace $X = (0, 1) \cup (2, 3) \subset \R$ is disconnected because $(0, 1)$ and $(2, 3)$ are disjoint open subsets of $X$ whose union is all of $X$. By contrast, the entire real line $\R$ and any closed interval $[a, b]$ are connected.
  
  Connectedness is the topological engine behind the Intermediate Value Theorem. If $X$ is connected and $f: X \to \R$ is continuous, the image $f(X)$ cannot skip any values: if it missed a value $c$ between two outputs, the preimages of $(-\infty, c)$ and $(c, \infty)$ would tear $X$ into two disjoint open pieces, a contradiction.

  \item \textbf{Compactness (Taming Infinity)}: A topological space $X$ is \textbf{compact} if every open cover has a finite subcover: whenever $X \subseteq \bigcup_{\alpha \in A} U_\alpha$ where each $U_\alpha$ is open in $X$, there exists a \emph{finite} subcollection $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_k\} \subseteq A$ such that
  \[
  X \subseteq U_{\alpha_1} \cup U_{\alpha_2} \cup \cdots \cup U_{\alpha_k}.
  \]
\end{itemize}

Compactness is the topological counterpart of finiteness. To see the intuition, contrast the open interval $(0, 1)$ with the closed interval $[0, 1]$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item On the open interval $(0, 1)$, consider the open cover $U_n = (1/n, 1)$ for $n = 2, 3, 4, \dots$. As $n$ grows, these intervals creep toward $0$, covering every point in $(0, 1)$. But if you select any finite subcollection, there is a maximum index $N$, covering only $(1/N, 1)$ and leaving $(0, 1/N]$ stranded. The points escaped through the missing boundary point at $0$. Thus $(0, 1)$ is not compact.
  \item On the closed interval $[0, 1]$, the boundary point $0$ belongs to the space. Any open cover must include an open set $U_0$ that contains $0$. Because $U_0$ is open in $[0, 1]$, it contains an interval $[0, \eps)$ for some $\eps > 0$. That single set contains all fractions $1/n$ with $n > 1/\eps$. Only finitely many points $1/n$ with $n \le 1/\eps$ remain outside $U_0$, each of which is covered by finitely many additional open sets.
\end{itemize}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Why is the entire real line $\R$ not compact?
Consider the family of open intervals $U_n = (-n, n)$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Every real number $x$ is covered (since $|x| < \lceil |x| \rceil + 1$), but any finite subcollection has a maximum index $M$, covering only $(-M, M)$ and leaving all numbers with $|x| \ge M$ uncovered. In $\R$, compactness requires a set to be both bounded (no escape to infinity) and closed (containing all limit points).
\end{recoverybox}

Compactness guarantees that continuous real-valued functions attain their maximum and minimum values (the Extreme Value Theorem), and that local behaviors (true in microscopic open bubbles) can be uniformly stitched together into a global finite bound.

\subsection{What Topology Unlocks---and Its Limits}

Topology provides the structural skeleton of modern geometry:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Rubber-Sheet Invariants}: Invariants like the Euler characteristic ($\chi = V - E + F$), the fundamental group $\pi_1(X)$ (which counts non-contractible loops), and homology groups $H_k(X)$ (which detect $k$-dimensional cavities and tunnels) classify spaces regardless of how they are stretched or deformed.
  \item \textbf{Tychonoff's Product Theorem}: Compactness is preserved across arbitrary infinite products of compact spaces. This serves as the foundation for modern probability theory (the Kolmogorov extension theorem, constructing infinite-dimensional stochastic processes) and functional analysis (the Banach--Alaoglu theorem, guaranteeing weak-star compactness of dual unit balls).
  \item \textbf{Fixed-Point Theorems}: The Brouwer fixed-point theorem (every continuous map from a closed disk to itself has a point satisfying $f(x) = x$) and its set-valued generalization, the Kakutani theorem, guarantee the existence of solutions in non-linear systems and prove the existence of Nash equilibria in game theory.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
Yet qualitative topology has deliberate omissions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Blindness to Metric Scale, Angles, and Curvature}: Topology cannot distinguish between a tiny pebble and a star, nor between a flat tabletop and a curved sphere, because both can be deformed into one another. It cannot compute tangent vectors, velocities, geodesics, or gravitational forces.
  \item \textbf{Topological Pathologies}: Point-set topology permits counterintuitive examples---such as the topologist's sine curve (the graph of $y = \sin(1/x)$ on $(0, 1]$ joined with the vertical segment $\{0\} \times [-1, 1]$, which is connected but has no continuous path connecting the bar to the curve) or space-filling Peano curves that map a one-dimensional interval onto an entire two-dimensional square continuously.
\end{itemize}

To do physics, compute curvature, and define tangent vectors without losing geometric realism, we must reintroduce calculus on top of topological spaces. In Chapter~38 (\emph{Second Pass: Geometry, Coordinates, and What Is Real}), we discover how smooth manifolds and coordinate charts allow calculus to live on curved spaces, culminating in Einstein's general relativity.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Let $X = \{a, b, c\}$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item List all topologies on $X$ that contain exactly 3 open sets.
    \item Is the collection $\mathcal{T} = \{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{b\}, X\}$ a valid topology on $X$? Why or why not?
  \end{enumerate}

  \item Prove that the open interval $(0, 1)$ is not compact by showing that the open cover $U_n = (1/n, 1)$ for $n = 2, 3, 4, \dots$ admits no finite subcover.
  \item Show that a discrete topological space $X$ (where every subset of $X$ is declared open) is compact if and only if $X$ is a finite set.
  \item \textbf{Compactness and the Extreme Value Theorem}: Let $X$ be a non-empty compact space and let $f: X \to \R$ be continuous (with $\R$ given its standard metric topology).
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Prove that the image $f(X) \subseteq \R$ is compact. (Hint: Take an open cover of $f(X)$ in $\R$ and pull it back to an open cover of $X$.)
    \item By the Heine--Borel theorem on $\R$, a compact subset of $\R$ is closed and bounded. Conclude that the supremum $M = \sup f(X)$ is finite and that there exists some $x_{\max} \in X$ such that $f(x_{\max}) = M$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
