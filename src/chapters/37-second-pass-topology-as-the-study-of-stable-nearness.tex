\section{Second Pass: Topology as the Study of Stable Nearness}
\chapterline

In Chapter~36, abstract algebra set aside particular numbers and worked only with the laws an operation obeys. This chapter asks the matching question for geometry and analysis: \emph{how can we describe space and continuity without measuring distances?}

In elementary geometry, everything is measured: lengths, angles, perimeters, and areas. In calculus, limits and derivatives depend on distances: you measure $|x - p|$, compare it to $\delta$, and demand that $|f(x) - f(p)|$ be smaller than $\eps$. But what if the space you are studying has no natural coordinate grid and no ruler? How can you say that two points are close to each other, that a shape is connected in one piece, or that a function does not tear?

The answer is the \textbf{open set}. Instead of a number saying how far apart two points are, we keep only a list of ``neighborhoods'': regions that count as surrounding a point. Topology studies the properties that survive under homeomorphisms (Chapter~22): continuous bijections whose inverses are also continuous, that is, ways of stretching and bending one space into another without tearing or gluing. The ``stable nearness'' of the title means exactly this: a property is \emph{stable} if it still holds after any small enough change of the point.

\begin{historybox}
Around 1900, mathematicians needed a notion of distance for new kinds of objects. In classical physics, calculus took place in three-dimensional space $\R^3$, where Euclidean distance was obvious. But in the calculus of variations---the branch of mathematics that seeks paths or surfaces minimizing physical quantities like energy, travel time, or soap-film area---the ``points'' under study were no longer numbers or vectors. Each point was an entire function, curve, or physical field.

In 1906, the French mathematician Maurice Fr\'echet generalized Euclidean distance by introducing \emph{metric spaces}, where any two elements have an assigned numerical distance satisfying the triangle inequality (such as measuring the distance between two functions by integrating the difference between their graphs). Such a distance function is called a \emph{metric}. Metrics still rest on numbers, and some natural notions of convergence (what it means for a sequence of objects to approach a limit) cannot be described by any single distance function.

In 1914, Felix Hausdorff published \emph{Grundz\"uge der Mengenlehre} (\emph{Foundations of Set Theory}). Hausdorff discarded numerical rulers entirely, defining a space purely through abstract open neighborhoods: regions enclosing each point with room to spare. In 1922, the Polish mathematician Kazimierz Kuratowski gave a set of axioms for the \emph{closure} of a set (the set together with all points on its brink).

Point-set topology (the part of topology built directly on open sets) became a foundation for functional analysis (the study of spaces whose points are functions), quantum mechanics (where physical states live in infinite-dimensional function spaces), and algebraic topology (which uses algebra to count holes in shapes). In 1930, the Soviet mathematician Andrey Tychonoff proved that the product of \emph{any} collection of compact spaces is compact (compactness is defined later in this chapter); the result is used to prove that solutions exist in differential equations and probability theory. Open questions remain. Grigori Perelman proved the 3-dimensional Poincar\'e conjecture (Chapter~22) in 2003, but the smooth 4-dimensional version is still open: it asks whether there is a 4-dimensional sphere that can be deformed into the standard 4-sphere continuously but not smoothly (``homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic'').
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Open Sets as Stable Properties}

On the real line $\R$, an open interval like $(2, 5)$ gives every point wiggle room. If $p = 2.001$, it is close to the edge, but you can still draw a small interval $(2.0005, 2.0015)$ around it that remains entirely inside $(2, 5)$.

In contrast, the closed interval $[2, 5]$ includes the boundary point $2$. No matter how tiny an open interval you place around $2$, its left half spills outside $[2, 5]$ into numbers less than $2$. Figure~\ref{37:fig-wiggle-room} shows both cases.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of an open set as describing a \textbf{stable property}: one that survives small changes. If a state satisfies $x \in U$, where $U$ is open, then every point close enough to $x$ is also in $U$. A closed set contains its boundary points, whereas every point of an open set is an interior point (it has a small ball around it inside the set).
\end{intuitionbox}

A \textbf{topological space} $(X, \mathcal{T})$ (Chapter~22) turns this picture into a definition. Here $X$ is any set and $\mathcal{T}$ (``tau'', for topology) is a collection of subsets of $X$ that we declare to be ``open,'' subject to three axioms:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The entire space $X$ and the empty set $\emptyset$ are open.
  \item The union of \emph{any} collection of open sets is open.
  \item The intersection of \emph{any finite} collection of open sets is open.
\end{enumerate}

Why does the third axiom restrict itself to \emph{finite} intersections? Consider what happens on the real line if you intersect infinitely many open intervals centered at $0$:
\[
U_n = \left(-\frac{1}{n}, \frac{1}{n}\right) \quad \text{for } n = 1, 2, 3, \dots.
\]
Each individual $U_n$ is open: every point inside has an open neighborhood contained in $U_n$. But their intersection across all $n \in \N$ leaves only a single number (Figure~\ref{37:fig-shrinking-intervals}):
\[
\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \left(-\frac{1}{n}, \frac{1}{n}\right) = \{0\}.
\]

The singleton set $\{0\}$ is not open in $\R$: every interval $(-\eps, \eps)$ around $0$ contains non-zero numbers. For any finite collection of open sets $U_1, \dots, U_k$ containing $p$, each set provides a positive radius $\eps_i > 0$. The minimum $\eps = \min\{\eps_1, \dots, \eps_k\}$ is strictly positive, so $(p - \eps, p + \eps)$ lies inside the intersection. With infinitely many sets there may be no smallest radius, and the infimum (greatest lower bound, Chapter~16) of the radii can be zero.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If an open set contains an open neighborhood around every one of its points, what is a \emph{closed set}?
A subset $C \subseteq X$ is defined to be closed if its complement $X \setminus C$ is open---meaning that every point outside $C$ has an open neighborhood completely disjoint from $C$. Consequently, $C$ contains all of its boundary points and all of its \emph{accumulation points}. (A point $q$ is an accumulation point of $C$ if every open set around $q$ contains a point of $C$ other than $q$; for example, $0$ is an accumulation point of $\{1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \dots\}$, as Figure~\ref{37:fig-accumulation} shows.)
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Competing Axioms and the Heine--Borel Illusion}

Dropping distance raises questions about which definitions are the right ones, and it sets some traps:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Axiomatic Debate on ``What Is a Space?''}: In the 1910s, Fr\'echet, Riesz, and Hausdorff considered competing foundations for abstract spaces. Fr\'echet used sequences ($L$-spaces, taking sequence convergence as primitive). Riesz focused on accumulation points. Hausdorff championed open neighborhoods, originally requiring that distinct points have disjoint neighborhoods (the $T_2$ or Hausdorff condition). In the 1950s, algebraic geometry showed that Hausdorff's separation axiom was not universal: the Zariski topology (Chapter~39) on an irreducible algebraic variety (a shape cut out by polynomial equations that does not split into two smaller such shapes), where the closed sets are the zero-sets of polynomials, has open sets so large that any two non-empty open sets intersect. The Zariski topology fails the Hausdorff condition and is still central to modern algebraic geometry.
  \item \textbf{The Heine--Borel Illusion in Infinite Dimensions}: Compactness is defined fully later in this chapter; for now, a set $K$ is \emph{compact} if every \emph{open cover} of $K$ (a collection of open sets whose union contains $K$) has a \emph{finite subcover} (finitely many of those sets already contain $K$). In Euclidean space $\R^n$, the Heine--Borel theorem (Chapter~35) states that a set is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded (fits inside some ball of finite radius). It is tempting to assume that ``closed and bounded'' implies compactness in every metric space. This fails in infinite dimensions. Consider the space $\ell^2$ (an example of a \emph{Hilbert space}) of infinite sequences $(x_1, x_2, \dots)$ that are \emph{square-summable}, meaning that the sum of their squares is finite: $x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots < \infty$. The distance between two sequences $x$ and $y$ is
  \[
  \|x - y\| = \sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (x_n - y_n)^2},
  \]
  the infinite-dimensional version of the Pythagorean distance, and $\|x\|$ is the distance from $x$ to the zero sequence. Look at the sequence of standard unit basis vectors:
  \[
  e_1 = (1, 0, 0, \dots), \quad e_2 = (0, 1, 0, \dots), \quad e_3 = (0, 0, 1, \dots), \quad \dots.
  \]
  The set $S = \{e_1, e_2, e_3, \dots\}$ is bounded, since each vector has $\|e_n\| = 1$. It is also closed. Two distinct basis vectors $e_n$ and $e_m$ differ by $1$ in coordinate $n$ and by $-1$ in coordinate $m$, so
  \[
  \|e_n - e_m\| = \sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{2} \qquad (n \neq m),
  \]
  and an open ball of radius less than $\sqrt2/2$ around any point contains at most one point of $S$; hence $S$ has no accumulation points. Yet $S$ is not compact: if we place an open ball of radius $1/2$ around each $e_n$, the balls are pairwise disjoint because $\|e_n - e_m\| = \sqrt{2} > 1$. Figure~\ref{37:fig-l2-basis} draws the first three. These balls form an open cover of $S$, and each ball contains exactly one point of $S$, so any finite collection of them misses all but finitely many basis vectors. Thus no finite subcover exists. In infinite dimensions, closed and bounded sets need not be compact; compactness must be checked against open covers.
  \item \textbf{The Forward-Open Fallacy}: A common beginner's guess is that if $f\colon X \to Y$ is continuous and $U \subseteq X$ is open, then the image $f(U)$ (also called the \emph{pushforward} of $U$) should be open. It is not. Consider the squaring function $f\colon \R \to \R$ defined by $f(x) = x^2$. The open interval $(-1, 1)$ has image $[0, 1)$, which contains the point $0$ but no interval around it, so it is not open in $\R$. Continuity is a backward guarantee: preimages of open sets are open ($f^{-1}(V)$ is open whenever $V$ is).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Continuity Without Rulers: Pulling Back Open Sets}

In calculus, you were taught that a function $f\colon \R \to \R$ is continuous at $x$ if
\[
\forall \eps > 0, \; \exists \delta > 0, \; \forall t \in \R, \quad |t - x| < \delta \implies |f(t) - f(x)| < \eps.
\]
Distances appear in that formula only to carve out open balls: $B_\delta(x)$, the set of points within distance $\delta$ of $x$, and $B_\eps(f(x))$, the set of points within distance $\eps$ of $f(x)$.

Once we express those balls directly as open sets, continuity no longer needs a metric at all. Write $\mathcal{T}_X$ and $\mathcal{T}_Y$ for the collections of open sets of $X$ and $Y$:
\begin{quote}
\emph{A function $f\colon X \to Y$ is continuous if and only if the preimage of every open set in $Y$ is open in $X$:}
\[
V \in \mathcal{T}_Y \implies f^{-1}(V) \in \mathcal{T}_X.
\]
\end{quote}

Why does continuity look backwards? Why do open sets pull back (taking the preimage of a set is called \emph{pulling it back}), rather than push forward?

Imagine an engineer adjusting input dials $x \in X$ to hit a target outcome $y = f(x) \in Y$. The observer at the output specifies an allowable margin of tolerance: an open set $V \subseteq Y$ around the desired result. The engineer needs to know: what range of dial settings guarantees that the outcome lands safely inside $V$? That safe tolerance region is precisely the preimage:
\[
f^{-1}(V) = \{x \in X : f(x) \in V\}.
\]
Continuity promises that whenever the target margin $V$ is open in $Y$, the allowable dial settings $f^{-1}(V)$ form an open set in $X$, so small variations around an input $x$ stay within the allowable tolerance (Figure~\ref{37:fig-pullback}). Discontinuity at $x$, by contrast, means that for some narrow tolerance $V$ the set of good inputs contains $x$ but no interval around $x$: settings arbitrarily close to $x$ miss the target (right panel of Figure~\ref{37:fig-eps-delta-band}).

The constant function gives a second example of an image that is not open. If $f\colon \R \to \R$ is $f(x) = 5$, the open interval $(1, 3)$ has image $\{5\}$, which is closed, not open. \paragraph{Connecting Back to Calculus: How Open Sets Recover $\eps$--$\delta$.}
On the real line, the open-set definition gives back the $\eps$--$\delta$ definition. Here is the derivation.

Suppose $f\colon \R \to \R$ satisfies the topological condition: preimages of open sets are open. Fix $x \in \R$ and let $\eps > 0$ be any positive tolerance. The interval $V = (f(x) - \eps, f(x) + \eps)$ is an open set in $\R$ containing $f(x)$. By hypothesis, its preimage $f^{-1}(V)$ is an open set in $\R$. Since $f(x) \in V$, the point $x$ belongs to $f^{-1}(V)$.

Now invoke the definition of openness in $\R$: because $x \in f^{-1}(V)$ and $f^{-1}(V)$ is open, there must exist an open buffer $(x - \delta, x + \delta) \subseteq f^{-1}(V)$ for some $\delta > 0$. What does this inclusion say? It means:
\begin{align*}
|t - x| < \delta
  &\implies t \in f^{-1}(V) && \text{(because $(x - \delta, x + \delta) \subseteq f^{-1}(V)$)}\\
  &\implies f(t) \in V && \text{(definition of preimage)}\\
  &\implies |f(t) - f(x)| < \eps && \text{(definition of $V$)}.
\end{align*}
The classical $\eps$--$\delta$ condition is the statement ``the preimage of an open interval is open,'' written in terms of distances. The left panel of Figure~\ref{37:fig-eps-delta-band} draws the argument on a graph.

\paragraph{Composition in One Line.}
In calculus, proving that the composition of two continuous functions $g \circ f$ is continuous requires juggling two nested layers of $\eps$ and $\delta$. With open sets, the proof is one line of set algebra.

If $f\colon X \to Y$ and $g\colon Y \to Z$ are continuous, let $W \subseteq Z$ be open. Then
\begin{align*}
g^{-1}(W) &\text{ is open in } Y && \text{(because $g$ is continuous)},\\
f^{-1}\bigl(g^{-1}(W)\bigr) &\text{ is open in } X && \text{(because $f$ is continuous)},
\end{align*}
and since $(g \circ f)^{-1}(W) = f^{-1}\bigl(g^{-1}(W)\bigr)$, the preimage $(g \circ f)^{-1}(W)$ is open in $X$. So $g \circ f$ is continuous. Figure~\ref{37:fig-composition} shows the two pull-backs one after the other.

\subsection{Connectedness and Compactness}

Two properties of spaces come up again and again in geometry and analysis: \textbf{connectedness} and \textbf{compactness}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Connectedness (No Tearing)}: A topological space $X$ is \textbf{connected} if it cannot be partitioned into two non-empty, disjoint open sets: there do not exist open sets $U, V \subseteq X$ such that
  \[
  X = U \cup V, \quad U \cap V = \emptyset, \quad U \neq \emptyset, \quad V \neq \emptyset.
  \]
  Equivalently, the only subsets of $X$ that are simultaneously open and closed (``clopen'') are the empty set $\emptyset$ and the whole space $X$. The two forms match because if such a pair $U, V$ existed, then $U = X \setminus V$ would be closed as well as open, and neither empty nor all of $X$. When $X$ is a subset of $\R$, ``open in $X$'' means open in the \emph{subspace topology}: a set $W \subseteq X$ is open in $X$ exactly when $W = O \cap X$ for some open set $O \subseteq \R$. For instance, the subspace $X = (0, 1) \cup (2, 3) \subset \R$ is disconnected because $(0, 1)$ and $(2, 3)$ are disjoint open subsets of $X$ whose union is all of $X$ (Figure~\ref{37:fig-subspace}). By contrast, the entire real line $\R$ and any closed interval $[a, b]$ are connected.

  \item \textbf{Compactness (Taming Infinity)}: An \textbf{open cover} of a space $X$ is a collection of open sets $U_\alpha$, one for each label $\alpha$ in some index set $A$, whose union contains all of $X$. A \textbf{finite subcover} is a finite selection of those sets that still covers $X$. The space $X$ is \textbf{compact} if every open cover has a finite subcover: whenever $X \subseteq \bigcup_{\alpha \in A} U_\alpha$ where each $U_\alpha$ is open in $X$, there exists a \emph{finite} subcollection $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_k\} \subseteq A$ such that
  \[
  X \subseteq U_{\alpha_1} \cup U_{\alpha_2} \cup \cdots \cup U_{\alpha_k}.
  \]
\end{itemize}

Compactness is the topological counterpart of finiteness. To see the idea, contrast the open interval $(0, 1)$ with the closed interval $[0, 1]$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item On the open interval $(0, 1)$, consider the open cover $U_n = (1/n, 1)$ for $n = 2, 3, 4, \dots$. As $n$ grows, these intervals creep toward $0$, covering every point in $(0, 1)$. But if you select any finite subcollection, there is a maximum index $N$, covering only $(1/N, 1)$ and leaving $(0, 1/N]$ uncovered. The uncovered points crowd toward the endpoint $0$, which is missing from the space. Thus $(0, 1)$ is not compact.
  \item On the closed interval $[0, 1]$, the boundary point $0$ belongs to the space. Any open cover must include an open set $U_0$ that contains $0$. Because $U_0$ is open in $[0, 1]$, it contains an interval $[0, \eps)$ for some $\eps > 0$, so the creeping trick above no longer strands anything near $0$. A full proof must handle every open cover at once; here is the standard argument. Call $t \in [0, 1]$ \emph{reachable} if $[0, t]$ can be covered by finitely many sets from the cover; $t = 0$ is reachable, so let $s$ be the supremum of the reachable points. The point $s$ lies in some cover set $U$, which contains $(s - \eta, s + \eta) \cap [0, 1]$ for some $\eta > 0$. Choose a reachable $t$ with $s - \eta < t \le s$; adding $U$ to the finitely many sets covering $[0, t]$ covers $[0, \min\{s + \eta/2, 1\}]$. If $s < 1$, this reaches past $s$, contradicting the choice of $s$ as the supremum. So $s = 1$, and the same step covers all of $[0, 1]$ with finitely many sets. Figure~\ref{37:fig-reachable} draws one step of this argument.
\end{itemize}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Why is the entire real line $\R$ not compact?
Consider the family of open intervals $U_n = (-n, n)$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Every real number $x$ is covered (since $|x| < \lceil |x| \rceil + 1$, where $\lceil |x| \rceil$ is $|x|$ rounded up to a whole number), but any finite subcollection has a maximum index $M$, covering only $(-M, M)$ and leaving all numbers with $|x| \ge M$ uncovered. In $\R$, compactness requires a set to be both bounded (no escape to infinity) and closed (containing all its accumulation points).
\end{recoverybox}

Compactness guarantees that a continuous real-valued function on a compact space attains a maximum and a minimum value (the Extreme Value Theorem; Exercise~5 walks through the proof). It also lets you turn local facts into global ones: if each point has an open set around it on which some quantity is bounded, finitely many of those open sets cover the space, and the largest of those finitely many bounds works everywhere.

\subsection{What Topology Gives Us, and Its Limits}

Three uses of topology:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Rubber-Sheet Invariants}: An \emph{invariant} is a quantity that does not change when a space is stretched or bent without tearing. Examples are the Euler characteristic $\chi = V - E + F$ (for a surface divided into polygons, $V$ counts corners, $E$ edges and $F$ faces), the fundamental group $\pi_1(X)$ (which records the loops in $X$ that cannot be shrunk to a point), and the homology groups $H_k(X)$ (which detect $k$-dimensional holes, such as the tunnel through a doughnut or the hollow inside a sphere). If two spaces have different invariants, they cannot be homeomorphic. Figure~\ref{37:fig-euler} computes $\chi$ for three surfaces.
  \item \textbf{Tychonoff's Product Theorem}: A product of compact spaces is compact, even a product of infinitely many. Probability theory uses it (in the Kolmogorov extension theorem) to build random processes that run over infinitely many time steps, and functional analysis uses it (in the Banach--Alaoglu theorem) to find convergent families inside infinite-dimensional spaces.
  \item \textbf{Fixed-Point Theorems}: A \emph{fixed point} of $f$ is a point with $f(x) = x$. The Brouwer fixed-point theorem says every continuous map from a closed disk to itself has a fixed point. Its version for maps that send each point to a set of points, the Kakutani theorem, guarantees that solutions exist in non-linear systems and proves that every finite game has a Nash equilibrium: a choice of (possibly randomized) strategies in which no player can do better by changing strategy alone.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
Topology also leaves things out:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Blindness to Metric Scale, Angles, and Curvature}: Topology cannot distinguish between a tiny pebble and a star, nor between a flat square and a curved dome, because one can be deformed into the other. It cannot compute tangent vectors, velocities, geodesics (shortest paths on a curved surface), or gravitational forces.
  \item \textbf{Topological Pathologies}: Point-set topology permits counterintuitive examples, called \emph{pathologies}. One is the topologist's sine curve: the graph of $y = \sin(1/x)$ on $(0, 1]$ joined with the vertical segment $\{0\} \times [-1, 1]$. It is connected, yet no continuous path inside it joins the segment to the curve (Figure~\ref{37:fig-sine-curve}). Another is a space-filling Peano curve, a continuous map from a one-dimensional interval onto an entire two-dimensional square.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Let $X = \{a, b, c\}$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item List all topologies on $X$ that contain exactly 3 open sets.
    \item Is the collection $\mathcal{T} = \{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{b\}, X\}$ a valid topology on $X$? Why or why not?
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Prove that if $f\colon X \to Y$ is continuous and $X$ is connected, then the image $f(X)$ is connected in $Y$. (Hint: Assume for contradiction that $f(X) = U \cup V$ is a separation in the subspace topology of $f(X)$, and examine the preimages $f^{-1}(U)$ and $f^{-1}(V)$ in $X$.)
  \item Prove that the open interval $(0, 1)$ is not compact by showing that the open cover $U_n = (1/n, 1)$ for $n = 2, 3, 4, \dots$ admits no finite subcover.
  \item Show that a discrete topological space $X$ (where every subset of $X$ is declared open) is compact if and only if $X$ is a finite set.
  \item \textbf{Compactness and the Extreme Value Theorem}: Let $X$ be a non-empty compact space and let $f\colon X \to \R$ be continuous (with $\R$ given its usual open sets, the unions of open intervals).
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Prove that the image $f(X) \subseteq \R$ is compact. (Hint: Take an open cover of $f(X)$ in $\R$ and pull it back to an open cover of $X$.)
    \item By the Heine--Borel theorem on $\R$, a compact subset of $\R$ is closed and bounded. Conclude that the supremum $M = \sup f(X)$ is finite and that there exists some $x_{\max} \in X$ such that $f(x_{\max}) = M$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
