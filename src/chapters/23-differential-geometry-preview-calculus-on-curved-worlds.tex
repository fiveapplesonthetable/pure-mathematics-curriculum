\subsection{The geodetic surveyor: how mountain peaks forced a new geometry}

In Chapter~22 we set aside the ruler. Without distances and angles, topology kept only the features of a space that survive continuous stretching: which sets are open, which points can be joined by a path, and where the holes are.

Physics and engineering need more than rubber sheets. To calculate planetary orbits, predict air flow over a curved wing, or describe gravity, we need numbers: velocities, accelerations, lengths, areas, and the equations that relate them. That means calculus.

The difficulty is that curved spaces only look flat up close. If you stand in the middle of an open field and look around, the ground appears flat. You can lay down a Cartesian coordinate grid, set up perpendicular $x$ and $y$ axes, and use ordinary calculus in two variables to compute slopes, tangent lines, and areas.

But from space you see that you are standing on the surface of a sphere. No single $xy$-grid covers the whole Earth. Any attempt to flatten the entire globe onto a single sheet of paper tears continents or distorts distances beyond recognition.

Between 1821 and 1825, King George IV commissioned Carl Friedrich Gauss to lead the \emph{geodetic survey} of the Kingdom of Hanover, measuring the terrain and computing accurate distances across the region. During the survey, Gauss used the \emph{heliotrope} (a precision mirror device to reflect flashes of sunlight across dozens of kilometers) to measure large triangles between the peaks of the Brocken, the Hoher Hagen, and the Inselsberg.

Working with these large-scale measurements led Gauss to consider whether an observer living \emph{entirely within} a two-dimensional surface could determine whether the surface was flat or curved, without referencing an ambient third dimension.

In his 1827 treatise, \emph{Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas} (``General Investigations of Curved Surfaces''), Gauss proved his \textbf{Theorema Egregium} (Latin for ``Remarkable Theorem''): \emph{curvature is intrinsic}. An observer living on a surface can measure its curvature purely by taking distance and angle measurements along paths within the surface itself, without any reference to a surrounding higher-dimensional space. Curvature of this kind is called \textbf{intrinsic}. By contrast, \textbf{extrinsic curvature} is bending that exists only relative to a surrounding space. Rolling a flat sheet of paper into a cylinder curves it in three dimensions, but an ant living on the paper still measures the same distances as on a flat sheet: its intrinsic curvature is zero (Figure~\ref{23:fig-cylinder-unroll}). On a sphere, however, the curvature is intrinsic: no patch of a sphere can be flattened without stretching or tearing.

\subsection{The challenge of curved space}

\textbf{Differential geometry} is calculus on curved spaces, done by approximating them near each point with flat spaces. Because a curved space rarely has a single coordinate grid covering all of it, derivatives, velocity vectors, and integrals have to be built out of small flat patches.

The strategy has three layers, shown in Figure~\ref{23:fig-chart-tangent} and made precise in the sections below:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The global curved space} (the manifold $M$).
\item \textbf{A local flat map} (a coordinate chart $\phi$), which sends a small open patch $U \subset M$ onto an ordinary open region of $\R^n$, so that each point of $U$ gets $n$ numbers as coordinates.
\item \textbf{A local linear space} (the tangent plane $T_p M$ at a point $p$), a flat plane touching $M$ at $p$, where the velocities of points moving on $M$ live as true vectors.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Manifolds: locally Euclidean worlds}

A space that may be curved overall but is flat up close is called a \textbf{manifold}: it may bend and twist, but any tiny patch looks like a piece of flat space $\R^n$ to someone standing in it.

\begin{definition}
An \textbf{$n$-dimensional topological manifold} $M$ is a topological space where every point $p \in M$ possesses an open neighborhood $U \subseteq M$ homeomorphic to an open subset of $\R^n$.
\end{definition}

Recall from Chapter~22 that a \textbf{homeomorphism} is a continuous bijection whose inverse is also continuous: a rubber-sheet equivalence that stretches and bends space freely without ever tearing or gluing. This means every point on an $n$-dimensional manifold sits inside a small neighborhood that is topologically identical to a flat patch of $\R^n$.
\begin{itemize}
\item A 1-dimensional manifold locally resembles a line (such as the circle $S^1$, the unit circle in the plane, or a smooth wire).
\item A 2-dimensional manifold locally resembles the plane (such as the sphere $S^2$, meaning the surface of a ball, not the solid ball; a torus, the surface of a doughnut; or a saddle-shaped surface).
\item A 4-dimensional manifold locally resembles $\R^4$ (such as spacetime in general relativity: three directions of space plus time).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Atlases and smooth coordinate charts}

Being homeomorphic to flat space is not enough to do calculus. A rubber-sheet homeomorphism allows sharp corners and crumpled bends where derivatives do not exist. To compute velocities and gradients, we need to differentiate. (For a function of several variables, a \emph{partial derivative} is the ordinary derivative with respect to one variable while the others are held constant, and the \emph{gradient} is the vector of all its partial derivatives.)

A single flat map cannot cover a sphere without pinching or tearing somewhere. A collection of overlapping flat maps, however, can cover the whole globe. In cartography, a bound book of regional flat maps that together describe the entire Earth is called an \textbf{atlas}. Differential geometry uses the same words:
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{coordinate chart} is a pair $(U, \phi)$, where $U \subseteq M$ is an open patch and $\phi: U \to \R^n$ is a homeomorphism onto an open subset of $\R^n$, assigning numerical coordinates $(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ to points in $U$.
\item An \textbf{atlas} is a collection of charts $\{(U_\alpha, \phi_\alpha)\}$ whose domains together cover all of $M$. (The index $\alpha$ is a name tag distinguishing one chart from another.)
\end{itemize}

Whenever two charts $(U_\alpha, \phi_\alpha)$ and $(U_\beta, \phi_\beta)$ overlap, any point in the intersection $U_\alpha \cap U_\beta$ gets two sets of coordinates, one from each chart. The function converting one set into the other is the \textbf{transition map}:
\[
\tau_{\alpha \beta} = \phi_\beta \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1} : \phi_\alpha(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta) \to \phi_\beta(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta).
\]
In this construction (illustrated in Figure~\ref{23:fig-transition-map}), $\phi_\alpha^{-1}$ lifts flat coordinates from $\R^n$ back onto the curved manifold patch, and $\phi_\beta$ maps that curved patch down into a different region of flat $\R^n$. So the transition map $\tau_{\alpha \beta}$ is an ordinary function from an open subset of $\R^n$ to $\R^n$, and ordinary calculus applies to it.

If all transition maps in an atlas are infinitely differentiable ($C^\infty$, meaning that partial derivatives of every order exist and are continuous, as for polynomials, $\sin$, and $e^x$), the atlas is called smooth, and $M$ is a \textbf{smooth manifold}. Now calculus on $M$ makes sense: we define a function $f: M \to \R$ to be smooth when its coordinate expressions $f \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1}: \phi_\alpha(U_\alpha) \to \R$ (that is, $f$ written as a function of the chart's coordinates) are smooth in the usual calculus sense. On any overlap, the expression of $f$ in the second chart is the expression in the first chart composed with a transition map:
\[
f \circ \phi_\beta^{-1} = (f \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1}) \circ \tau_{\beta\alpha}, \qquad \text{where } \tau_{\beta\alpha} = \phi_\alpha \circ \phi_\beta^{-1}.
\]
By the chain rule (a composition of smooth functions is smooth), it is then automatically smooth too, so two charts never disagree about whether $f$ is smooth.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} Can a single coordinate chart cover the entire circle $S^1$ without missing a point or tearing?

No. A single chart would be a homeomorphism $\phi$ from all of $S^1$ onto an open set $V \subseteq \R$. Two facts rule this out. First, $S^1$ is \emph{compact}: for subsets of $\R^n$ this means closed (it contains all its boundary points) and bounded. Continuous functions send compact sets to compact sets, but a non-empty open subset of $\R$ is either unbounded or misses its own endpoints, so it is never compact. Second, removing any single point from $S^1$ leaves one connected arc (a single piece), whereas removing a point from an interval splits it into two pieces, and a homeomorphism would have to preserve this difference (Figure~\ref{23:fig-circle-one-chart}). A closed loop cannot be laid out on an open line without a seam. To map the circle smoothly, we must use an atlas of at least two overlapping charts (Exercise~3 builds one; see Figure~\ref{23:fig-stereographic}).
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Tangent spaces: local linear approximations}

In single-variable calculus, the derivative of a function at a point gives the slope of the tangent line. On a curved surface $M$, what plays the role of the tangent line?

Imagine a particle traveling along a smooth curve $\gamma: (-\eps, \eps) \to M$ lying on the surface, with $\gamma(0) = p$; here $\gamma(t)$ is the particle's position at time $t$. At the instant $t = 0$, the particle has an instantaneous velocity vector $\gamma'(0)$ (in any chart, it is the ordinary derivative of the coordinates of $\gamma(t)$).

If you gather the velocity vectors of \emph{all} possible smooth curves passing through $p$, they fill out a flat \textbf{vector space}: a set of arrows that can be added to one another and multiplied by real numbers, following the same rules as arrows in the plane. This space is the \textbf{tangent space} $T_p M$.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $M$ is a 2D surface, $T_p M$ is a 2D plane touching the surface at $p$.
\item You can add tangent vectors and scale them, even though the curved surface itself has no addition of points.
\end{itemize}

If $f: M \to N$ is a smooth map between manifolds, its derivative at $p$, written $df_p$, is a \textbf{linear map} between tangent spaces, meaning it respects addition and scaling ($df_p(au + bv) = a\, df_p(u) + b\, df_p(v)$), so in coordinates it is a matrix:
\[
df_p : T_p M \to T_{f(p)} N, \qquad df_p\bigl(\gamma'(0)\bigr) = (f \circ \gamma)'(0).
\]
It sends the velocity vector of any curve $\gamma$ through $p$ to the velocity vector of its image curve $f \circ \gamma$ through $f(p)$, as Figure~\ref{23:fig-derivative-map} shows. Near each point, curved geometry is handled with linear algebra: matrices acting on vectors.

\subsection{The metric tensor: measuring lengths and angles}

A smooth manifold gives us coordinate charts and tangent spaces, but no way to measure. A vector in $T_p M$ specifies a direction, but we cannot yet measure its length, nor can we calculate the angle between two intersecting curves or the area of a surface region.

To measure, we attach to every tangent space an \textbf{inner product}: a rule that takes two vectors $u, v$, returns a number, and is linear in each input, generalizing the dot product $u \cdot v = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2$ of the plane. From an inner product $g_p$ come lengths and angles:
\[
|v| = \sqrt{g_p(v, v)}, \qquad \cos(\text{angle between } u \text{ and } v) = \frac{g_p(u, v)}{|u|\,|v|}.
\]
A choice of inner product at every point, varying smoothly from point to point, is called a \textbf{Riemannian metric}, after Bernhard Riemann. It is also called the \textbf{metric tensor}; ``tensor'' is the general name for such objects that take several vectors as input and are linear in each.
\begin{definition}
A \textbf{Riemannian metric} $g$ on a smooth manifold $M$ assigns to each point $p \in M$ an inner product
\[
g_p : T_p M \times T_p M \to \R
\]
that is symmetric ($g_p(u, v) = g_p(v, u)$), positive-definite ($g_p(v, v) > 0$ for all $v \neq 0$), and depends smoothly on $p$.
\end{definition}

In a local coordinate chart $(x^1, \dots, x^n)$ (by differential-geometric convention, coordinate indices are written as superscripts rather than subscripts---they are indices, not exponents), let $\partial/\partial x^i$ denote the tangent vector along the $i$-th coordinate direction: the velocity of the curve that increases $x^i$ at unit rate and keeps the other coordinates fixed. The metric at $x$ is represented by an $n \times n$ symmetric matrix of smooth functions
\[
g_{ij}(x) = g\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}, \frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\right), \qquad i, j = 1, \dots, n.
\]
The squared length $ds^2$ of a tiny step whose coordinates change by $dx^1, \dots, dx^n$ is summarized by the \textbf{line element}:
\[
ds^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n g_{ij}(x) \, dx^i \, dx^j.
\]
In the flat plane with ordinary coordinates, $g_{ij}$ is the identity matrix and this is Pythagoras' theorem: $ds^2 = (dx^1)^2 + (dx^2)^2$ (Figure~\ref{23:fig-line-element}, left).
Along a smooth curve $\gamma(t) = \bigl(x^1(t), \dots, x^n(t)\bigr)$ for $a \le t \le b$, the velocity has coordinate components $dx^i/dt$ and squared speed
\[
g_{\gamma(t)}\bigl(\gamma'(t), \gamma'(t)\bigr) = \sum_{i,j=1}^n g_{ij}\bigl(\gamma(t)\bigr) \, \frac{dx^i}{dt} \, \frac{dx^j}{dt}.
\]
Integrating the instantaneous speed adds up the lengths $ds$ of the tiny steps and gives the curve's arc length:
\[
L(\gamma) = \int_a^b \sqrt{g_{\gamma(t)}\bigl(\gamma'(t), \gamma'(t)\bigr)} \, dt.
\]

\begin{example}[The metric of a sphere]
Consider a sphere of radius $R$. In spherical coordinates $(\theta, \phi)$, where $\theta \in (0, \pi)$ is colatitude (the angle measured down from the North Pole) and $\phi \in [0, 2\pi)$ is longitude (here $\phi$ is an angle, not a chart map), the line element is:
\[
ds^2 = R^2 \, d\theta^2 + R^2 \sin^2\theta \, d\phi^2.
\]
Here the metric matrix is diagonal: $g_{\theta\theta} = R^2$, $g_{\phi\phi} = R^2 \sin^2\theta$, and $g_{\theta\phi} = 0$. Figure~\ref{23:fig-sphere-coords} shows the two angles and where the factor $\sin\theta$ comes from; Figure~\ref{23:fig-line-element} (right) shows the resulting small step.

The formula tells us two things about the sphere without referring to the surrounding three-dimensional space:
\begin{itemize}
\item Walking along a meridian (a north--south line of constant longitude, $d\phi = 0$), the distance element is $ds = R \, d\theta$. Every meridian behaves like a circle of radius $R$.
\item Walking along a parallel of latitude (an east--west circle of constant $\theta$, $d\theta = 0$), the distance element is $ds = R \sin\theta \, d\phi$, so the parallel is a circle of radius $R\sin\theta$. At the equator ($\theta = \pi/2$), $\sin\theta = 1$, giving a circumference of $2\pi R$. Toward the North Pole ($\theta \to 0$), $\sin\theta \to 0$ and the parallels shrink to a single point, where all the meridians meet.
\end{itemize}
The functions $g_{ij}$ determine all of the sphere's intrinsic geometry: lengths, angles, areas, and curvature.
\end{example}

\subsection{Geodesics, parallel transport, and curvature}

Once we can measure lengths, two natural questions arise: what is the straightest possible path between two points on a curved surface, and how does a directional vector change as you carry it along that path?

In flat Euclidean space, the shortest path between two points is a straight line; traced at constant speed, it has zero acceleration: $\gamma''(t) = 0$. On a curved manifold, the counterpart is a \textbf{geodesic}: a curve whose acceleration has no component along the surface (any acceleration points straight out of the surface, perpendicular to the tangent plane). A walker following a geodesic never turns left or right; the path bends only because the surface does. Short pieces of geodesics are the shortest paths between their endpoints. On a sphere, the geodesics are the \textbf{great circles}, the circles whose center is the center of the sphere, such as the equator and the meridians. Now imagine carrying an arrow (a tangent vector) while walking along a path. On flat paper, you keep the arrow pointing in the same direction by keeping its Cartesian components constant. On a curved surface, the tangent plane tilts at every step. \textbf{Parallel transport} is the rule for sliding a vector along a curve so that it never turns as seen from within the surface: the part of its rate of change that lies in the tangent plane is zero.

Consider the spherical triangle shown in Figure~\ref{23:fig-parallel-transport-sphere}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Start at the North Pole $N$ with an arrow $v_{\text{start}}$ pointing South along the prime meridian toward $A$.
\item Walk down to the equator at $A$. The meridian is a geodesic and the arrow points along it, so the arrow stays pointing South: $v_A$ points along the meridian past the equator.
\item Turn left $90^\circ$ and walk East along the equator to $B$. The arrow is perpendicular to your direction of travel. Parallel transport keeps it perpendicular to the equator at every step, so at $B$ it still points directly South ($v_B$).
\item Turn left $90^\circ$ and walk North along the meridian back to $N$. Your direction of motion is North, so the arrow pointing South points directly backward along your path.
\item When you arrive back at the North Pole $N$, the arrow $v_{\text{end}}$ points along the meridian $NB$, toward $B$: it is rotated by exactly $90^\circ$ ($\pi/2$ radians) from your starting vector $v_{\text{start}}$.
\end{enumerate}

The arrow was never turned along any segment, yet it returns rotated. This net angle of rotation around a closed loop is called \textbf{holonomy}.

Holonomy measures curvature. To say how, we need the \textbf{Gaussian curvature} $K$: a number attached to each point of a surface that measures how curved the surface is there. $K > 0$ where the surface bends the same way in every direction, like a sphere; $K = 0$ where it is flat or can be unrolled flat, like paper or a cylinder; $K < 0$ where it bends up in one direction and down in another, like a saddle. Gauss and Pierre Ossian Bonnet showed that for any \textbf{geodesic triangle} $T$ (three points joined by geodesic arcs) with interior angles $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3$ (measured in radians), the \textbf{angle excess} (angle sum minus $\pi$) equals the integral of $K$ over the triangle's interior. Here $dA$ is the area element, so the integral adds up curvature times area over the triangle:
\[
\sum_{i=1}^3 \alpha_i - \pi = \iint_T K \, dA.
\]
For our triangle on a sphere of radius $R$:
\begin{itemize}
\item All three angles are right angles: $\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \alpha_3 = \pi/2$. The angle excess is $3\pi/2 - \pi = \pi/2$.
\item The triangle covers one-eighth of the sphere, so its area is $\tfrac{1}{8}(4\pi R^2) = \tfrac{\pi}{2} R^2$.
\item The Gaussian curvature of a sphere of radius $R$ is $K = 1/R^2$ at every point.
\end{itemize}
Integrating the curvature yields:
\begin{align*}
\iint_T K \, dA &= \frac{1}{R^2} \cdot \operatorname{area}(T) && \text{($K$ is the same at every point)}\\
&= \frac{1}{R^2} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} R^2 = \frac{\pi}{2}.
\end{align*}

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} Suppose you walk around a small circle of radius $r$ on a flat parking lot, carrying an arrow that you never turn (you parallel-transport it). When you return to your starting point, does the arrow point the same way as when you started?

Yes. On flat ground, parallel-transporting a vector around any closed loop returns it with zero rotation. On a sphere of radius $R$, by contrast, the arrow comes back rotated by an angle equal to the enclosed area divided by $R^2$ (the integral of $K = 1/R^2$ over the enclosed region). As the circle shrinks toward zero radius ($r \to 0$), the enclosed area and the rotation both go to zero: a small enough piece of the sphere behaves like its flat tangent plane.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{historybox}
Before Gauss, curvature was generally treated extrinsically: a curve or surface was thought to bend only because it sat inside a higher-dimensional space, like a circle in the plane or a twisted ribbon in three-dimensional space. Gauss's 1827 \emph{Theorema Egregium} proved that curvature is intrinsic, computable entirely from measurements along the surface itself without reference to an external container.

On June 10, 1854, Gauss sat in the audience at the University of G\"ottingen to evaluate the \emph{Habilitation} lecture (the lecture required to qualify as a university teacher) of his former student, Bernhard Riemann. Riemann's lecture, titled \emph{\"Uber die Hypothesen, welche der Geometrie zu Grunde liegen} (``On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Bases of Geometry''), marked a turning point in geometry.

At the time, the philosophy of Immanuel Kant held that Euclidean three-dimensional space was a fixed framework built into human thought and known \emph{a priori}, that is, before any experience. Riemann challenged this view. He showed that an $n$-dimensional space (a \emph{manifold}) requires no external container or higher dimensions. It is defined intrinsically, equipped with a metric that measures distances between nearby points. In dimensions above two, a single number per point no longer describes the curvature; Riemann replaced it with a larger table of numbers, now called the Riemann curvature tensor. Riemann argued that whether physical space is flat or curved, three-dimensional or higher-dimensional, is an empirical question to be settled by physical experiment.

Six decades later, Albert Einstein---assisted by Marcel Grossmann and building on Hermann Minkowski's four-dimensional spacetime---found that Riemann's geometry was the mathematical language of general relativity. Spacetime is a four-dimensional manifold whose metric treats time with the opposite sign to space (a \emph{pseudo-Riemannian} metric, so $g_p(v, v)$ can be negative). Matter and energy determine the curvature of spacetime via Einstein's field equations, and that curvature determines how matter moves: gravitation is described as the intrinsic curvature of spacetime itself.

The geometry started by Gauss and Riemann remains an active area of research. In 1969, Roger Penrose formulated the \textbf{cosmic censorship conjecture}: are physical gravitational singularities (places where curvature becomes infinite) always hidden behind black hole event horizons (the boundary from inside which nothing, not even light, escapes), or can ``naked singularities'' be exposed to the outside universe? In topology, Grigori Perelman proved the 3-dimensional Poincar\'e conjecture in 2003 (roughly: a finite three-dimensional manifold without boundary on which every loop can be shrunk to a point is a three-dimensional sphere), using Richard Hamilton's Ricci flow, a rule that deforms a metric over time so that its curvature evens out, much as heat spreads through metal. The \textbf{smooth 4-dimensional Poincar\'e conjecture} remains open: can a four-dimensional manifold be homeomorphic to the 4-sphere while carrying a smooth atlas that is not equivalent to the usual one? Finally, uniting the smooth geometric spacetime of general relativity with quantum mechanics remains an unsolved challenge in theoretical physics.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Differential geometry---and singular spaces}

Differential geometry lets us write calculus and physics in a form that does not depend on an observer's choice of coordinates. Tools built on this foundation, called differential forms and tensor fields (objects attached to each point that take tangent vectors as input and return numbers), state physical laws in the same way in every coordinate system. The integral theorems of vector calculus, such as Stokes' theorem and the divergence theorem, which relate an integral over a region to an integral over its boundary, carry over from $\R^n$ to smooth manifolds, where they govern fluid flow, electromagnetism, and gravity.

That machinery, however, depends on smoothness everywhere. Every small patch must look smoothly like flat Euclidean space, with differentiable transition maps on overlaps. As soon as a shape develops a sharp pinch or crosses itself---like the curve $y^2 = x^3 + x^2$, which crosses itself at the origin (Figure~\ref{23:fig-nodal-cubic})---there is no single flat tangent space to linearize around, and smooth manifold calculus breaks down at that point (a \emph{singular point}). Derivatives also do not answer counting questions, such as how many times to count a meeting point where two curves touch without crossing.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Geodesics on a cylinder.} (Mental check)
An ant crawls on the surface of an infinite cylinder $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ in $\R^3$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If you cut the cylinder along the vertical line $x = 1$, $y = 0$ and unroll it flat onto a desk, straight lines on the flat paper correspond to which curves on the cylinder?
\item An ant wants to walk from $(1, 0, 0)$ to $(1, 0, 4\pi)$ along a geodesic path. How many complete loops around the cylinder might such a geodesic make, and how does its total length depend on the number of turns $k \in \Z$?
\item Explain why the Gaussian curvature of the cylinder is $K = 0$ everywhere, even though the cylinder appears bent to an outside observer in $\R^3$.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Spherical excess and area.} (Routine practice)
On the unit sphere $S^2$ (radius $R = 1$, Gaussian curvature $K = 1$), a surveyor measures a geodesic triangle whose interior angles are $\alpha = 75^\circ$, $\beta = 85^\circ$, and $\gamma = 80^\circ$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Convert these angles to radians and compute the spherical excess $E = \alpha + \beta + \gamma - \pi$.
\item Using the Gauss-Bonnet formula $E = \iint_T K \, dA$, find the exact surface area of the triangle.
\item What fraction of the sphere's total surface area ($4\pi$) does this triangle cover?
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{The circle atlas.} (Routine practice)
Consider the unit circle $S^1 = \{(x, y) \in \R^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$. Let $N = (0, 1)$ be the North pole and $S = (0, -1)$ be the South pole. \emph{Stereographic projection} from $N$ sends a point $(x, y) \neq N$ of the circle to the place where the straight line from $N$ through $(x, y)$ crosses the $x$-axis (Figure~\ref{23:fig-stereographic}). Stereographic projection from $N$ and from $S$ give the two chart maps
\begin{align*}
\phi_1 &: S^1 \setminus \{N\} \to \R, & \phi_1(x, y) &= u = \frac{x}{1 - y},\\
\phi_2 &: S^1 \setminus \{S\} \to \R, & \phi_2(x, y) &= v = \frac{x}{1 + y}.
\end{align*}

\item Conclude that this two-chart atlas gives $S^1$ the structure of a 1-dimensional smooth manifold.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{The metric tensor and lengths on the sphere.} (Computation)
In spherical coordinates $(\theta, \phi)$ with colatitude $\theta \in (0, \pi)$ and longitude $\phi \in [0, 2\pi)$, the line element on a sphere of radius $R$ is:
\[
ds^2 = R^2 \, d\theta^2 + R^2 \sin^2\theta \, d\phi^2.
\]
\begin{enumerate}
\item Write the matrix of metric coefficients $[g_{ij}]$ in the $(\theta, \phi)$ coordinate basis.
\item Consider a path along a line of constant latitude $\theta = \theta_0$: $\gamma(t) = (\theta_0, t)$ for $t \in [0, 2\pi]$. Compute the velocity vector $\gamma'(t)$ and the speed $\sqrt{g(\gamma'(t), \gamma'(t))}$.
\item Integrate the speed from $t = 0$ to $2\pi$ to find the circumference of the latitude circle as a function of $\theta_0$. For which value of $\theta_0$ is the circumference maximized, and what is its value?
\end{enumerate}
