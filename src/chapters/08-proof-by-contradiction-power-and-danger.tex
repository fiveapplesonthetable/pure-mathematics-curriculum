\section{Proof by Contradiction: Power and Danger}
\chapterline

In Chapter~7, we saw two dependable strategies for proving a conditional promise $P \implies Q$. When the hypothesis $P$ provides explicit algebraic formulas, you deduce $Q$ directly. When the conclusion is simpler to negate, you prove the contrapositive ($\neg Q \implies \neg P$, showing that the failure of the conclusion forces the hypothesis to fail as well).

But what do you do when a mathematical statement does not provide an explicit hypothesis to unpack?

Consider two celebrated assertions in the history of mathematics:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
\item There are infinitely many prime numbers.
\end{itemize}
Neither statement is framed as an ``if-then'' promise. There is no initial hypothesis to unpack. Instead, each asserts that something cannot occur: the first asserts that no two integers $a$ and $b$ can satisfy $(a/b)^2 = 2$; the second asserts that the sequence of prime numbers never terminates. We cannot verify such claims by checking cases one by one, because there are infinitely many possibilities to consider.

This is where the third strategy from Chapter~7 applies: \textbf{proof by contradiction}. When direct and contrapositive proofs offer no direct route, contradiction allows us to assume the opposite of what we want to prove and reason forward until reaching a logical impossibility.

The English mathematician G.~H.~Hardy described this maneuver in \emph{A Mathematician's Apology}:
\begin{quote}
``Reductio ad absurdum, which Euclid loved so much, is one of a mathematician's finest weapons. It is a far finer gambit than any chess gambit: a chess player may offer the sacrifice of a pawn or even a piece, but a mathematician offers the game.''
\end{quote}
The phrase \emph{reductio ad absurdum} is Latin for ``reduction to absurdity.'' The strategy begins by supposing the claim is false, then reasoning lawfully from that assumption until reaching an impossibility.

Because it starts by assuming something false, contradiction must be used carefully. A proof by contradiction succeeds only when two mutually exclusive mathematical facts collide: $A \land \neg A$ (a statement and its negation cannot both be true).

\begin{historybox}
Around 500~BCE, the followers of Pythagoras lived by a mystical doctrine: ``All is Number.'' To the Pythagoreans, every relationship in nature, music, and geometry could be expressed as a ratio of whole numbers. When studying a right triangle with two perpendicular legs of length $1$, they sought two whole numbers $a$ and $b$ whose ratio $a/b$ measured the hypotenuse (the longest side of a right triangle, opposite the right angle).

According to ancient legend, a Pythagorean named Hippasus of Metapontum realized that assuming such a fraction exists leads to an inescapable trap: the numerator $a$ must be even, which forces the denominator $b$ to be even, destroying the requirement that the fraction was reduced to lowest terms (sharing no common integer factor larger than $1$). The diagonal was incommensurable with the side---meaning no single common unit of measurement, no matter how microscopic, could divide evenly into both the side and the diagonal. Legend has it that the brotherhood was so unsettled by this scandal that they drowned Hippasus at sea to bury the secret. Yet two centuries later, Euclid of Alexandria (c.~300~BCE) recorded this exact parity contradiction in Book~X of the \emph{Elements}, alongside his proof that prime numbers never run out.

In the 1920s, a fierce controversy erupted over whether contradiction proofs are legitimate for infinite mathematical objects. The Dutch mathematician L.~E.~J.~Brouwer argued that proving an object exists merely by showing that its non-existence causes an absurdity is non-constructive: it proves that non-existence is impossible, but provides no recipe, coordinates, or algorithm to actually produce the object. Brouwer rejected the classical Law of Excluded Middle (the principle that every mathematical assertion is either true or false, with no third possibility) for infinite sets. The German mathematician David Hilbert defended contradiction passionately, retorting that taking the Law of Excluded Middle away from mathematicians would be like forbidding a boxer from using his fists.

In modern computer science, constructive proofs are prized because algorithms require tangible witnesses. Yet proof by contradiction remains a primary tool for establishing impossibility results: proving that no fraction can equal $\sqrt{2}$, and no finite list contains all primes.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Visualizing proof by contradiction}

To prove a statement $P$ by contradiction, we temporarily assume its negation, $\neg P$. From this temporary assumption, we deduce consequences using standard rules of logic and arithmetic. If these lawful deductions lead to a contradiction---a statement that is simultaneously asserted to be true and false, such as $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ and $\gcd(a, b) \ge 2$---the assumption $\neg P$ cannot hold. Since an assertion must be either true or false, we conclude that $P$ must be true (Figure~\ref{08:fig-split-reality}).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$}{The irrationality of sqrt(2)}}

The classical proof of the irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ is preserved in Book X of Euclid's \emph{Elements}. It was one of the great intellectual shocks of antiquity: geometry produced lengths (such as the diagonal of a unit square) that arithmetic, if restricted to ratios of integers, could not measure.

\begin{theorem}
$\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that $\sqrt{2}$ is rational. 

By definition of rational numbers, we may express $\sqrt{2}$ as a ratio of integers:
\[
\sqrt{2} = \frac{a}{b}, \qquad \text{where } a, b \in \Z \text{ and } b \neq 0.
\]
We can choose this fraction in \textbf{lowest terms}, meaning that $a$ and $b$ share no common factors other than $1$ (that is, their greatest common divisor $\gcd(a, b) = 1$). In particular, $a$ and $b$ cannot both be even numbers.

Now we perform lawful algebraic operations:
\begin{align*}
\sqrt{2} &= \frac{a}{b} \\
2 &= \frac{a^2}{b^2} \qquad \text{(squaring both sides)} \\
a^2 &= 2b^2 \qquad \text{(multiplying by } b^2\text{)}.
\end{align*}
Since $b$ is an integer, $b^2$ is an integer. Thus $a^2$ is an integer multiple of $2$, which means $a^2$ is even.

We proved in Chapter~7 that for any integer $a$, if $a^2$ is even, then $a$ must be even. (Recall that Chapter~7 proved this by contrapositive: if $a$ were odd, say $a = 2m+1$, then $a^2 = (2m+1)^2 = 4m^2 + 4m + 1 = 2(2m^2+2m)+1$ would be odd as well.) Therefore:
\[
a = 2k \quad \text{for some integer } k.
\]

Substitute this expression for $a$ back into our equation $a^2 = 2b^2$:
\begin{align*}
(2k)^2 &= 2b^2 \\
4k^2 &= 2b^2 \\
2k^2 &= b^2 \qquad \text{(dividing both sides by } 2\text{)}.
\end{align*}
Because $k$ is an integer, $k^2$ is an integer. This equation shows that $b^2$ is an integer multiple of $2$, meaning $b^2$ is even. Again invoking our theorem, since $b^2$ is even, $b$ must be even!

Now look at what has happened:
\begin{itemize}
\item We proved that $a$ is even ($2 \mid a$, where the vertical bar denotes divisibility: $2 \mid a$ means $2$ divides $a$ evenly, with zero remainder).
\item We proved that $b$ is even ($2 \mid b$).
\end{itemize}
This means $2$ is a common divisor of $a$ and $b$. But this directly contradicts our initial specification that $a/b$ was chosen in lowest terms ($\gcd(a, b) = 1$).

Both statements cannot be true simultaneously in mathematics. Because every deductive step followed lawful arithmetic rules, the initial assumption that $\sqrt{2}$ can be written as a ratio of integers must be false. 

Therefore, $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
\end{proof}

Notice the structure of the argument: assuming rationality forces $a$ to be even, which in turn forces $b$ to be even, contradicting the assumption that the fraction was in lowest terms.

Another perspective on this contradiction comes from the principle of \textbf{infinite descent}. If $\sqrt{2} = a/b$, then setting $a = 2k$ gave $b^2 = 2k^2$, which can be rearranged as:
\[
\frac{b}{k} = \sqrt{2}.
\]
Notice what just happened: we began with a fraction $a/b = \sqrt{2}$, and from it we produced a strictly smaller fraction $b/k = \sqrt{2}$ with $0 < k < b < a$. If $\sqrt{2}$ were truly rational, you could repeat this division step indefinitely, generating an infinite strictly decreasing chain of positive integers:
\[
a > b > k > k_1 > k_2 > \cdots > 0.
\]
Yet positive integers cannot decrease indefinitely below $1$. In mathematics, the fact that every non-empty set of positive integers contains a smallest element is called the \emph{well-ordering principle} of $\N$. Because an infinite descent of positive integers is impossible, the initial fraction $a/b$ could never exist in the first place.

\itshape \textbf{Pause and think:} What happens if you try to use this exact argument to prove that $\sqrt{4}$ is irrational? You assume $\sqrt{4} = a/b$ in lowest terms, so $a^2 = 4b^2$. This means $a^2$ is a multiple of $4$, which implies $a$ is even: $a = 2k$. Substitute this back in: $(2k)^2 = 4b^2 \implies 4k^2 = 4b^2 \implies k^2 = b^2$. Does this equation force $b$ to be even? Not at all! Here $k = b$, which can easily be odd (for example, taking $a = 2$ and $b = 1$ gives $k = 1$, which is odd, yielding the completely valid rational fraction $2/1$). The contradiction does not arise because $4$ is already a square, so the factor of $2$ cancels completely.
\end{center}

\subsection{The infinitude of primes}

Another milestone of proof by contradiction is Euclid's demonstration that prime numbers never run out.

A \textbf{prime number} is an integer $p \ge 2$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and itself. Primes are the multiplicative building blocks of arithmetic: every integer strictly greater than $1$ is either prime itself or can be factored into a product of primes. 

As we move further along the number line, primes become less frequent, and gaps between consecutive primes can be arbitrarily large. This raises a natural question: does the sequence of primes eventually terminate?

\begin{theorem}[Euclid]\label{08:thm-primes}
There are infinitely many prime numbers.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that the set of all prime numbers is finite. 

Because the set is finite, we can write down all prime numbers in a complete, terminating list:
\[
\{p_1, p_2, p_3, \ldots, p_n\}.
\]
Here $p_1 = 2$, $p_2 = 3$, $p_3 = 5$, continuing up to the supposed final prime $p_n$.

Now construct the integer $N$ obtained by multiplying all $n$ of these primes together and adding $1$:
\[
N = (p_1 \cdot p_2 \cdot p_3 \cdots p_n) + 1.
\]
Because $p_1 = 2$, the product $P = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n \ge 2$, which means $N \ge 3$. Every integer strictly greater than $1$ possesses at least one prime divisor. Therefore, $N$ must be divisible by some prime number $q$.

Could this prime divisor $q$ be one of the primes on our complete list?
\begin{itemize}
\item If $q = p_i$ for some index $i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$, then $q$ divides the product $P = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n$. That is, $P = q \cdot v$ for some integer $v$.
\item But $q$ also divides $N = P + 1$, meaning $N = q \cdot u$ for some integer $u$.
\item If an integer divides two numbers, it must divide their difference:
\[
N - P = q \cdot u - q \cdot v = q(u - v).
\]
Because $N - P = 1$, we obtain $1 = q(u - v)$, which means $q \mid 1$.
\end{itemize}
The only positive divisor of $1$ is $1$ itself. But by definition, every prime number is strictly greater than $1$, so $q \ge 2$. A number greater than or equal to $2$ cannot divide $1$.

This is an impossible deduction. The assumption that the finite list $\{p_1, \ldots, p_n\}$ contained all prime numbers has led to a contradiction. Therefore, the set of prime numbers is infinite.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The common trap: phantom contradictions}

Beginners sometimes reach for a proof by contradiction when a clean direct proof is sitting in plain sight:
\begin{quote}
\itshape ``Theorem: If $n$ is even, then $n+1$ is odd.\\
Attempted contradiction: Suppose $n$ is even and $n+1$ is even. Then $n = 2k$ and $n+1 = 2m$. Subtracting gives $1 = 2(m-k)$, which means $1$ is even. Contradiction!''
\end{quote}
While logically valid, this argument adds unnecessary complexity. From $n = 2k$, we immediately have $n+1 = 2k+1$, which satisfies the definition of an odd number directly. The assumption that $n+1$ is even was never used to deduce any property of $n$; it served only to provide an artificial contradiction at the final line.

To keep your proofs lean and transparent, distinguish carefully between the three main strategies:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Direct proof ($P \implies Q$)}: Assume $P$ is true. Unpack definitions, calculate, and deduce $Q$.
\item \textbf{Contrapositive proof ($\neg Q \implies \neg P$)}: Assume only that $Q$ is false. Calculate until you deduce that $P$ must be false. You never assume $P$ at the start.
\item \textbf{Contradiction ($P \land \neg Q \implies A \land \neg A$)}: Assume both $P$ and $\neg Q$ simultaneously. Deduce consequences until reaching a contradiction ($A \land \neg A$).
\end{itemize}

Whenever you find yourself writing a proof by contradiction, apply the \textbf{Phantom Test}:
\begin{center}
\itshape ``Did the assumption $\neg Q$ provide necessary information, or did the argument simply prove $Q$ directly and attach a contradiction at the end?''
\end{center}
If the latter, discard the contradiction setup. You have already written a direct proof.

Always state the exact contradiction in your final sentence. Never end a proof with ``\ldots which is impossible, so the theorem is proved.'' State plainly: ``This contradicts the fact that $q \ge 2$'' or ``This contradicts our initial assumption that $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.''
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{What contradiction buys us---and why we need counterexamples}

Proof by contradiction is especially effective for \textbf{proving impossibility}. A direct calculation works with objects that exist; contradiction shows that a hypothesized object cannot exist because its existence would lead to an impossibility ($A \land \neg A$). This is why contradiction is the natural tool for showing that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational and that primes never end.

Yet proof by contradiction has a limitation: \textbf{it does not tell you if a conjecture is false}. If you attempt to prove a claim by contradiction and find no contradiction, you cannot tell whether the proof stalled because you missed a step or because the claim is false.

To test claims before trying to prove them, mathematicians search for concrete counterexamples. In Chapter~9 (\emph{Examples, Non-Examples, and Counterexamples}), we explore how to test statements systematically against examples, edge cases, and potential counterexamples.

\subsection{Exercises}

\paragraph{Warm-up and mental checks.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Spotting the phantom.} Consider the following attempted proof:
\begin{quote}
\itshape ``Claim: If $x > 3$, then $2x + 1 > 7$.\\
Proof: Suppose, for contradiction, that $x > 3$ and $2x + 1 \le 7$. Since $x > 3$, multiplying by $2$ yields $2x > 6$, and adding $1$ gives $2x + 1 > 7$. But this contradicts $2x + 1 \le 7$. Thus the claim is proved.''
\end{quote}
Explain why this is a phantom contradiction. Rewrite the argument as a lean, direct two-sentence proof.

\item \textbf{Parity breakdown on perfect squares.} If you apply the $\sqrt{2}$ contradiction proof to $\sqrt{9}$, you write $\sqrt{9} = a/b$ in lowest terms, so $a^2 = 9b^2$. Then $a^2$ is a multiple of $3$, so $a = 3k$. Show the next step of algebraic substitution, and identify the exact moment where the proof fails to force $b$ to share a factor of $3$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Core practice.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item Prove that $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational. (You may use without proof the lemma: for any integer $n$, if $3 \mid n^2$, then $3 \mid n$.)
\item Prove that the sum of a rational number and an irrational number is always irrational.
\item Prove by contradiction that there is no smallest positive rational number. (Hint: If $r \in \Q$ with $r > 0$ were the smallest, what can you say about $r/2$?)
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Deeper exploration.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item Prove that $\log_{10}(2)$ is irrational. (Hint: Assume $\log_{10}(2) = a/b$ with positive integers $a, b$, rewrite in exponential form $10^a = 2^b$, and invoke the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic on the prime factorizations.)
\item Re-examine the Euclid number $N = (2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13) + 1 = 30031 = 59 \times 509$. Using the stepping-stone geometry of Figure~\ref{08:fig-prime-strides}, explain why neither $59$ nor $509$ could possibly have been any of the first six primes, without performing any manual division by $2, 3, 5, 7, 11,$ or $13$.
\item Let $p$ be any prime number. Using Euclid's lemma (if a prime $p$ divides a product $ab$, then $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$), prove that $\sqrt{p}$ is irrational.
\end{enumerate}
