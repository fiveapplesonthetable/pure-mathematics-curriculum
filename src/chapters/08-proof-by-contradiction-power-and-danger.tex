\section{Proof by Contradiction: Power and Danger}
\chapterline

In Chapter~7, we saw two dependable strategies for proving a conditional promise $P \implies Q$. When the hypothesis $P$ provides explicit algebraic formulas, you deduce $Q$ directly. When the conclusion is simpler to negate, you prove the contrapositive ($\neg Q \implies \neg P$, showing that the failure of the conclusion forces the hypothesis to fail as well).

But what do you do when a mathematical statement does not provide an explicit hypothesis to unpack?

Consider two famous claims from the history of mathematics:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
\item There are infinitely many prime numbers.
\end{itemize}
Neither statement is framed as an ``if-then'' promise. There is no initial hypothesis to unpack. Instead, each asserts that something cannot occur: the first asserts that no two integers $a$ and $b$ can satisfy $(a/b)^2 = 2$; the second asserts that the sequence of prime numbers never terminates. We cannot verify such claims by checking cases one by one, because there are infinitely many possibilities to consider.

For claims like these we use the third strategy from Chapter~7: \textbf{proof by contradiction}. We assume the opposite of what we want to prove and reason forward until we reach a logical impossibility.

The English mathematician G.~H.~Hardy described this maneuver in \emph{A Mathematician's Apology}:
\begin{quote}
``Reductio ad absurdum, which Euclid loved so much, is one of a mathematician's finest weapons. It is a far finer gambit than any chess gambit: a chess player may offer the sacrifice of a pawn or even a piece, but a mathematician offers the game.''
\end{quote}
The phrase \emph{reductio ad absurdum} is Latin for ``reduction to absurdity,'' the old name for proof by contradiction. A \emph{gambit} is a chess opening in which a player gives up material (usually a pawn) on purpose to gain a better position. Hardy's point: in a proof by contradiction you hand your opponent the whole claim (you assume it is false) and still win.

Because it starts by assuming something we expect to be false, contradiction must be used carefully. A proof by contradiction succeeds only when it produces some statement $A$ together with its negation: $A \land \neg A$, read ``$A$ and not $A$.'' A statement and its negation cannot both be true, so reaching $A \land \neg A$ shows the starting assumption was wrong.

\begin{historybox}
Around 500~BCE, the followers of Pythagoras held a religious belief: ``All is Number.'' To the Pythagoreans, every relationship in nature, music, and geometry could be expressed as a ratio of whole numbers. When studying a right triangle with two perpendicular legs of length $1$, they sought two whole numbers $a$ and $b$ whose ratio $a/b$ measured the hypotenuse (the longest side of a right triangle, opposite the right angle).

According to ancient legend, a Pythagorean named Hippasus of Metapontum realized that assuming such a fraction exists leads to an inescapable trap: the numerator $a$ must be even, which forces the denominator $b$ to be even, destroying the requirement that the fraction was reduced to lowest terms (sharing no common integer factor larger than $1$). The diagonal was incommensurable with the side---meaning no single common unit of measurement, no matter how small, could divide evenly into both the side and the diagonal. Legend has it that the brotherhood was so unsettled that they drowned Hippasus at sea to bury the secret. Two centuries later, Euclid of Alexandria (c.~300~BCE) wrote down this argument, which turns on \emph{parity} (whether a number is even or odd), in Book~X of the \emph{Elements}; his proof that prime numbers never run out appears in Book~IX.

In the 1920s, a controversy arose over whether contradiction proofs are legitimate for infinite mathematical objects. This is the dispute between L.~E.~J.~Brouwer and David Hilbert described in Chapter~5. For proof by contradiction, Brouwer's objection was that showing an object's non-existence leads to an absurdity proves only that non-existence is impossible; it gives no recipe or algorithm to produce the object.

In computer science, constructive proofs (proofs that build the object they claim exists) are valued because such a proof can be turned into a program that produces the object. Proof by contradiction remains the main tool for \emph{impossibility results}, proofs that something cannot exist: no fraction equals $\sqrt{2}$, and no finite list contains all primes.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Visualizing proof by contradiction}

To prove a statement $P$ by contradiction, we temporarily assume its negation, $\neg P$. From this temporary assumption, we deduce consequences using the ordinary rules of logic and arithmetic. If these valid deductions lead to a contradiction---two statements that cannot both be true, such as $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ and $\gcd(a, b) \ge 2$---the assumption $\neg P$ cannot hold. Since a statement must be either true or false (the Law of Excluded Middle), we conclude that $P$ is true. Figure~\ref{08:fig-split-reality} shows this structure, using the proof about $\sqrt{2}$ from the next section as the example.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$}{The irrationality of sqrt(2)}}

The classical proof of the irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ is preserved in Book X of Euclid's \emph{Elements}. It was a great shock in ancient Greece: geometry produced lengths (such as the diagonal of a square with side $1$) that no ratio of whole numbers could measure. Figure~\ref{08:fig-unit-square} shows where $\sqrt{2}$ comes from: by the Pythagorean theorem, the diagonal $d$ of a square with side $1$ satisfies
\[
d^2 = 1^2 + 1^2 = 2, \qquad\text{so}\qquad d = \sqrt{2}.
\]
Saying $\sqrt{2}$ is rational would mean that some small unit of length fits a whole number of times ($b$ times) along the side and a whole number of times ($a$ times) along the diagonal.

\begin{theorem}
$\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that $\sqrt{2}$ is rational. 

By definition of rational numbers (and since $\sqrt{2} > 0$), we may express $\sqrt{2}$ as a ratio of positive integers:
\[
\sqrt{2} = \frac{a}{b}, \qquad \text{where } a, b \in \N.
\]
We can choose this fraction in \textbf{lowest terms} (if $a$ and $b$ share a factor, divide it out of both), meaning that $a$ and $b$ share no common factors other than $1$ (that is, their greatest common divisor $\gcd(a, b) = 1$). In particular, $a$ and $b$ cannot both be even numbers.

Square both sides and clear the denominator:
\begin{align*}
\sqrt{2} &= \frac{a}{b} \\
2 &= \frac{a^2}{b^2} && \text{(squaring both sides)} \\
a^2 &= 2b^2 && \text{(multiplying both sides by } b^2\text{)}.
\end{align*}
Since $b$ is an integer, $b^2$ is an integer. Thus $a^2$ is an integer multiple of $2$, which means $a^2$ is even.

We proved in Chapter~7 that for any integer $a$, if $a^2$ is even, then $a$ must be even. (Recall that Chapter~7 proved this by contrapositive: if $a$ were odd, say $a = 2m+1$, then
\[
a^2 = (2m+1)^2 = 4m^2 + 4m + 1 = 2(2m^2+2m) + 1
\]
would be odd as well.) Therefore
\[
a = 2k \quad \text{for some integer } k.
\]

Substitute this expression for $a$ back into our equation $a^2 = 2b^2$:
\begin{align*}
(2k)^2 &= 2b^2 \\
4k^2 &= 2b^2 && \text{(expanding the square)} \\
2k^2 &= b^2 && \text{(dividing both sides by } 2\text{)}.
\end{align*}
Because $k$ is an integer, $k^2$ is an integer. This equation shows that $b^2$ is an integer multiple of $2$, meaning $b^2$ is even. Again invoking our theorem, since $b^2$ is even, $b$ must be even.

Now look at what has happened:
\begin{itemize}
\item We proved that $a$ is even ($2 \mid a$, where the vertical bar denotes divisibility: $2 \mid a$ means $2$ divides $a$ evenly, with zero remainder).
\item We proved that $b$ is even ($2 \mid b$).
\end{itemize}
This means $2$ is a common divisor of $a$ and $b$. This contradicts our choice of $a/b$ in lowest terms ($\gcd(a, b) = 1$).

Both statements cannot be true. Because every step followed valid rules of arithmetic, the initial assumption that $\sqrt{2}$ can be written as a ratio of integers must be false. 

Therefore, $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
\end{proof}

In short: assuming rationality forces $a$ to be even, which in turn forces $b$ to be even, contradicting the assumption that the fraction was in lowest terms.

Another view of this contradiction comes from the principle of \textbf{infinite descent}: if an assumption lets you produce a smaller positive integer from any positive integer, and so on forever, the assumption is false. If $\sqrt{2} = a/b$, then setting $a = 2k$ gave $b^2 = 2k^2$, which can be rearranged as
\[
\frac{b^2}{k^2} = 2, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{b}{k} = \sqrt{2}.
\]
We began with a fraction $a/b = \sqrt{2}$, and from it we produced another fraction $b/k = \sqrt{2}$ whose numerator and denominator are strictly smaller positive integers. To see this, use $1 < \sqrt{2} < 2$:
\begin{align*}
1 < \frac{a}{b} < 2 \quad &\Longrightarrow \quad b < a < 2b && \text{(multiplying by } b\text{)} \\
&\Longrightarrow \quad 0 < k = \frac{a}{2} < b < a && \text{(since } a < 2b \text{ gives } a/2 < b\text{)}.
\end{align*}
If $\sqrt{2}$ were rational, you could repeat this step forever (writing $k_1, k_2, \ldots$ for the new denominators), generating an infinite strictly decreasing chain of positive integers:
\[
a > b > k > k_1 > k_2 > \cdots > 0.
\]
Positive integers cannot keep decreasing forever: after at most $a$ steps you would pass below $1$. In mathematics, the fact that every non-empty set of positive integers contains a smallest element is called the \emph{well-ordering principle} of $\N$. Because an infinite descent of positive integers is impossible, the initial fraction $a/b$ cannot exist.

\begin{center}
\itshape \textbf{Pause and think:} What happens if you try to use this exact argument to prove that $\sqrt{4}$ is irrational? You assume $\sqrt{4} = a/b$ in lowest terms, so $a^2 = 4b^2$. This means $a^2$ is a multiple of $4$, which implies $a$ is even: $a = 2k$. Substitute this back in:
\[
(2k)^2 = 4b^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; 4k^2 = 4b^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; k^2 = b^2.
\]
Does this equation force $b$ to be even? No. Here $k = b$, which can be odd (for example, taking $a = 2$ and $b = 1$ gives $k = 1$, which is odd, and $2/1$ is a valid fraction for $\sqrt{4}$). The contradiction does not arise because $4$ is already a square, so the factor of $2$ cancels completely.
\end{center}

\subsection{The infinitude of primes}

The second classic proof by contradiction is Euclid's proof that prime numbers never run out.

A \textbf{prime number} is an integer $p \ge 2$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and itself. Primes are the multiplicative building blocks of arithmetic: every integer greater than $1$ is either prime itself or can be factored into a product of primes. 

As we move further along the number line, primes become less frequent, and gaps between consecutive primes can be as large as you like. Figure~\ref{08:fig-prime-grid} shows the thinning: there are $25$ primes among $1$ to $100$, four of them in the first row of ten ($1$ to $10$) but only one, $97$, in the last row ($91$ to $100$). So does the sequence of primes eventually stop?

\begin{theorem}[Euclid]\label{08:thm-primes}
There are infinitely many prime numbers.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that the set of all prime numbers is finite. 

Because the set is finite, we can write down all prime numbers in a complete, terminating list:
\[
\{p_1, p_2, p_3, \ldots, p_n\}.
\]
Here $p_1 = 2$, $p_2 = 3$, $p_3 = 5$, continuing up to the supposed final prime $p_n$.

Now construct the integer $N$ obtained by multiplying all $n$ of these primes together and adding $1$. Writing $M = p_1 \cdot p_2 \cdot p_3 \cdots p_n$ for the product,
\[
N = M + 1 = (p_1 \cdot p_2 \cdot p_3 \cdots p_n) + 1.
\]
Because $p_1 = 2$, the product $M \ge 2$, which means $N \ge 3$. Every integer greater than $1$ has at least one prime divisor. Therefore, $N$ is divisible by some prime number $q$.

Could this prime divisor $q$ be one of the primes on our complete list?
\begin{itemize}
\item If $q = p_i$ for some index $i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$, then $q$ divides the product $M = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n$. That is, $M = q \cdot v$ for some integer $v$.
\item But $q$ also divides $N = M + 1$, meaning $N = q \cdot u$ for some integer $u$.
\item If an integer divides two numbers, it must divide their difference:
\begin{align*}
1 = N - M &= q \cdot u - q \cdot v \\
&= q \cdot (u - v).
\end{align*}
Since $u - v$ is an integer, this means $q \mid 1$.
\end{itemize}
The only positive divisor of $1$ is $1$ itself. But by definition, every prime number is greater than $1$, so $q \ge 2$, and a number greater than or equal to $2$ cannot divide $1$.

So $q$ is a prime that is not on the list, which contradicts the assumption that the finite list $\{p_1, \ldots, p_n\}$ contained all prime numbers. Therefore, the set of prime numbers is infinite.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The common trap: phantom contradictions}

Beginners sometimes reach for a proof by contradiction when a clean direct proof is sitting in plain sight:
\begin{quote}
\itshape ``Theorem: If $n$ is even, then $n+1$ is odd.\\
Attempted contradiction: Suppose $n$ is even and $n+1$ is even. Then $n = 2k$ and $n+1 = 2m$. Subtracting gives $1 = 2(m-k)$, which means $1$ is even, a contradiction.''
\end{quote}
This argument is logically valid, but it adds steps that do nothing. From $n = 2k$, we immediately have $n+1 = 2k+1$, which satisfies the definition of an odd number. The assumption that $n+1$ is even was never used to deduce any property of $n$; it served only to provide an artificial contradiction at the final line. We call this a \textbf{phantom contradiction}: the proof looks like a contradiction proof, but the contradiction does no work.

To keep your proofs short and easy to check, distinguish carefully between the three main strategies:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Direct proof ($P \implies Q$)}: Assume $P$ is true. Unpack definitions, calculate, and deduce $Q$.
\item \textbf{Contrapositive proof ($\neg Q \implies \neg P$)}: Assume only that $Q$ is false. Calculate until you deduce that $P$ must be false. You never assume $P$ at the start.
\item \textbf{Contradiction ($P \land \neg Q \implies A \land \neg A$)}: Assume both $P$ and $\neg Q$ at once. Deduce consequences until reaching a contradiction ($A \land \neg A$).
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{08:fig-three-strategies} puts the three side by side: what each one lets you assume at the start, and where each one has to arrive.

Whenever you find yourself writing a proof by contradiction, apply the \textbf{Phantom Test}:
\begin{center}
\itshape ``Did the assumption $\neg Q$ provide necessary information, or did the argument prove $Q$ directly and attach a contradiction at the end?''
\end{center}
If the latter, discard the contradiction setup. You have already written a direct proof.

\begin{trapbox}
Always state the exact contradiction in your final sentence. Never end a proof with ``\ldots which is impossible, so the theorem is proved.'' State plainly: ``This contradicts the fact that $q \ge 2$'' or ``This contradicts our initial assumption that $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.''
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{What contradiction buys us---and why we need counterexamples}

Proof by contradiction is especially effective for \textbf{proving impossibility}. A direct calculation works with objects that exist; contradiction shows that a hypothesized object cannot exist because its existence would lead to an impossibility ($A \land \neg A$). This is why contradiction is the natural tool for showing that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational and that primes never end.

Proof by contradiction has a limitation: \textbf{it does not tell you if a conjecture is false}. (A \emph{conjecture} is a claim that someone believes but no one has yet proved.) If you attempt to prove a claim by contradiction and find no contradiction, you cannot tell whether the proof stalled because you missed a step or because the claim is false. Figure~\ref{08:fig-stalled-attempt} shows the two possible outcomes of an attempt.

\subsection{Exercises}

\paragraph{Warm-up and mental checks.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Spotting the phantom.} Consider the following attempted proof:
\begin{quote}
\itshape ``Claim: If $x > 3$, then $2x + 1 > 7$.\\
Proof: Suppose, for contradiction, that $x > 3$ and $2x + 1 \le 7$. Since $x > 3$, multiplying by $2$ yields $2x > 6$, and adding $1$ gives $2x + 1 > 7$. But this contradicts $2x + 1 \le 7$. Thus the claim is proved.''
\end{quote}
Explain why this is a phantom contradiction. Rewrite the argument as a short, direct two-sentence proof.

\item \textbf{Parity breakdown on perfect squares.} If you apply the $\sqrt{2}$ contradiction proof to $\sqrt{9}$, you write $\sqrt{9} = a/b$ in lowest terms, so $a^2 = 9b^2$. Then $a^2$ is a multiple of $3$, so $a = 3k$. Show the next step of algebraic substitution, and identify the exact moment where the proof fails to force $b$ to share a factor of $3$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Core practice.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item Prove that $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational. (You may use without proof the lemma: for any integer $n$, if $3 \mid n^2$, then $3 \mid n$.)
\item Prove that the sum of a rational number and an irrational number is always irrational.
\item Prove by contradiction that there is no smallest positive rational number. (Hint: If $r \in \Q$ with $r > 0$ were the smallest, what can you say about $r/2$?)
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Deeper exploration.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item Prove that $\log_{10}(2)$ is irrational. (Here $\log_{10}(2)$ is the power to which $10$ must be raised to give $2$, so $10^{\log_{10}(2)} = 2$. Hint: Assume $\log_{10}(2) = a/b$ with positive integers $a, b$, rewrite in exponential form $10^a = 2^b$, and use the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, which says every integer greater than $1$ factors into primes in exactly one way, apart from the order of the factors.)
\item Re-examine the Euclid number
\[
N = (2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13) + 1 = 30031 = 59 \cdot 509.
\]
Using the number-line picture of Figure~\ref{08:fig-prime-strides}, explain why none of the first six primes $2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13$ divides $30031$, without performing any division. Why does this force the prime factors $59$ and $509$ to be primes missing from the list?
\item Let $p$ be any prime number. Using Euclid's lemma (if a prime $p$ divides a product $ab$, then $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$), prove that $\sqrt{p}$ is irrational.
\end{enumerate}
