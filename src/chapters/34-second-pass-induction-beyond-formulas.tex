\section{Second Pass: Induction Beyond Formulas}
\chapterline

In Chapter~33, we learned to view mathematical structures through classification: equivalence relations partitioned existing sets into classes, and quotients collapsed internal details to build new objects like modular numbers and geometric tori. But equivalence classes and quotient spaces treat a universe of objects as complete collections.

What happens when mathematical objects are not given all at once, but are generated recursively? And how do we prove a guarantee holds across an infinite family of objects without checking each one individually?

Most students first meet the answer---mathematical induction---as a method for verifying summation formulas:
\[
1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
\]
You check $n=1$, assume the statement for $k$, add $k+1$ to both sides, factor an expression, and complete the induction step. 

While valid, this formulaic introduction obscures what induction truly is (recall the visual proof of this sum via interlocking staircases in Chapter~17). Induction extends far beyond summation formulas: it is a general method for reasoning about inductively defined structures, including integers, syntax trees, and recursive data types.

\begin{historybox}
In 1904, the German mathematician Ernst Zermelo tackled a problem that had stumped mathematicians for decades: Georg Cantor's conjecture that \emph{every} set can be well-ordered. A set is \emph{well-ordered} if every non-empty subset has a unique smallest element---just like the positive integers, where any collection you choose has an absolute minimum. While the counting numbers $1 < 2 < 3 < \dots$ come equipped with a natural bottom element, no one knew how to order the continuous real numbers $\R$ so that even open intervals like $(0, 1)$ would possess a least element.

To solve this, Zermelo introduced the \emph{Axiom of Choice}---the principle stating that given an infinite collection of non-empty bins, you can select exactly one item from each bin simultaneously, even without an explicit recipe for choosing. Zermelo proved that with this axiom, every set can indeed be well-ordered, justifying \emph{transfinite induction}---a technique that extends induction far past the counting numbers into infinite ordinal hierarchies. Yet his proof triggered fierce controversy: French analysts \'Emile Borel, Ren\'e Baire, and Henri Lebesgue rejected the result, arguing that a well-ordering you cannot compute or write down is a metaphysical fiction.

Six decades later in 1962, computer science pioneer John McCarthy faced a different puzzle: how can you mathematically prove that a computer program terminates, or that a compiler translates code correctly without introducing bugs? Programs do not process flat lists of numbers; they manipulate recursive trees, compilers, nested grammars, and syntax trees. Building on McCarthy's ideas, Rod Burstall (1969) formulated \emph{structural induction}---proving properties of branching data structures directly from atomic leaves to root, without ever counting along a number line. 

Today, structural induction forms the mathematical backbone of modern proof assistants such as Lean, Coq, and Isabelle. Yet foundational questions remain alive: the boundary between constructive mathematics (which works without the Axiom of Choice) and classical set theory continues to shape modern type theory, while automated induction for arbitrary program loops remains undecidable in general due to Alan Turing's halting problem.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The Minimal Counterexample: Dominoes in Reverse}

To understand why induction works, it is best to view it through the lens of the \textbf{Well-Ordering Principle (WOP)}:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Every non-empty subset of positive integers contains a smallest element.}
\end{quote}
No matter how complicated the rule defining the subset is, if at least one positive integer satisfies it, there is a unique first positive integer that satisfies it.

This property provides a direct proof strategy in pure mathematics: the \textbf{method of minimal counterexample}.

Consider a sequence of statements $P(1), P(2), P(3), \dots$. Standard induction proves the base case $P(1)$ and shows that $P(k)$ implies $P(k+1)$, establishing that the property holds for all successive integers.

The minimal counterexample method takes the contrapositive perspective. Suppose the claim fails for at least one positive integer. Then the set of counterexamples is non-empty. By the Well-Ordering Principle, there must be a \emph{first} integer where the claim fails. Let that earliest failure be $m$.

Because $m$ is the minimal failure, every integer strictly before it satisfies the property: $P(1), P(2), \dots, P(m-1)$ are all true.

We then use the truth of those preceding cases to deduce that $P(m)$ holds after all. This contradicts the choice of $m$ as a counterexample. Consequently, no counterexample exists, and the claim holds for all positive integers.

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Mental Check:} Why does the minimal counterexample method fail if you try to apply it to a claim about the set of all integers $\Z$ or the positive rational numbers $\Q^+$?
\\[0.3em]
\emph{Answer:} Neither $(\Z, <)$ nor $(\Q^+, <)$ satisfies the Well-Ordering Principle! In $\Z$, the subset of all negative integers has no minimum element; you can walk leftward toward $-\infty$ forever. In $\Q^+$, the subset $\{1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, \dots\}$ gets arbitrarily close to $0$, but has no minimum element inside the set. Without a guaranteed earliest failure $m = \min(S)$, the minimal counterexample strategy cannot even begin.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{Subtle Induction Traps: Missing Overlaps and Infinite Descent}

Mathematical induction harbors subtle traps when hypotheses or bases are checked carelessly:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{P\'olya's Horse Paradox}: In a celebrated cautionary tale, George P\'olya showed how a flawed inductive step can ``prove'' that all horses are the same color. Let $P(n)$ be the claim that in any set of $n$ horses, all have the same color. The base case $n=1$ holds immediately: in any single-horse set, every horse has the same color. In the induction step, assume $P(k)$ holds and consider $k+1$ horses. Remove horse $1$; the remaining $k$ horses share a color. Reinsert horse $1$ and remove horse $k+1$; those $k$ horses share a color. If $k \ge 2$, these two subsets overlap, forcing all $k+1$ horses to share the same color. The error occurs when transitioning from $k=1$ to $k=2$: the subsets $\{1\}$ and $\{2\}$ have no common element, so the induction step does not hold for $k=1$. An induction step that fails for even a single early value invalidates the proof.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Infinite Descending Chain Trap}: Ordinary induction works on $\N$ because $\N$ has a least element ($1$) and contains no infinite descending chains ($n > n-1 > \dots$). A partially ordered set is \emph{well-founded} if every non-empty subset has a minimal element---or equivalently, if it contains no infinite strictly decreasing sequence $x_0 > x_1 > x_2 > \dots$. If you attempt induction on the integers $\Z$ or the positive rationals $\Q^+$, the order is not well-founded: $\dots < -3 < -2 < -1$, or $1 > 1/2 > 1/4 > \dots > 0$. Without a well-founded order, an inductive argument has no base case to ground the descent.
\end{itemize}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Mental Check:} Suppose you verify $P(1)$ is true, and you prove that $P(k) \implies P(k+2)$ for every integer $k \ge 1$. Which statements have been proven?
\\[0.3em]
\emph{Answer:} Only the odd statements $P(1), P(3), P(5), \dots$. The even statements $P(2), P(4), P(6), \dots$ form a separate sequence. When the inductive step has stride $2$, one must verify two base cases ($P(1)$ and $P(2)$) to cover all positive integers.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{Structural Induction: Beyond Flat Numbers}

In computer science, logic, and abstract algebra, mathematical structures are often defined recursively rather than indexed by linear sequences. Examples include arithmetic expressions, syntax trees in compilers, propositional formulas, and graph structures.

Consider arithmetic expressions formed by numbers, variables, and binary operations ($+$ and $\times$):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Base Cases (Leaves)}: Any numeric constant $c$ or variable $x$ is a valid expression.
  \item \textbf{Recursive Rule (Internal Nodes)}: If $E_1$ and $E_2$ are valid expressions, then $(E_1 + E_2)$ and $(E_1 \times E_2)$ are valid expressions.
  \item \textbf{Extremal Clause}: Nothing else is an expression unless constructed through these rules.
\end{itemize}

To prove a property $P(E)$ holds for \emph{all possible} expressions $E$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Base Step}: Show the property holds for all atomic leaves (every constant $c$ and variable $x$).
  \item \textbf{Inductive Step}: Assume the property holds for arbitrary sub-expressions $E_1$ and $E_2$ (the induction hypotheses). Prove that the composite expression $(E_1 \star E_2)$ inherits the property for any operation $\star \in \{+, \times\}$.
\end{enumerate}

No numerical index is required. The proof proceeds inductively from the leaves to the root, establishing the property for all expressions generated by the recursive definition.

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Mental Check:} In the expression tree for $2 \times (x + 5)$, what exact assumption constitutes the inductive hypothesis when proving that the root operator $\times$ satisfies the desired property?
\\[0.3em]
\emph{Answer:} The inductive hypothesis assumes the property has already been established for the left subtree (the leaf $2$) and the right subtree (the sub-expression $(x+5)$). You do not assume the property holds for the combined expression; your task is to show that combining two valid subtrees through the multiplication operator preserves the guarantee.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{Worked Example: The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic}

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic states that every integer $n \ge 2$ can be factored into prime numbers, and that this factorization is unique up to the order of factors. Let us prove the \emph{existence} of prime factorizations using the minimal counterexample method.

\begin{theorem}[Existence of Prime Factorizations]
Every integer $n \ge 2$ can be factored into a product of one or more prime numbers.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that there exist integers $n \geq 2$ that cannot be factored into primes. Let $S$ be the set of all such failing integers:
\[
S = \{n \in \Z : n \ge 2 \text{ and } n \text{ has no prime factorization}\}.
\]
By our contradictory assumption, $S$ is non-empty. Because $S$ is a non-empty subset of the positive integers, the Well-Ordering Principle guarantees that $S$ contains a smallest element:
\[
m = \min(S).
\]
Could $m$ be a prime number? No. If $m$ were prime, it would already be a product of a single prime ($m$ itself), which would mean $m \notin S$.

Therefore, $m$ must be composite. By definition of a composite integer, $m$ factors into two strictly smaller positive integers:
\[
m = a \cdot b, \qquad \text{with } 1 < a < m \quad \text{and} \quad 1 < b < m.
\]
Now look at where $a$ and $b$ live. Because both $a$ and $b$ are strictly smaller than $m$, and $m$ was chosen to be the \emph{absolute smallest} member of $S$, neither $a$ nor $b$ can belong to $S$!

Consequently, both $a$ and $b$ must have valid prime factorizations:
\[
a = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_j, \qquad b = q_1 q_2 \cdots q_k,
\]
where each $p_i$ and $q_i$ is prime. Multiplying these factorizations together gives:
\[
m = a \cdot b = (p_1 p_2 \cdots p_j)(q_1 q_2 \cdots q_k).
\]
This displays $m$ as a product of primes, which directly contradicts our statement that $m \in S$. 

Because assuming $S$ was non-empty led to a direct contradiction, $S$ must be empty. Every integer $n \geq 2$ can be factored into primes.
\end{proof}

Figure~\ref{34:fig-prime-factorization-wop} diagrams the structure of this minimal counterexample proof. By defining $m$ as the minimal element of $S$, every integer strictly between $2$ and $m-1$ factors into primes. Because $m$ is composite, it factors as $m = a \cdot b$ with $1 < a, b < m$. Since both $a$ and $b$ are strictly smaller than $m$, both have prime factorizations. Multiplying these factorizations together produces a prime factorization for $m$, which contradicts $m \in S$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Minimal Counterexample Versus Standard Induction:}
Proving this theorem with standard induction ($k \implies k+1$) is difficult because the factorization of $k$ provides no direct information about the factorization of $k+1$ (for instance, knowing $14 = 2 \cdot 7$ does not determine the factors of $15 = 3 \cdot 5$). 

Standard induction would require strong induction, assuming every integer from $2$ up to $k$ factors into primes. The minimal counterexample method organizes the same logic concisely: by choosing the minimal counterexample $m = \min(S)$, the induction hypothesis automatically applies to all strictly smaller integers $a, b < m$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{What Generalized Induction Unlocks---and Its Limits}

Induction extends naturally beyond arithmetic formulas to structured domains:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Software Verification and Compilers}: Structural induction proves that compilers preserve semantic program behavior, that type checkers guarantee memory safety, and that recursive algorithms (like tree traversals or quicksort) terminate.
  \item \textbf{Minimal Counterexample Clarity}: The Well-Ordering Principle proves structural theorems in discrete mathematics---such as graph coloring bounds, the Division Algorithm, and the Eulerian path theorem---by isolating the minimal failing instance directly.
  \item \textbf{Transfinite Reasoning}: In set theory, transfinite induction across ordinal numbers allows mathematicians to prove algebraic closure existence and construct maximal ideals in ring theory via Zorn's Lemma.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
Induction has definite theoretical and practical boundaries:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Continuum Barrier}: Well-ordering on uncountable sets (guaranteed by the Axiom of Choice) is non-constructive. One cannot use induction to prove statements about continuous functions on the real line $\R$ by stepping from one real number to the next, because under the standard ordering of $\R$, there is no next real number: between any two distinct reals lies another real number.
  \item \textbf{Cyclic and Continuous Settings}: Structural induction strictly requires a well-founded partial order with no cycles and no infinite descending paths. For infinite data streams, cyclic network graphs, or differential equations, ordinary induction does not apply directly. Handling these settings requires coinduction or topological compactness (which reduces infinite open covers to finite subcovers).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Warm-up and Fallacy Audits}:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item A student claims to prove $P(n)$ for all integers $n \ge 1$ by showing that $P(1)$ is true and that whenever $P(k)$ is true, $P(k+2)$ is true. Which integers have actually been proven to satisfy $P$? What minimal additional work would prove the claim for all $n \ge 1$?
    \item Which of the following subsets of $\R$ have a smallest element? Explain using the Well-Ordering Principle or the concept of well-foundedness.
    \[
    A = \{x \in \Q : x > 0\}, \quad B = \left\{n - \frac{1}{m} : n, m \in \Z^+\right\}, \quad C = \{n^2 - 10n + 30 : n \in \Z^+\}.
    \]
    \item In P\'olya's horse paradox, for a set of $n=2$ horses $\{H_1, H_2\}$, write down explicitly the two subsets of size $n-1 = 1$, and compute their intersection. Why does this calculation show why the proof fails?
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Full Binary Trees via Structural Induction}:
  A full binary tree is defined recursively as follows:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Base Case}: A single leaf node is a full binary tree with $L=1$ leaf and $I=0$ internal nodes.
    \item \emph{Recursive Step}: If $T_1$ and $T_2$ are full binary trees, joining them under a new root node creates a new full binary tree $T$ whose leaf set is the union of the leaves of $T_1$ and $T_2$, and whose internal nodes consist of the internal nodes of $T_1$ and $T_2$ plus the new root.
  \end{itemize}
  Use structural induction to prove that for every full binary tree $T$,
  \[
  L(T) = I(T) + 1.
  \]

  \item \textbf{Parenthesis Balancing in Propositional Logic}:
  Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the set of propositional formulas generated from variable symbols $\{p, q, r, \dots\}$ by the following grammar:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Any variable symbol $p$ is in $\mathcal{F}$.
    \item If $A \in \mathcal{F}$, then $(\neg A) \in \mathcal{F}$.
    \item If $A, B \in \mathcal{F}$, then $(A \land B) \in \mathcal{F}$ and $(A \lor B) \in \mathcal{F}$.
  \end{itemize}
  Use structural induction to prove that for every formula $F \in \mathcal{F}$, the number of left parentheses in $F$ equals the number of right parentheses in $F$.

  \item \textbf{The Division Algorithm via Minimal Counterexample}:
  Let $a$ and $b$ be integers with $b > 0$. We wish to prove that there exist integers $q$ and $r$ such that $a = bq + r$ with $0 \le r < b$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Define the set of non-negative differences:
    \[
    S = \{a - bq : q \in \Z \text{ and } a - bq \ge 0\}.
    \]
    Show that $S$ is non-empty. (Hint: consider $q = -|a|$.)
    \item By the Well-Ordering Principle, let $r = \min(S)$, and let $q \in \Z$ be the quotient such that $r = a - bq$. Prove by contradiction that $r < b$. (Hint: if $r \ge b$, consider $r - b = a - b(q+1)$.)
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ via Minimal Counterexample}:
  Suppose for contradiction that $\sqrt{2}$ is rational, so $\sqrt{2} = a/b$ for positive integers $a, b$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Define the set $S = \{k \in \Z^+ : k\sqrt{2} \in \Z^+\}$. Show that $S$ is non-empty.
    \item Since $S$ is a non-empty subset of $\Z^+$, the Well-Ordering Principle guarantees a minimum $m = \min(S)$.
    \item Define $m' = m(\sqrt{2} - 1)$. Show that $m'$ is an integer, that $m' > 0$, and that $m'\sqrt{2} \in \Z^+$, so $m' \in S$.
    \item Show that $m' < m$, producing a direct contradiction with $m = \min(S)$.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Tournaments and Hamiltonian Paths}:
  A round-robin tournament among $n \ge 2$ players is a directed graph where every pair of distinct players $u, v$ has exactly one directed edge between them (either $u \to v$, meaning $u$ defeated $v$, or $v \to u$, meaning $v$ defeated $u$).
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item A \emph{Hamiltonian path} is a linear ordering of all $n$ players $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n$ such that $p_i \to p_{i+1}$ for all $1 \le i \le n-1$. Verify that every $2$-player tournament has a Hamiltonian path.
    \item Assume that every tournament on $k$ players has a Hamiltonian path $p_1 \to p_2 \to \dots \to p_k$. Add a new player $v$. If $v$ beats $p_1$, where can you insert $v$? If $p_k$ beats $v$, where can you insert $v$?
    \item If neither of those occurs, show that there must be an index $i$ such that $p_i \to v$ and $v \to p_{i+1}$. Deduce that every tournament on $n$ players has a Hamiltonian path.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
