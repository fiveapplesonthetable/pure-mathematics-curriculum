\section{Orders: Another Kind of Relation}
\chapterline

In Chapter~15, we studied equivalence relations. An equivalence relation sorts the elements of a set into groups of equivalent elements: two fractions like $2/4$ and $3/6$ represent the same number; two integers with the same remainder modulo $12$ land on the same hour of a clock face. Equivalence partitions a set into disjoint equivalence classes.

An \textbf{order relation} does something different. Instead of grouping elements, it ranks them: it says which elements come before, sit inside, or are smaller than which others.

On the real line $\R$, every pair of numbers is comparable: given $x$ and $y$, either $x < y$, $x = y$, or $x > y$. But many mathematical structures do not arrange themselves along a single linear chain.

Consider getting dressed in the morning. You must put on socks before shoes, and a shirt before a sweater. But which comes first: your socks or your shirt? Your watch or your belt? Neither has to come first. You can put your watch on before or after your shirt without any conflict. The morning routine has definite rules---you cannot put on shoes before socks---yet the tasks do not form a single straight line; they branch. Figure~\ref{16:fig-dressing} draws these rules.

Branching rankings like this appear throughout mathematics. Among sets, $\{1, 2\}$ contains $\{1\}$, but of $\{1, 2\}$ and $\{2, 3\}$, neither is contained in the other. The mathematical tool for describing rankings that may branch is the \textbf{partial order}.

\begin{historybox}
For centuries, mathematicians treated ordering as identical to the real number line, where any two numbers can be compared. When George Boole (\emph{The Laws of Thought}, 1854) and Charles Sanders Peirce (1870s--1880s) expressed logic and set theory algebraically, they observed that subset inclusion $\subseteq$ and implication $\implies$ act like orders, but with a key difference: two subsets or propositions can be \emph{incomparable}, meaning neither one comes before the other.

In the 1930s, Garrett Birkhoff developed modern order theory in his book \emph{Lattice Theory}. He showed that the same ordering structures (partially ordered sets, and the special kind called lattices, both defined in this chapter) turn up in the algebra of sets and logic, in geometry, and in analysis.

Order theory also explains a problem with voting. In 1785, the Marquis de Condorcet showed that even if every voter ranks the options consistently, the majority can go in a circle: most voters prefer $A$ to $B$, most prefer $B$ to $C$, and yet most prefer $C$ to $A$. In 1951, Kenneth Arrow proved in his Impossibility Theorem that no ranked voting system can convert individual preferences into a single collective order while satisfying a small set of fair conditions.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Partial Orders: The Definition}

We write $\preceq$ (read ``precedes or equals'') for a general order relation. It is a stand-in symbol: in one example it will mean $\le$, in another $\subseteq$, in another ``divides''.

\begin{definition}[Partial Order]
A relation $\preceq$ on a set $A$ is a \textbf{partial order} if it satisfies three conditions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Reflexivity:} For all $a \in A$,
\[
a \preceq a.
\]
(Every element is related to itself.)
\item \textbf{Antisymmetry:} For all $a, b \in A$,
\[
(a \preceq b \text{ and } b \preceq a) \implies a = b.
\]
(If two elements precede each other, they are identical.)
\item \textbf{Transitivity:} For all $a, b, c \in A$,
\[
(a \preceq b \text{ and } b \preceq c) \implies a \preceq c.
\]
(If $a$ comes before $b$ and $b$ comes before $c$, then $a$ comes before $c$.)
\end{enumerate}
A set $A$ equipped with a partial order $\preceq$ is called a \textbf{partially ordered set}, or \textbf{poset} $(A, \preceq)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{intuitionbox}
\emph{Antisymmetry} is the condition that separates orders from equivalence relations. In an equivalence relation, symmetry means that if $a \sim b$, then $b \sim a$. In an order relation, two distinct elements cannot precede each other: if $a \preceq b$ and $b \preceq a$, then $a$ and $b$ must be equal.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{marginnotebox}
\textbf{Weak versus Strict Orders:} The symbol $\preceq$ represents a \textbf{weak} partial order (analogous to $\le$ or $\subseteq$), where every element is related to itself ($a \preceq a$). In contrast, a \textbf{strict} partial order $\prec$ (analogous to $<$ or to $\subset$, ``is a subset of but not equal to'') is defined by
\[
a \prec b \iff (a \preceq b \text{ and } a \neq b).
\]
It is \textbf{irreflexive} (no element is related to itself: $a \not\prec a$) and transitive. Mathematicians use ``partial order'' to mean a weak order unless specified otherwise.
\end{marginnotebox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Is the strict inequality relation $<$ on $\R$ a partial order under our definition? Test the three axioms: reflexivity fails immediately, since $5 < 5$ is false. The relation $<$ is a \emph{strict} order. To be a partial order in our standard sense, reflexivity is mandatory, which is why we work with $\le$.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Partial Versus Total Orders}

\begin{definition}[Comparability and Total Order]
Let $(A, \preceq)$ be a poset. Two elements $x, y \in A$ are \textbf{comparable} if $x \preceq y$ or $y \preceq x$. If neither condition holds, $x$ and $y$ are \textbf{incomparable}, written $x \parallel y$.

A partial order $\preceq$ is a \textbf{total order} (or \textbf{linear order}) if every pair of elements is comparable:
\[
(\forall x, y \in A)(x \preceq y \text{ or } y \preceq x).
\]
\end{definition}

On the real numbers $(\R, \le)$, any two numbers are comparable: given $3$ and $\pi$, we know $3 \le \pi$. The real numbers line up in a single queue with no branching.

Now consider the power set $\pow(\{1, 2, 3\})$ ordered by subset inclusion $\subseteq$. 
Is $\{1\} \subseteq \{2\}$? No.
Is $\{2\} \subseteq \{1\}$? No.
Neither set contains the other. They are incomparable under inclusion: $\{1\} \parallel \{2\}$. The elements do not line up in a single linear chain; they form a partially ordered set. Figure~\ref{16:fig-chain-vs-cube} draws the two shapes side by side, with each element placed above the elements it is larger than.

Similarly, consider the positive integers $\N = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ ordered by divisibility ($a \mid b$). Is $4 \mid 6$? No. Is $6 \mid 4$? No. Thus $4 \parallel 6$. So divisibility is not a total order. That it is a partial order is proved below.

A \textbf{Hasse diagram} (named after the German number theorist Helmut Hasse) removes this clutter using three rules:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{Upward Flow:} If $x \prec y$ (that is, $x \preceq y$ and $x \neq y$), place $y$ higher on the page than $x$. Height shows the direction, so no arrowheads are needed.
\item \textbf{Omit Reflexive Self-Loops:} Reflexivity ($x \preceq x$) is taken for granted; no loops are drawn.
\item \textbf{Omit Transitive Shortcuts:} Draw a line between $x$ and $y$ only if $y$ \emph{covers} $x$ (defined next).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{definition}[Covering Relation]
Let $(A, \preceq)$ be a poset. We say that $y$ \textbf{covers} $x$ (written $x \lessdot y$) if $x \prec y$ and there is no intermediate element $z \in A$ such that $x \prec z \prec y$.
\end{definition}

In plain words, $y$ covers $x$ if $y$ is the next rung above $x$, with nothing in between. A Hasse diagram draws only these covering lines. To read off the full order, follow lines upward: $x \preceq y$ exactly when you can climb from $x$ to $y$ (or $x = y$).

\subsection{Worked Proof: Divisibility as a Poset}

Let $\N = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ be the positive integers. We define the relation $a \preceq b \iff a \mid b$ ($a$ divides $b$).

\begin{theorem}
Divisibility is a partial order on $\N$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We verify the three required conditions:

\textbf{1. Reflexivity:} For any $a \in \N$, $a = a \cdot 1$. Since $1 \in \N$, $a \mid a$, so $a \preceq a$.

\textbf{2. Antisymmetry:} Suppose $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$.
Then there exist positive integers $k, \ell \in \N$ such that $b = k a$ and $a = \ell b$.
Substituting $b$ into the second equation:
\begin{align*}
a &= \ell b \\
  &= \ell (k a) && \text{(since $b = k a$)} \\
  &= (\ell k) a.
\end{align*}
Since $a > 0$, we divide by $a$ to obtain $\ell k = 1$.
Because $\ell$ and $k$ are positive integers, the only solution to $\ell k = 1$ is $\ell = k = 1$.
Substituting $k = 1$ into $b = k a$ yields $b = a$. Hence antisymmetry holds.

\textbf{3. Transitivity:} Suppose $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$.
Then $b = j a$ and $c = m b$ for some $j, m \in \N$.
Substituting $b$:
\begin{align*}
c &= m b \\
  &= m (j a) && \text{(since $b = j a$)} \\
  &= (m j) a.
\end{align*}
Since $m j$ is a positive integer, $a \mid c$. Hence transitivity holds.

Since all three conditions hold, $(\N, \mid)$ is a poset.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
Why did we restrict divisibility to the positive integers $\N$ rather than all integers $\Z$?
On $\Z$, $2 \mid (-2)$ (since $-2 = (-1) \cdot 2$) and $(-2) \mid 2$ (since $2 = (-1) \cdot (-2)$). Yet $2 \neq -2$. Antisymmetry fails on $\Z$ because the equation $\ell k = 1$ has two solutions in $\Z$: $\ell = k = 1$ and $\ell = k = -1$. So in $\Z$, if $a$ and $b$ divide each other, we can only conclude $a = b$ or $a = -b$. The same rule can be a partial order on one set and fail to be one on a larger set, so always check which set a relation lives on.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Extremal Elements: Maximal, Minimal, Greatest, and Least}

In everyday language, ``maximal'' and ``maximum'' are often used interchangeably. In order theory, they have distinct mathematical meanings.

\begin{definition}[Extremal Elements]
Let $(A, \preceq)$ be a poset, and let $S \subseteq A$ be a subset.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item An element $M \in S$ is the \textbf{greatest element} (or \textbf{maximum}) of $S$ if for every $x \in S$,
\[
x \preceq M.
\]
(Plain meaning: $M$ is greater than or equal to every element in the set.)
\item An element $m \in S$ is a \textbf{maximal element} of $S$ if for all $x \in S$,
\[
m \preceq x \implies x = m.
\]
(Plain meaning: no element in the set is strictly greater than $m$.)
\item An element $L \in S$ is the \textbf{least element} (or \textbf{minimum}) of $S$ if for every $x \in S$,
\[
L \preceq x.
\]
(Plain meaning: $L$ is less than or equal to every element in the set.)
\item An element $\ell \in S$ is a \textbf{minimal element} of $S$ if for all $x \in S$,
\[
x \preceq \ell \implies x = \ell.
\]
(Plain meaning: no element in the set is strictly less than $\ell$.)
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The difference between ``greatest'' and ``maximal'':
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{greatest element} must be comparable to, and greater than or equal to, every element in the set ($x \preceq M$ for all $x$).
\item A \textbf{maximal element} only requires that no element in the set is strictly greater than it ($m \preceq x \implies x = m$).
\end{itemize}

Every greatest element $M$ is automatically maximal: if $M \preceq x$ for some $x \in S$, then because $M$ is greatest we also have $x \preceq M$, so antisymmetry forces $x = M$. The converse fails: in a poset that is not totally ordered, a maximal element need not be comparable to every other element. It only needs to have nothing strictly above it. Figure~\ref{16:fig-maximal-vs-greatest} shows both situations.

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of Greatest and Least Elements]
If a subset $S$ of a poset has a greatest element, that element is unique. Similarly, if $S$ has a least element, it is unique.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $M_1, M_2 \in S$ are both greatest elements of $S$. Since $M_1 \in S$ and $M_2$ is a greatest element, $M_1 \preceq M_2$. Reversing roles, since $M_2 \in S$ and $M_1$ is a greatest element, $M_2 \preceq M_1$. By the antisymmetry of $\preceq$, $M_1 = M_2$. The argument for the least element is identical with inequalities reversed.
\end{proof}

While a poset can have at most \emph{one} greatest element, it can have \emph{many} maximal elements.

\begin{example}[Maximal Without Maximum]
Let $S = \pow(\{1, 2, 3\}) \setminus \{\emptyset, \{1, 2, 3\}\}$ be the collection of all subsets of $\{1, 2, 3\}$ other than the empty set and the whole set (the non-empty \emph{proper} subsets), ordered by $\subseteq$:
\[
S = \big\{ \{1\}, \, \{2\}, \, \{3\}, \, \{1, 2\}, \, \{1, 3\}, \, \{2, 3\} \big\}.
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item The maximal elements of $S$ are $\{1, 2\}$, $\{1, 3\}$, and $\{2, 3\}$. None of these subsets is contained in any other element of $S$. Yet none of them is a greatest element: for example, $\{1, 2\}$ is not greatest because $\{2, 3\} \not\subseteq \{1, 2\}$. The set $S$ has three maximal elements and \emph{no} greatest element.
\item Symmetrically, the minimal elements of $S$ are $\{1\}$, $\{2\}$, and $\{3\}$. None contains any other element of $S$, but none is a least element (for example, $\{1\} \not\subseteq \{2\}$). The set $S$ has three minimal elements and \emph{no} least element.
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{16:fig-max-without-max} draws $S$ as a Hasse diagram.
\end{example}

\subsection{Bounds, Suprema, and Lattices}

A subset $S$ may have no greatest element of its own. We can still ask which elements of the whole poset sit above or below all of $S$.

\begin{definition}[Bounds, Supremum, and Infimum]
Let $(A, \preceq)$ be a poset, and let $S \subseteq A$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item An element $u \in A$ is an \textbf{upper bound} for $S$ if $x \preceq u$ for all $x \in S$.
\item If the set of all upper bounds of $S$ has a least element, that element is called the \textbf{least upper bound} or \textbf{supremum} of $S$, written $\sup S$.
\item An element $\ell \in A$ is a \textbf{lower bound} for $S$ if $\ell \preceq x$ for all $x \in S$.
\item If the set of all lower bounds of $S$ has a greatest element, that element is called the \textbf{greatest lower bound} or \textbf{infimum} of $S$, written $\inf S$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

An upper bound of $S$ may lie outside $S$; it only has to sit at or above every element of $S$. The supremum is the lowest of all the upper bounds, and the infimum is the highest of all the lower bounds. For example, in the divisors of $12$ under divisibility, take $S = \{2, 3\}$. The upper bounds are the divisors of $12$ that both $2$ and $3$ divide, namely $6$ and $12$; the lowest of these is $6$, so $\sup S = 6$. The only lower bound is $1$, so $\inf S = 1$. Figure~\ref{16:fig-bounds} shows this example and one on the real line.

When $S$ has two elements $\{a, b\}$, its supremum is called the \textbf{join} of $a$ and $b$, and its infimum is called the \textbf{meet}. We write
\[
a \lor b = \sup\{a, b\}, \qquad a \land b = \inf\{a, b\}.
\]
The symbols $\lor$ and $\land$ are borrowed from logical ``or'' and ``and'', but here they name the join and meet. In our examples they are familiar operations:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sets under inclusion ($\subseteq$):} The join is union, and the meet is intersection:
\[
A \lor B = A \cup B, \qquad A \land B = A \cap B.
\]
The union $A \cup B$ is the smallest set that contains both $A$ and $B$; the intersection $A \cap B$ is the largest set contained in both.
\item \textbf{Positive integers under divisibility ($\mid$):} The join is the least common multiple $\operatorname{lcm}(a, b)$ (the smallest positive number that both $a$ and $b$ divide; for example $\operatorname{lcm}(4, 6) = 12$), and the meet is the greatest common divisor:
\[
a \lor b = \operatorname{lcm}(a, b), \qquad a \land b = \gcd(a, b).
\]
The number $\operatorname{lcm}(a, b)$ is the least upper bound in $(\N, \mid)$: both $a$ and $b$ divide it, and it divides every common multiple of $a$ and $b$. Symmetrically, $\gcd(a, b)$ is the greatest lower bound in $(\N, \mid)$: it divides both $a$ and $b$, and every common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides $\gcd(a, b)$.
\item \textbf{Real numbers under standard order ($\le$):} Consider the open interval $S = (0, 1) \subset \R$. Any real number $u \ge 1$ is an upper bound. The lowest upper bound is $\sup S = 1$. Here $1 \notin S$: a supremum does not need to belong to the set it bounds. Real analysis, the study of limits (Chapter~20), uses this fact all the time.
\end{itemize}

A poset in which every pair of elements has a meet and a join is called a \textbf{lattice}. Figure~\ref{16:fig-meet-join} shows the meet and join of two incomparable elements. We return to lattices in Chapter~31, where we see that the algebra of sets and of true/false logic forms a lattice.

\subsection{Where Orders Matter and Where We Go Next}

Orders appear wherever there is ranking or dependency:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Compilers and scheduling:} Build tools must compile each piece of software after the pieces it depends on. ``Must come before'' is a partial order, and the tool has to choose a single run order that respects it. This is the dressing problem of Figure~\ref{16:fig-dressing}; finding such an order is called a \textbf{topological sort}.
\item \textbf{Sets and logic:} Sets under $\subseteq$, with union as join and intersection as meet, form a lattice; so do true/false statements under implication.
\item \textbf{Analysis:} Suprema and infima of sets of real numbers are central to the study of limits.
\end{itemize}

An order relation describes how elements line up. But on an infinite ordered set like $\N = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, knowing that $1 < 2 < 3 < \dots$ does not by itself prove a statement for all elements at once.

To prove a statement for every element of such an infinite sequence, we need a new proof method. In Chapter~17, we turn to \textbf{Induction: Infinite Claims, Finite Proofs}, which uses the order structure of the natural numbers to establish claims for all $n \in \N$ through finite proofs.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{(Mental Check)} Consider the divisibility poset $(\N, \mid)$ on the positive integers $\N = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Determine whether the following pairs are comparable: $(3, 12)$, $(4, 10)$, and $(7, 7)$.
\item Does $(\N, \mid)$ possess a least element? Does it possess a greatest element?
\item Explain why $(\N, \mid)$ is a partial order but not a total order.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{(Subset Inclusion as a Poset)} Let $S$ be any set. Prove that subset inclusion $\subseteq$ is a partial order on the power set $\pow(S)$ by directly verifying reflexivity, antisymmetry, and transitivity.

\item \textbf{(Hasse Diagram of Divisors)} Draw the Hasse diagram for the divisibility poset on the set of divisors of $30$:
\[
D(30) = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30\}.
\]
\emph{Hint:} $30 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$ is a product of three distinct prime numbers. How does your diagram relate to the three-dimensional cube representing $\pow(\{2, 3, 5\})$?

\item \textbf{(Maximal versus Greatest)} Let $S = \pow(\{1, 2, 3\}) \setminus \{\emptyset, \{1, 2, 3\}\}$ be the collection of all proper, non-empty subsets of $\{1, 2, 3\}$, ordered by subset inclusion $\subseteq$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item List all maximal elements and all minimal elements of $S$.
\item Does $S$ have a greatest element? Does $S$ have a least element?
\item In your own words, explain how a set can have several maximal elements without having any greatest element.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{(The Product Order)} Let $(A, \preceq_A)$ and $(B, \preceq_B)$ be posets. We define the \emph{product order} $\preceq$ on the Cartesian product $A \times B$ by:
\[
(a_1, b_1) \preceq (a_2, b_2) \iff (a_1 \preceq_A a_2 \text{ and } b_1 \preceq_B b_2).
\]
Prove that $\preceq$ is a partial order on $A \times B$.

\item \textbf{(Lexicographic Order versus Product Order)} Let $(A, \le_A)$ and $(B, \le_B)$ be total orders. The \emph{lexicographic order} (or dictionary order) $\le_{\text{lex}}$ on $A \times B$ is defined by:
\[
(a_1, b_1) \le_{\text{lex}} (a_2, b_2) \iff (a_1 <_A a_2) \text{ or } (a_1 = a_2 \text{ and } b_1 \le_B b_2).
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove that if $\le_A$ and $\le_B$ are total orders, then $\le_{\text{lex}}$ is a total order on $A \times B$.
\item Find two points in $\R \times \R$ that are comparable under lexicographic order but incomparable under the product order of Exercise~5.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{(Lattice Operations in Divisibility)} In the divisibility poset $(\N, \mid)$, let $a, b \in \N$. Remember that ``lower/upper'' and ``least/greatest'' here refer to the divisibility order $\mid$, not $\le$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove that the greatest lower bound $\inf\{a, b\}$ in $(\N, \mid)$ is $\gcd(a, b)$ (using B\'ezout's identity $\gcd(a, b) = ax + by$ for some $x, y \in \Z$, or prime factorizations, to show every common divisor divides $\gcd(a, b)$).
\item Prove that the least upper bound $\sup\{a, b\}$ in $(\N, \mid)$ is $\operatorname{lcm}(a, b)$ (hint: divide any common multiple $m$ by $\ell = \operatorname{lcm}(a, b)$ as $m = q\ell + r$ with $0 \le r < \ell$, and check whether $r$ would also be a common multiple).
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
