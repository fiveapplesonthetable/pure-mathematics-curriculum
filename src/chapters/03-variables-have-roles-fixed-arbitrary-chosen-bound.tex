\section{Variables Have Roles: Fixed, Arbitrary, Chosen, and Bound}
\chapterline

In Chapter~2 (\emph{Mathematical Sentences: Statements, Predicates, and Claims}), we saw that a predicate is an open sentence containing empty slots, like $P(x)$: ``$x$ is prime.'' To evaluate whether the sentence is true or false, those slots must be filled by concrete objects or bound by quantifiers.

In elementary algebra, a variable often represents an unknown number in an equation. You solve $3x + 4 = 19$, isolate $x$, and find $x = 5$, where $x$ represents a specific numerical value.

In proof-based mathematics, a letter can do several different jobs. It may name a number handed to you by the problem, stand for any element of a set at all, name a specific object you build yourself, or serve as an internal counter inside a formula. Each job comes with its own rules: what you may assume about the letter, who decides its value, and in which part of the text the letter has a meaning. Mixing up these jobs is a frequent source of error in proofs.

Consider a beginner trying to prove that every integer $n \ge 2$ has a prime factor:
\begin{quote}
\itshape Flawed attempt: Let $n = 12$. Since $12 = 3 \times 4$ and $3$ is prime, $n$ has a prime factor. Therefore, every integer $n \ge 2$ has a prime factor.
\end{quote}
The arithmetic is correct ($12 = 3 \times 4$), yet the argument is invalid. When a claim begins ``for every integer $n$,'' the argument must work for an \emph{arbitrary} integer: one about which we assume nothing except that it is an integer $n \ge 2$. Checking one specific number cannot prove a universal statement (a ``for every'' claim). Figure~\ref{03:fig-one-case} shows how little of the claim the check covers.

This chapter names the four roles a variable can play and the rules that go with each.

\begin{historybox}
For most of human history, even elementary algebra was purely rhetorical (written out entirely in words without mathematical symbols). Medieval mathematicians had no notation for unknowns or coefficients; every problem was phrased verbally using terms like \emph{cosa} (the Italian word for ``the thing'') and \emph{census} (``the square''). In 1591, the French mathematician Fran\c{c}ois Vi\`ete introduced, in \emph{In artem analyticem isagoge}, the systematic use of letters: vowels ($A, E, I$) for unknown quantities and consonants ($B, C, D$) for known parameters. For the first time, mathematicians could manipulate entire families of equations at once.

Yet introducing letters created a stubborn misconception that lasted for two centuries. In the 1700s, Leonhard Euler, Jean le Rond d'Alembert, and Daniel Bernoulli often treated variables as physical quantities that ``flow with time''---an intuition inherited from Isaac Newton's calculus of fluxions (Newton's term for the rates of change of quantities flowing continuously through time). This confusion erupted in the famous vibrating string controversy of 1747--1755, where d'Alembert, Euler, and Bernoulli argued bitterly over the motion of a plucked violin string. Because they lacked a precise definition of a function and had no formal rules for whether a letter was an arbitrary fixed input or an actively moving quantity, they frequently treated a variable as fixed in one step and varied it in the next, falling into contradictory results.

The German logician Gottlob Frege (in 1879) and the Italian mathematician Giuseppe Peano (between 1889 and 1895) cleared up this confusion by giving precise rules for variable binding (tying a letter to a quantifier such as $\forall$ or $\exists$) and for lexical scope (the stretch of text in which a variable name has a meaning). In the 1930s, Alonzo Church introduced the $\lambda$-calculus (lambda calculus, a formal system in which every variable is explicitly bound as the input of a function); its scoping rules were later adopted by many programming languages. In this view a variable is neither an unknown number nor a moving physical quantity: it is a placeholder with rules about where it has a meaning. Keeping the roles of variables apart keeps proofs sound and avoids variable capture (an expression accidentally binding a letter that was meant to refer to something outside it; the section on free and bound variables below gives an example).

Computer proof assistants (programs such as Lean, Coq, and Isabelle that check every step of a proof) still have to handle bound variables with care. Substituting an expression for a variable must never cause variable capture, and avoiding it by renaming letters is slow on large libraries of mathematics. Other ways of writing bound variables, such as De Bruijn indices (numbering placeholders by how many nested scopes enclose them instead of naming them with letters), are still being studied.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The four roles of variables}

Figure~\ref{03:fig-four-roles} summarizes the four roles. For each role it shows the words that introduce the variable, its \emph{scope} (the part of the text in which the letter has a meaning), and who decides its value. Two words in the figure need a gloss. An \emph{adversary} is an imagined opponent who picks values to try to break your claim. A \emph{witness} (as in Chapter~2) is a specific object that shows a ``there exists'' claim is true.

\subsection{Lexical scope: mathematical proofs as programs}

If you have written software, you already know variable scope (the region of source code where a name is recognized). A local variable declared inside a block of code, such as a loop body between curly braces, ceases to exist when the block ends. Using that variable after the closing brace is an error that the compiler reports before the program ever runs.

Mathematical proofs obey the same scoping rule, as Figure~\ref{03:fig-scope-code-proof} shows.

When a proof says ``Let $x \in \R$ be arbitrary,'' it opens a local scope block. Inside that block, $x$ is a fixed but unknown real number: its value does not change during the argument, and nothing is known about it except that $x \in \R$. Once you show that the desired property holds for that generic $x$, the universal statement $\forall x \in \R, \ldots$ is established, and the scope block closes. You cannot later refer to ``that $x$'' in other arguments.

A related error is reusing a witness's name. Suppose you are asked to prove a property about two even integers, $n$ and $m$. By definition, $n$ is even if there exists an integer $k$ such that $n = 2k$. A common beginner mistake is to write:
\begin{quote}
``Since $n$ is even, $n = 2k$. Since $m$ is even, $m = 2k$.''
\end{quote}
Here, the letter $k$ was a local witness tied specifically to the evenness of $n$. Reusing the exact same variable name for $m$ accidentally forces $n = m$, meaning you are no longer proving the claim for two arbitrary even integers, but only for the special case where both integers are equal. To maintain proper variable scoping, every fresh existential claim requires a fresh variable name: $n = 2k$ and $m = 2j$ for integers $k$ and $j$. Figure~\ref{03:fig-witness-names} shows the difference with $n = 6$ and $m = 10$.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: Spotting the scope leak}
Recall the set notation from Chapter~2: $A \subseteq B$ ($A$ is a subset of $B$), the intersection $A \cap B$, and the empty set $\emptyset$. A student has just proved $A \subseteq B$ and then adds one extra conclusion at the end:
\begin{quote}
``To show that $A \subseteq B$, let $x \in A$ be arbitrary. [Valid steps showing $x \in B$.] Hence $A \subseteq B$ is proved. Therefore, that element $x$ belongs to both $A$ and $B$, which proves that $A \cap B \neq \emptyset$.''
\end{quote}
What is wrong with the student's reasoning?
\\[0.3em]
\emph{Answer:} The variable $x$ was introduced as an arbitrary element inside the scope used to prove $A \subseteq B$. Once $A \subseteq B$ is established, that scope closes and $x$ no longer has a meaning. The argument does not show that $A$ contains any elements at all. If $A = \emptyset$, the claim $\emptyset \subseteq B$ is true automatically, because there is no element of $\emptyset$ to check (logicians call such a claim \emph{vacuously true}); yet $\emptyset \cap B = \emptyset$. Claiming that $x \in A \cap B$ in the next sentence uses a variable whose scope has already closed.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Free versus bound variables}

Look at the following three mathematical expressions:
\[
\text{(A)}\quad \sum_{i=1}^n i^2, \qquad\qquad \text{(B)}\quad \int_0^1 x^2 \, dx, \qquad\qquad \text{(C)}\quad x^2 + y^2 = 1.
\]
In (A), the symbol $\sum_{i=1}^n$ means ``add up the following term for $i = 1, 2, \ldots, n$,'' so (A) is $1^2 + 2^2 + \cdots + n^2$. In (B), $\int_0^1 \ldots \, dx$ is the integral from calculus: the area under the graph of $x^2$ between $x = 0$ and $x = 1$, where $dx$ marks $x$ as the letter that runs from $0$ to $1$.

In expression (A), the index $i$ is \emph{bound} by the summation. The letter $n$, however, is \emph{free}: the numerical value of the sum depends on what $n$ is. If you change $n$ from $3$ to $4$, the sum changes from $14$ to $30$. But if you replace every occurrence of $i$ with $j$, getting $\sum_{j=1}^n j^2$, the value is unchanged. The index $i$ is an internal counter, often called a \emph{dummy} variable.

In expression (B), the variable $x$ is bound by the integral. The expression evaluates to the number $\tfrac{1}{3}$, with no $x$ left in the answer. Writing ``let $x = 10$'' before the integral has no effect on its value. Figure~\ref{03:fig-integral-area} shows why: $x$ only marks positions along the horizontal axis while the area is measured, and the area is a single number.

In expression (C), both $x$ and $y$ are free. Without additional context, the equation is neither true nor false; it is a predicate, satisfied exactly by the pairs $(x, y)$ that lie on the unit circle (the circle of radius $1$ centred at the origin).

Set-builder notation binds a variable too: $\{x \in \R : x > 0\}$ is read ``the set of all real numbers $x$ such that $x > 0$.'' The $x$ is bound by the braces; the same set could be written $\{t \in \R : t > 0\}$.

In short: \textbf{free variables are parameters (values supplied by the surrounding context), while bound variables are internal placeholders local to an operator} such as $\sum$, $\int$, $\lim$, set-builder braces, or a quantifier. Figure~\ref{03:fig-bound-variable-capsule} shows both kinds in one expression.

Because a bound variable is internal, you may always rename it to any other symbol that is not already in use. In mathematical logic, this principle is called $\alpha$-equivalence (alpha-equivalence: two formulas count as the same if they differ only in the names of bound variables):
\[
\int_a^b f(t) \, dt = \int_a^b f(u) \, du = \int_a^b f(w) \, dw.
\]
The choice of letter $t, u, w$ carries no mathematical information.

However, you must never rename a bound variable to a letter that is already free in the surrounding context. In the sum $\sum_{k=1}^n k$, renaming $k$ to $n$ produces $\sum_{n=1}^n n$. This name collision is \emph{variable capture}: the free $n$ in the upper limit is captured by the summation index, and the result no longer has a meaningful upper limit, so the original formula is destroyed. Figure~\ref{03:fig-capture} compares a safe renaming with this one.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: Free or bound?}
In each of the following expressions, identify which letters are free and which are bound:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\displaystyle \int_1^x \frac{1}{t} \, dt = \ln x$.
\item $\displaystyle \{y \in \R : y^2 < c\}$.
\item $\displaystyle \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{Answers:} In (a), $x$ is free (it is the upper limit of integration); $t$ is bound by $dt$. In (b), $c$ is free (a parameter); $y$ is bound by the set-builder braces. In (c), $x$ and $f$ are free (the point and the function); $h$ is bound by the limit operator.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{A worked example of quantifier and role parsing}

Consider the statement:
\[
\forall x \in \R, \, \exists y \in \R \text{ such that } x + y = 0.
\]

It helps to picture a proof as a game between you (the prover) and the adversary from Figure~\ref{03:fig-four-roles}. A $\forall$ is a move by the adversary, who picks the value; an $\exists$ is your move, where you pick the value. Here are the roles, step by step:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The adversary moves first}: The statement begins with $\forall x \in \R$. Therefore, in our proof, we must begin by accepting an \emph{arbitrary} real number:
\begin{quote}
``Let $x \in \R$ be arbitrary.''
\end{quote}
We cannot choose $x = 0$ or $x = 5$. We must leave $x$ completely general.
\item \textbf{The prover responds}: The next quantifier is $\exists y \in \R$. We must now produce a \emph{chosen witness}. Because $y$ comes \emph{after} $x$ in the quantifier order, our choice of $y$ is allowed to depend on $x$:
\begin{quote}
``Choose $y = -x$.''
\end{quote}
\item \textbf{Verify the requirements}: We verify two things: first, that $-x \in \R$ (it is, since the negative of a real number is a real number), and second, that
\[
x + y = x + (-x) = 0.
\]
\item \textbf{Conclude and close scope}: Both conditions are satisfied, so the statement is proved, and the scopes of $x$ and $y$ close.
\end{enumerate}

Now reverse the quantifiers:
\[
\exists y \in \R \text{ such that } \forall x \in \R, \, x + y = 0.
\]
Here, $y$ must be chosen \emph{first}, independently of $x$. Any proposed value of $y$ (say $y = 0$) fails for $x = 1$, since $1 + 0 = 1 \neq 0$. In fact, for any fixed real number $y$, the adversary can answer with $x = -y + 1$, and then
\[
x + y = (-y + 1) + y = 1 \neq 0.
\]
Reversing the quantifier order changes the dependence between the variables. Figure~\ref{03:fig-move-order} lays out the two games move by move, and Figure~\ref{03:fig-line-picture} shows the same contrast as a graph.

\begin{trapbox}
Never define an existential witness using a variable that has not yet entered scope. In the statement $\forall x \, \exists y$, your formula for $y$ may use $x$. In $\exists y \, \forall x$, your formula for $y$ must be a constant chosen before $x$ is announced. Writing ``Choose $y = -x$'' before $\forall x$ has appeared is a forward reference: it uses a name before that name has been introduced.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{The bridge to quantifiers: who moves first?}

Scoping rules tell us where a variable is visible and what we may do with it. But theorems rarely involve a single variable in isolation. They involve chains of variables that depend on one another.

When you prove a theorem in analysis or algebra, you often handle several variables at once: a fixed set, an arbitrary element, a chosen witness, and a bound index. Who chooses which letter, and which choices may depend on earlier ones?

In Chapter~4 (\emph{Quantifiers: The Game of For Every and There Exists}), we examine how quantifiers ($\forall$ and $\exists$) govern these choices as a game, and why their order matters.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Role Identification (Warm-up):} In the statement
\[
\forall n \in \Z, \, \exists m \in \Z \text{ such that } n = 2m + 1,
\]
identify the roles of $n$ and $m$. Is the statement true or false? Even a false statement has variables with well-defined roles.

\item \textbf{Multi-Variable Parsing (Calculus):} In the definition of continuity of a function $f \colon \R \to \R$ at a point $c$ (here $f \colon \R \to \R$ means $f$ takes real numbers to real numbers, $\eps$ and $\delta$ are the Greek letters epsilon and delta, and $|x - c|$ is the distance between $x$ and $c$):
\begin{quote}
``For every $\eps > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x \in \R$, if $|x - c| < \delta$, then $|f(x) - f(c)| < \eps$,''
\end{quote}
identify the exact role of each letter: $c$, $\eps$, $\delta$, and $x$. Which variable is a fixed constant, which are arbitrary universals, and which is a chosen witness? On which prior variables is the formula for $\delta$ permitted to depend?

\item \textbf{Bound Dummies and Evaluation:} In the expression
\[
L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{k}{n^2},
\]
answer the following.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Which variables are bound dummies, and what binding operator captures each of them?
\item Compute the value of the expression by first using
\[
\sum_{k=1}^n k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}
\]
and then evaluating the limit as $n \to \infty$. Does any variable survive into the final number?
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Diagnosing Proof Bugs (Illegal Specialization):} Explain the exact error in the following pseudo-proof:
\begin{quote}
\itshape Claim: Every integer is even.\\
False proof: Let $n \in \Z$ be arbitrary. Choose $n = 4$. Since $4 = 2 \cdot 2$, $n$ is even. Therefore every integer is even.
\end{quote}
Which role permission was violated? Why does setting $n = 4$ destroy the universal validity of the proof?

\item \textbf{Diagnosing Proof Bugs (Forward Referencing):} Explain what is wrong with the following proof attempt:
\begin{quote}
\itshape Claim: $\exists y \in \R \text{ such that } \forall x \in \R, \, x + y = x$.\\
False proof: Let $x \in \R$ be arbitrary. Choose $y = x - x = 0$. Since $x + 0 = x$, the number $y$ works for all $x$. Therefore such a $y$ exists.
\end{quote}
Although the claim is true (the number $0$ works for all $x$), why is this proof invalid as written? (Look at the quantifier order and the scope-opening line.)

\item \textbf{Variable Capture in Calculus:} Consider the function
\[
F(x) = \int_0^x (x - t) \, dt.
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Evaluate $F(x)$ by integrating with respect to $t$.
\item A student reasons: ``Because $t$ is a dummy variable, I can rename it to $x$. Then the integrand becomes $(x - x) = 0$, so $\int_0^x 0 \, dx = 0$.'' Explain why this reasoning is wrong, identifying the exact collision between a free variable and a bound variable.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Proof Lab (Writing Scopes Explicitly):} Write a rigorous, step-by-step proof of the claim:
\[
\forall x \in \R, \, (x > 3 \implies x^2 - 9 > 0).
\]
For each line of your proof, explicitly state whether you are opening a scope, introducing an arbitrary element, assuming a hypothesis, or closing a scope.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{walkbox}
When reading an advanced textbook, pause at each variable. Ask yourself: ``Is this a fixed parameter, an arbitrary element of a set, a chosen witness satisfying an existential claim, or a bound index local to an expression?''
\end{walkbox}
