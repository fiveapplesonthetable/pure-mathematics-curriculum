\section{Variables Have Roles: Fixed, Arbitrary, Chosen, Bound}
\chapterline

In elementary algebra, a variable is usually introduced as an ``unknown number'' waiting to be solved. You manipulate an equation like $3x + 4 = 19$, isolate $x$, and conclude that $x = 5$.

\begin{historybox}
For most of human history, even elementary algebra was purely rhetorical. Medieval mathematicians had no symbols for unknowns or coefficients; every problem was written out verbally using terms like \emph{cosa} (``the thing'') and \emph{census} (``the square''). In 1591, the French mathematician Fran\c{c}ois Vi\`ete revolutionized the subject in \emph{In artem analyticem isagoge} by systematically using letters: vowels ($A, E, I$) for unknowns and consonants ($B, C, D$) for known parameters. For the first time, mathematicians could manipulate entire families of equations at once.

Yet introducing letters created a stubborn misconception that lasted for two centuries. In the 1700s, Euler, d'Alembert, and the Bernoullis often treated variables as physical quantities that ``flow with time''---an intuition inherited from Isaac Newton's calculus of fluxions. This temporal metaphor caused serious errors in multivariable calculus, where mathematicians frequently treated an arbitrary parameter as a fixed constant in one step and varied it in the next, accidentally deducing contradictory identities.

Between 1889 and 1895, Giuseppe Peano dismantled this confusion by formalizing variable binding and lexical scope. A variable is neither an unknown number nor a moving physical particle; it is a scoped placeholder governed by strict visibility permissions. Distinguishing the four hats a variable wears creates leak-proof proofs, preventing variable capture and illegal substitutions. Yet scoping alone does not tell us who moves first when multiple variables interact. In Chapter~4 (\emph{Quantifiers: The Game of For Every and There Exists}), we see how quantifiers ($\forall, \exists$) turn variable scoping into a dynamic strategic game.
\end{historybox}

In proof-based mathematics, treating every variable as an unknown number to solve will quickly lead to confusion. Letters in a proof are not uniform; they perform radically different jobs, carry distinct permissions, and live inside specific boundaries of visibility. Conflating these roles is the single most common cause of invalid proofs.

To read and write proofs with confidence, you need to recognize which of the four fundamental hats a variable is wearing at any given sentence.

\subsection{Lexical scope: mathematical proofs as programs}

If you have written software, you are already comfortable with variable scope. A local variable declared inside a function body ceases to exist once that function returns. Trying to access that variable outside the curly braces generates a compiler error.

Mathematical proofs obey identical scoping disciplines:

When a proof says ``Let $x \in \R$ be arbitrary,'' it opens a local scope block. Inside that block, $x$ is treated as an immutable object about which nothing is known except that $x \in \R$. Once you show that the desired property holds for that $x$, the universal statement $\forall x \in \R, \ldots$ is established, and the scope block closes. You cannot subsequently refer to ``that $x$'' in later arguments.

\subsection{Free versus bound variables}

Look at the following three mathematical expressions:
\[
\text{(A)}\quad \sum_{i=1}^n i^2, \qquad\qquad \text{(B)}\quad \int_0^1 x^2 \, dx, \qquad\qquad \text{(C)}\quad x^2 + y^2 = 1.
\]

In expression (A), the index $i$ is \emph{bound} by the summation. The letter $n$, however, is \emph{free}: the numerical value of the sum depends directly on what $n$ is. If you change $n$ from $3$ to $4$, the sum changes from $14$ to $30$. But if you replace every occurrence of $i$ with $j$, getting $\sum_{j=1}^n j^2$, the value is completely unchanged. The index $i$ is merely a dummy counter.

In expression (B), the variable $x$ is bound by the integral. The expression evaluates to the concrete number $1/3$. There is no $x$ remaining in the answer.

In expression (C), both $x$ and $y$ are free. Without additional context, the equation does not assert a truth; it defines a geometric relationship (a circle) satisfied by certain pairs of real numbers.

\subsection{A worked example of quantifier and role parsing}

Consider the statement:
\[
\forall x \in \R, \, \exists y \in \R \text{ such that } x + y = 0.
\]

Let us trace the roles and permissions line by line:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The universal player moves first}: The statement begins with $\forall x \in \R$. Therefore, in our proof, we must begin by accepting an \emph{arbitrary} real number:
\begin{quote}
``Let $x \in \R$ be arbitrary.''
\end{quote}
We cannot choose $x = 0$ or $x = 5$. We must leave $x$ completely general.
\item \textbf{The existential player responds}: The next quantifier is $\exists y \in \R$. We must now produce a \emph{chosen witness}. Because $y$ comes \emph{after} $x$ in the quantifier order, our choice of $y$ is legally permitted to depend on $x$:
\begin{quote}
``Choose $y = -x$.''
\end{quote}
\item \textbf{Verify the requirements}: We verify two things: first, that $-x \in \R$ (it is), and second, that $x + y = x + (-x) = 0$.
\item \textbf{Conclude and close scope}: Both conditions are satisfied, so the statement is proved.
\end{enumerate}

Now observe what happens if the quantifiers are reversed:
\[
\exists y \in \R \text{ such that } \forall x \in \R, \, x + y = 0.
\]
Here, $y$ must be chosen \emph{first}, before $x$ is revealed! Any candidate $y$ you propose (say $y = 0$) can be immediately defeated by the adversary choosing $x = 1$, since $1 + 0 = 1 \neq 0$. Reversing the order switches who plays first.

\begin{trapbox}
Never define an existential witness using a variable that has not yet been introduced. In the statement $\forall x \, \exists y$, your formula for $y$ may use $x$. In $\exists y \, \forall x$, your formula for $y$ must be an absolute constant independent of $x$.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{What role discipline unlocked---and where quantifiers enter the game}

Classifying variables into their four operational hats brings the discipline of modern software engineering into mathematical proof. Just as a compiler halts when a program tries to access a private variable outside its local block, mathematical rigor rejects any proof that attempts to treat an arbitrary universal input as a chosen constant or leak a bound dummy index into an outer paragraph.

Yet scoping discipline alone is only half the battle. In theorems involving multiple variables, what determines who chooses first? When can a chosen witness depend on an input, and when is it required to work uniformly for all inputs simultaneously? In Chapter~4 (\emph{Quantifiers: The Game of For Every and There Exists}), we turn variable scoping into an interactive game of strategy, discovering how the precise ordering of $\forall$ and $\exists$ resolved one of the greatest crises in the history of calculus.

\begin{walkbox}
When reading an advanced textbook, pause at every variable name. Ask yourself: ``Is this an input I was given, an adversary's move, a witness I built, or a local counter that will vanish at the end of the sentence?''
\end{walkbox}
