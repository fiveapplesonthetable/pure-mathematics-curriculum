\section{Second Pass: Algebra as Lawful Manipulation}
\chapterline

In Chapter~35, we saw how real analysis brings discipline to approximations and limits, translating vague ideas of ``approaching'' into an adversarial contract of quantifiers. Now, in this second pass through algebra, we encounter an entirely different discipline: lawful manipulation.

In elementary algebra, the rules of arithmetic are often taken as given: terms are added, factors commute, and equations are solved by dividing by nonzero quantities. 

Abstract algebra begins when you realize that those familiar properties are specific to particular number systems, such as $\Q$ and $\R$. When you manipulate geometric transformations, multiply matrices, compose permutations, or work in modular arithmetic, commutativity and division do not always hold. Rotating a book and then flipping it does not yield the same result as flipping first and rotating second. In general mathematical structures, algebraic manipulation is governed by explicitly stated axioms.

\begin{historybox}
For centuries, algebra was centered on finding explicit numerical formulas for roots of polynomials---such as the quadratic, cubic, and quartic formulas. After finding formulas for degrees up to 4, mathematicians sought general radical formulas for degree 5 polynomials without success.

The perspective changed in 1832 when \'Evariste Galois connected the solvability of polynomial equations to the symmetries of their roots. This led to the modern concept of a \emph{group}: a set equipped with an associative binary operation, an identity element, and inverses.

In the 1920s at G\"ottingen and Hamburg, Emmy Noether and Emil Artin showed that abstract structures explain calculation: rings (systems where you can add and multiply, like $\Z$), fields (rings where you can divide, like $\Q$), and ideals (subsets that absorb multiplication, like even numbers inside $\Z$). In 1930, Bartel Leendert van der Waerden published \emph{Moderne Algebra}, synthesizing their lectures and turning mathematics toward the axiomatic study of groups, rings, and fields.

This structural approach culminated in the late twentieth century with the \emph{Classification of Finite Simple Groups}. Led by Daniel Gorenstein, John Thompson, Michael Aschbacher, and hundreds of others, this decades-long effort classified all atomic building blocks of symmetry---groups that have no smaller non-trivial quotient groups, much like prime numbers have no non-trivial factors. They sorted every finite simple group into cyclic groups of prime order, alternating groups of even permutations, groups of Lie type (symmetries of matrix spaces), and 26 ``sporadic'' groups ending with the enormous Monster group.

Yet a foundational question remains open today: the \emph{Inverse Galois Problem}. Posed in the nineteenth century, it asks whether every finite group appears as the Galois group of some polynomial with rational coefficients. We can construct polynomials for many families of groups, but after nearly two centuries, nobody knows if every finite symmetry group can be realized over the rationals.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Why You Cannot ``Divide'': Left versus Right Inverses}

A \textbf{group} $(G, \cdot)$ is a set $G$ equipped with an operation $\cdot$ satisfying three fundamental rules:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Associativity}: grouping does not change the result: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$ for all $a, b, c \in G$.
  \item \textbf{Identity}: there exists a neutral element $e \in G$ that leaves every element unchanged: $e \cdot g = g \cdot e = g$ for all $g \in G$.
  \item \textbf{Inverses}: every element $g \in G$ has a unique partner $g^{-1} \in G$ that undoes it: $g \cdot g^{-1} = g^{-1} \cdot g = e$.
\end{enumerate}

Notice what is missing from this list: \emph{commutativity}. In a general group, $a \cdot b$ and $b \cdot a$ can be entirely different objects. A group where the order of multiplication does not matter is called \emph{abelian} (named after Niels Henrik Abel); all other groups are \textbf{non-commutative}.

To see non-commutativity in physical space, consider the symmetries of a square (the dihedral group $D_4$). The square has eight symmetries: four rotations ($0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ$) and four reflections. Let $r$ be a clockwise rotation by $90^\circ$, and let $s$ be reflection across the vertical midline.

What happens if you combine them? In function composition notation, $s \circ r$ means: apply rotation $r$ first, then apply reflection $s$ second. Conversely, $r \circ s$ means: apply reflection $s$ first, then rotate by $r$.

As Figure~\ref{36:fig-d4-noncommutative} demonstrates, the outcomes are completely different. A reflection reverses the orientation of the plane: looking into a mirror turns a clockwise spin into a counterclockwise spin. Therefore, rotating clockwise and then reflecting ($s \circ r$) yields the same geometric result as reflecting first and then rotating \emph{counterclockwise} ($r^{-1} \circ s$). Since rotating counterclockwise by $90^\circ$ is three clockwise quarter-turns ($r^3$), we have:
\[
s \circ r = r^{-1} \circ s = r^3 \circ s \;\neq\; r \circ s.
\]
Because order matters, the high school phrase ``divide by $a$'' is meaningless. Which side would you divide from? 

To isolate $x$ in the equation $a \cdot x = b$, you must multiply by $a^{-1}$ on the \textbf{left}:
\[
a^{-1}(ax) = a^{-1}b \implies (a^{-1}a)x = a^{-1}b \implies ex = a^{-1}b \implies x = a^{-1}b.
\]
If you had multiplied on the right, you would obtain $(ax)a^{-1} = ba^{-1}$, which simplifies only to $axa^{-1} = ba^{-1}$. Because $x$ and $a^{-1}$ cannot swap places, $x$ remains trapped in the middle. (Similarly, to solve $x \cdot a = b$, you must multiply on the right by $a^{-1}$.) In noncommutative algebra, the side on which an inverse is applied must be tracked carefully.

\subsection{Gordan's ``Theology'' and Pitfalls of Naive Cancellation}

The shift from explicit formulas to abstract axiomatic algebra encountered fierce resistance and conceptual pitfalls:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gordan's Cry of ``Theology!''}: In the late nineteenth century, Paul Gordan was celebrated as the ``King of Invariants'' for his legendary skill in calculating explicit generating systems for polynomial invariants---quantities that remain unchanged when geometric coordinates are rotated or sheared. In 1890, David Hilbert solved the central open problem of invariant theory: he proved that every such system has a finite generating set (the Hilbert Basis Theorem). But Hilbert's proof was completely non-constructive. He showed that an infinite ascending chain of polynomial ideals must terminate, without producing a single explicit formula. Gordan famously cried: \emph{``Das ist nicht Mathematik. Das ist Theologie!''} (This is not mathematics; it is theology!). Yet within a decade, Gordan embraced Hilbert's and Noether's structural approach, conceding that abstract axioms revealed truths that brute computation could never touch.
  \item \textbf{The Zero-Divisor Cancellation Trap}: In high school arithmetic, if $ab = 0$, you instantly conclude $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. In abstract rings (systems with addition and multiplication, like clock arithmetic), this intuition fails completely. In $\Z_6$ (integers modulo 6), we have $2 \cdot 3 \equiv 6 \equiv 0 \pmod 6$, even though neither $2$ nor $3$ is zero! Elements that multiply to produce zero are called \emph{zero divisors}.

  The trap appears when students try to cancel common factors. Suppose $2x \equiv 2y \pmod 6$. Subtracting gives $2(x - y) \equiv 0 \pmod 6$. If you naively ``divide by $2$,'' you would conclude $x \equiv y \pmod 6$. But if $x = 4$ and $y = 1$, we have $2 \cdot 4 = 8 \equiv 2$ and $2 \cdot 1 = 2$. Both products equal $2$, yet $4 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 6$! You cannot cancel $2$ because $2$ is a zero divisor. Cancellation $ab = ac \implies b = c$ is lawful only in \emph{integral domains} (rings with no non-zero zero divisors, like $\Z$) and \emph{fields} (rings where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse, like $\Q$ and $\R$).
  \item \textbf{The Non-Normal Quotient Collapse}: A \emph{subgroup} $H \le G$ is a subset that forms a group in its own right, and a \emph{coset} $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$ is a shifted copy of $H$. Students often assume that for any subgroup $H \le G$, the set of cosets $G/H$ forms a quotient group with multiplication $(aH)(bH) = (ab)H$. But if $H$ is not a \emph{normal} subgroup, coset multiplication is not even a well-defined function!

  To see this breakdown in action, take the symmetric group $S_3$ (all six permutations of $\{1, 2, 3\}$), and let $H = \{e, (1\;2)\}$ be the two-element subgroup swapping 1 and 2. Consider the coset $C = (2\;3)H = \{(2\;3), (1\;3\;2)\}$. If coset multiplication were legitimate, the square $C \cdot C$ would be a unique coset. But let us compute using different representatives of the same coset:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Choosing $(2\;3) \in C$: $(2\;3)(2\;3) = e \in H$, giving the coset $H$.
    \item Choosing $(1\;3\;2) \in C$: $(1\;3\;2)(1\;3\;2) = (1\;2\;3) \notin H$, giving the coset $(1\;2\;3)H$.
  \end{itemize}
  Since $H \neq (1\;2\;3)H$, the product $C \cdot C$ gives two contradictory results depending on the representative. Without normality, coset multiplication is not well-defined.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Kernel: Measuring Lost Information}

When you move between algebraic systems, you need maps that respect their internal rules. A \textbf{homomorphism} is a structure-preserving function between groups: a map $\phi: G \to H$ satisfying
\[
\phi(a \cdot b) = \phi(a) \cdot \phi(b) \quad \text{for all } a, b \in G.
\]
A homomorphism preserves algebraic operations: multiplying two elements in $G$ and then applying $\phi$ yields the same result as applying $\phi$ first and multiplying the images in $H$. A homomorphism can collapse elements together, but it respects products everywhere.

Because $\phi$ respects multiplication, it automatically maps the identity to the identity:
\[
\phi(e_G) = \phi(e_G \cdot e_G) = \phi(e_G) \cdot \phi(e_G) \implies \phi(e_G) = e_H,
\]
and preserves inverses: $\phi(g^{-1}) = \phi(g)^{-1}$.

The elements in $G$ that map to the target identity $e_H \in H$ form the \textbf{kernel}:
\[
\ker(\phi) = \{g \in G : \phi(g) = e_H\}.
\]
The kernel measures the \emph{information lost} by the mapping. If $\ker(\phi) = \{e_G\}$, nothing is collapsed except the identity itself, which means $\phi$ is injective (one-to-one) and $G$ is embedded faithfully inside $H$. But when the kernel contains non-identity elements, $\phi$ collapses cosets of elements into single image points.

The kernel is never an arbitrary subset of $G$. It is always a subgroup, and it possesses a central property called \textbf{normality}.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check:} Consider the determinant map $\det: \mathrm{GL}_2(\R) \to \R^\times$ sending invertible $2 \times 2$ matrices with real entries to non-zero real numbers. The group operation on $\mathrm{GL}_2(\R)$ is matrix multiplication, and the operation on $\R^\times$ is ordinary multiplication.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Why is $\det$ a group homomorphism?
  \item What is the identity element of the target group $(\R^\times, \cdot)$?
  \item What is the kernel $\ker(\det)$?
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Answer:} (a) The determinant product formula guarantees $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$. (b) The identity in $(\R^\times, \cdot)$ is the number $1$. (c) The kernel consists of all matrices with determinant $1$:
\[
\ker(\det) = \{A \in \mathrm{GL}_2(\R) : \det(A) = 1\} = \mathrm{SL}_2(\R).
\]
This is the Special Linear Group, representing area-preserving linear transformations of the plane.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Normal Subgroups: Why Coset Multiplication Must Work}

If we want to form a quotient group $G/N$ whose elements are the cosets $gN$, we need a rule to multiply them:
\[
(aN) \cdot (bN) \stackrel{?}{=} (ab)N.
\]
This looks deceptively simple, but there is an immediate danger: this definition refers to specific representatives $a$ and $b$. What if we picked different elements from the exact same cosets, say $a' \in aN$ and $b' \in bN$? Would the product $(a'b')N$ land in the identical coset $(ab)N$?

In general, \textbf{no}! Coset multiplication is well-defined if and only if the subgroup $N$ is \textbf{normal}.

To see why, suppose $a' = an_1$ and $b' = bn_2$ for some elements $n_1, n_2 \in N$. We need their product $a'b'$ to belong to the coset $(ab)N$. Let us expand the product:
\[
a'b' = (an_1)(bn_2) = a(n_1b)n_2.
\]
We want to express this in the form $(ab)n_3$ for some $n_3 \in N$. Factoring out $ab$ on the left gives:
\[
a(n_1b)n_2 = ab(b^{-1}n_1b)n_2.
\]
Since $n_2 \in N$, this product belongs to $(ab)N$ if and only if the conjugated element $b^{-1}n_1b$ belongs to $N$. Because $b$ was an arbitrary element of $G$ and $n_1$ was an arbitrary element of $N$, coset multiplication works if and only if conjugation by any element of $G$ maps every element of $N$ into $N$.

\begin{formalbox}
A subgroup $N \le G$ is \textbf{normal} (written $N \triangleleft G$) if it is invariant under conjugation by every element of $G$:
\[
gNg^{-1} = N \quad \text{for all } g \in G.
\]
Equivalently, for every $n \in N$ and $g \in G$, the element $gng^{-1} \in N$. In terms of cosets, normality means that left cosets and right cosets coincide: $gN = Ng$ for all $g \in G$.
\end{formalbox}

Every kernel is automatically a normal subgroup. If $n \in \ker(\phi)$ and $g \in G$, we test whether $gng^{-1}$ is in the kernel by applying $\phi$:
\[
\phi(gng^{-1}) = \phi(g)\phi(n)\phi(g)^{-1} = \phi(g) e_H \phi(g)^{-1} = \phi(g)\phi(g)^{-1} = e_H.
\]
Therefore $gng^{-1} \in \ker(\phi)$, so $\ker(\phi) \triangleleft G$.

With normality secured, the \textbf{First Isomorphism Theorem for groups} crystallizes:
\[
G/\ker(\phi) \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}(\phi).
\]
This theorem establishes the fundamental relationship between quotient groups and homomorphisms:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Every homomorphism $\phi$ partitions $G$ into the cosets of its kernel.
  \item The quotient group $G/\ker(\phi)$ is isomorphic to the image subgroup $\operatorname{im}(\phi) \le H$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What Abstract Algebra Unlocks---and Its Limits}

The structural perspective championed by Noether and van der Waerden led to major developments in modern mathematics and theoretical physics:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Galois Theory and Insolvability}: Galois theory showed that the unsolvability of the general quintic polynomial ($x^5 - 4x + 1 = 0$) by radicals is not a failure of algebraic ingenuity, but a structural barrier: the alternating group $A_5$ of even permutations of five roots is simple and non-abelian, so its symmetry cannot be broken down into abelian sub-stages. It also resolved centuries-old Greek geometry problems, proving that trisecting an arbitrary angle or squaring the circle with straightedge and compass is impossible.
  \item \textbf{The Symmetry Language of Physics}: In physics, continuous symmetries are governed by \emph{Lie groups} (smooth groups whose elements form curved geometric spaces). Emmy Noether's 1915 theorem proved that every continuous symmetry of a physical system yields a conservation law: time translation yields conservation of energy, spatial translation yields conservation of momentum, and rotational symmetry yields conservation of angular momentum. In quantum field theory, fundamental particles are classified as irreducible representations of the Lie group $\mathrm{SU}(3) \times \mathrm{SU}(2) \times \mathrm{U}(1)$.
  \item \textbf{Modern Cryptography}: Public-key cryptosystems (RSA, Diffie--Hellman key exchange, and Elliptic Curve Cryptography) rely entirely on the asymmetric computational difficulty of finite group operations. Computing powers $g^k$ using repeated squaring takes milliseconds, but recovering the exponent $k$ from $g^k$ (the discrete logarithm problem) is computationally intractable for large groups.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
Yet algebra has clear limitations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Algorithmic Undecidability (The Word Problem)}: Although group axioms are concise, computing with them can be provably impossible. The Novikov--Boone theorem (1955) proved that there exists a finitely presented group for which no algorithm can determine whether an arbitrary sequence of generators simplifies to the identity element. Simple declared rules can harbor undecidable behavior.
  \item \textbf{No Built-In Notion of Distance or Continuity}: Purely algebraic axioms talk only about exact equality ($a \cdot b = c$). On their own, they have no vocabulary for ``close,'' ``approaching,'' or ``continuous''---distinguishing a discrete group from a smooth sphere or torus of symmetries requires combining algebra with topology.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mental Warm-Up: Cancellation and Inverses.}
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item In the additive group $(\Z_{12}, +)$, solve the equation $4 + x \equiv 1 \pmod{12}$ by adding the additive inverse of $4$.
    \item In the multiplicative system $(\Z_{12}, \cdot)$, find all solutions $x \in \{0, 1, \dots, 11\}$ to the equation $3x \equiv 6 \pmod{12}$. Explain why you cannot ``divide both sides by $3$'' to obtain a unique solution.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Socks-and-Shoes Property.} In any group $G$, prove that for all $a, b \in G$,
  \[
  (ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}.
  \]
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Verify algebraically that $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1}) = e$ and $(b^{-1}a^{-1})(ab) = e$, identifying where associativity is used.
    \item Explain why reversing the composition of successive actions (such as putting on socks then shoes) illustrates why the order of inverses must reverse: $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Subgroups of Index 2 Are Always Normal.} Let $G$ be a group and let $H \le G$ be a subgroup of index $2$, meaning there are exactly two left cosets: $H$ and $G \setminus H$.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item What are the two right cosets of $H$ in $G$?
    \item For any $g \in G$, show that the left coset $gH$ equals the right coset $Hg$ by considering two cases: $g \in H$ and $g \notin H$.
    \item Conclude that $H \triangleleft G$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Determinant Homomorphism and $\mathrm{SL}_n(\R)$.} Consider the determinant mapping $\det: \mathrm{GL}_n(\R) \to \R^\times$ on invertible $n \times n$ real matrices.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Prove that $\det$ is surjective: for any non-zero real number $r \in \R^\times$, exhibit an invertible $n \times n$ matrix with determinant $r$.
    \item Apply the First Isomorphism Theorem to deduce that $\mathrm{GL}_n(\R)/\mathrm{SL}_n(\R) \cong \R^\times$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Alternating Group via the First Isomorphism Theorem.} Consider the sign homomorphism $\operatorname{sgn}: S_n \to \{+1, -1\}$ on the symmetric group of permutations, which sends each even permutation to $+1$ and each odd permutation to $-1$.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item What is the kernel $\ker(\operatorname{sgn})$? (This is called the alternating group $A_n$.)
    \item What is the size $|A_n|$ relative to $|S_n| = n!$?
    \item Apply the First Isomorphism Theorem to deduce that $S_n/A_n \cong \Z_2$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
