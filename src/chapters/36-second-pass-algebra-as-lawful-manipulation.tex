\section{Second Pass: Algebra as Lawful Manipulation}
\chapterline

In Chapter~35, real analysis turned the vague idea of ``approaching'' into a contract written with quantifiers. This chapter asks a different question about algebra: which manipulations of symbols are allowed, and why?

In school algebra, the rules of arithmetic are taken as given: terms are added, factors \emph{commute} (swap places, $ab = ba$), and equations are solved by dividing by nonzero quantities.

Abstract algebra begins when you realize that those familiar properties belong to particular number systems, such as $\Q$ and $\R$. When you combine geometric transformations, multiply matrices, compose permutations, or work in modular arithmetic, commutativity and division do not always hold. Rotating a book and then flipping it does not give the same result as flipping first and rotating second. So in a general structure, a manipulation is allowed only if it follows from rules stated in advance, called \emph{axioms}.

\begin{historybox}
For centuries, algebra was centered on finding explicit numerical formulas for roots of polynomials---such as the quadratic, cubic, and quartic formulas. After finding formulas for degrees up to 4, mathematicians sought general radical formulas for degree 5 polynomials without success.

The perspective changed in 1832 when \'Evariste Galois connected the solvability of polynomial equations to the symmetries of their roots. This led to the modern concept of a \emph{group}: a set with a way of combining two elements, obeying the three rules listed in the next section.

In the 1920s at G\"ottingen and Hamburg, Emmy Noether and Emil Artin showed that abstract structures explain calculation: rings (systems where you can add and multiply, like $\Z$), fields (rings where you can divide, like $\Q$), and ideals (subsets that absorb multiplication, like even numbers inside $\Z$), all introduced in Chapter~24. In 1930, Bartel Leendert van der Waerden published \emph{Moderne Algebra}, synthesizing their lectures and turning mathematics toward the axiomatic study of groups, rings, and fields.

This structural approach led, in the late twentieth century, to the \emph{Classification of Finite Simple Groups}. Led by Daniel Gorenstein, John Thompson, Michael Aschbacher, and hundreds of others, this decades-long effort listed all the basic building blocks of finite symmetry: the \emph{simple} groups, which cannot be broken into smaller pieces (they have no quotient groups other than themselves and the one-element group), much as prime numbers have no factors other than themselves and $1$. Every finite simple group turned out to be a cyclic group of prime order, an alternating group (Chapter~21), a group of Lie type (built from matrices), or one of 26 ``sporadic'' exceptions, the largest being the Monster group.

A foundational question remains open today: the \emph{Inverse Galois Problem}. Posed in the nineteenth century, it asks whether every finite group appears as the symmetry group of the roots (the \emph{Galois group}) of some polynomial with rational coefficients. We can construct polynomials for many families of groups, but after nearly two centuries, nobody knows if every finite symmetry group can be realized over the rationals.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Why You Cannot ``Divide'': Left versus Right Inverses}

A \textbf{group} $(G, \cdot)$ is a set $G$ with an operation $\cdot$ that combines any two elements $a, b \in G$ into an element $a \cdot b \in G$, satisfying three rules:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Associativity}: grouping does not change the result: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$ for all $a, b, c \in G$.
  \item \textbf{Identity}: there exists a neutral element $e \in G$ that leaves every element unchanged: $e \cdot g = g \cdot e = g$ for all $g \in G$.
  \item \textbf{Inverses}: every element $g \in G$ has a partner $g^{-1} \in G$ that undoes it: $g \cdot g^{-1} = g^{-1} \cdot g = e$. (One can prove from the rules that this partner is unique.)
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Commutativity} is missing from this list. In a general group, $a \cdot b$ and $b \cdot a$ can be different elements. A group where the order of multiplication does not matter is called \emph{abelian} (named after Niels Henrik Abel); all other groups are \textbf{non-commutative}.

To see non-commutativity in physical space, consider the symmetries of a square. Its symmetry group is called the \emph{dihedral group} $D_4$ (``dihedral'' groups are the symmetry groups of regular polygons; Chapter~21 studied $D_3$, the triangle's). The square has eight symmetries: four rotations ($0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ$) and four reflections. Let $r$ be a clockwise rotation by $90^\circ$, and let $s$ be reflection across the vertical midline.

What happens if you combine them? In function composition notation, $s \circ r$ means: apply rotation $r$ first, then apply reflection $s$ second. Conversely, $r \circ s$ means: apply reflection $s$ first, then rotate by $r$.

Figure~\ref{36:fig-d4-noncommutative} shows that the two orders give different results. A reflection reverses the orientation of the plane: looking into a mirror turns a clockwise spin into a counterclockwise spin. Therefore, rotating clockwise and then reflecting ($s \circ r$) yields the same geometric result as reflecting first and then rotating \emph{counterclockwise} ($r^{-1} \circ s$). Since rotating counterclockwise by $90^\circ$ is three clockwise quarter-turns ($r^3$), we have:
\[
s \circ r = r^{-1} \circ s = r^3 \circ s \;\neq\; r \circ s.
\]
Because order matters, the school phrase ``divide by $a$'' is ambiguous. Which side would you divide from?

To isolate $x$ in the equation $a \cdot x = b$, you must multiply by $a^{-1}$ on the \textbf{left}:
\begin{align*}
a^{-1}(ax) &= a^{-1}b && \text{(multiply both sides on the left by } a^{-1}\text{)}\\
(a^{-1}a)x &= a^{-1}b && \text{(associativity)}\\
ex &= a^{-1}b && \text{(inverse rule: } a^{-1}a = e\text{)}\\
x &= a^{-1}b. && \text{(identity rule)}
\end{align*}
If you had multiplied on the right, you would obtain $(ax)a^{-1} = ba^{-1}$, which simplifies only to $axa^{-1} = ba^{-1}$. Because $x$ and $a^{-1}$ cannot swap places, $x$ remains trapped in the middle. (Similarly, to solve $x \cdot a = b$, you must multiply on the right by $a^{-1}$.) In noncommutative algebra, the side on which an inverse is applied must be tracked carefully.

\subsection{Gordan's ``Theology'' and Pitfalls of Naive Cancellation}

The shift from explicit formulas to axiomatic algebra met resistance, and it exposes some common mistakes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gordan's Cry of ``Theology!''}: In the late nineteenth century, Paul Gordan was known as the ``King of Invariants'' for his skill at computing \emph{polynomial invariants}: polynomial expressions in the coefficients of a polynomial that stay unchanged when its coordinates are rotated or sheared. The goal was to find a finite \emph{generating set}: a list of invariants from which every other invariant can be built using sums and products. In 1890, David Hilbert proved that such a finite generating set always exists (using his Basis Theorem). But Hilbert's proof was \emph{non-constructive}: it showed the list must exist without saying how to find it. Its key step shows that a chain of ever-larger ideals of polynomials cannot keep growing forever, and it produces no explicit formula. Gordan reportedly exclaimed: \emph{``Das ist nicht Mathematik. Das ist Theologie!''} (``This is not mathematics; it is theology!''). Hilbert's methods soon won out, and Gordan himself later conceded, ``I have convinced myself that theology also has its merits.''
  \item \textbf{The Zero-Divisor Cancellation Trap}: In school arithmetic, if $ab = 0$, you conclude $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. In abstract rings (systems with addition and multiplication, like clock arithmetic), this fails. In $\Z_6$ (integers modulo 6), we have $2 \cdot 3 \equiv 6 \equiv 0 \pmod 6$, even though neither $2$ nor $3$ is zero. A non-zero element $a$ is called a \emph{zero divisor} if $ab = 0$ for some non-zero element $b$; here both $2$ and $3$ are zero divisors in $\Z_6$.

  The trap appears when you try to cancel common factors. Suppose $2x \equiv 2y \pmod 6$. Subtracting gives $2(x - y) \equiv 0 \pmod 6$. If you ``divide by $2$,'' you would conclude $x \equiv y \pmod 6$. But take $x = 4$ and $y = 1$:
  \[
  2 \cdot 4 = 8 \equiv 2 \pmod 6 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 2 \cdot 1 = 2.
  \]
  Both products equal $2$, yet $4 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 6$. You cannot cancel $2$ because $2$ is a zero divisor. Figure~\ref{36:fig-z6-doubling} draws the map $x \mapsto 2x$ on $\Z_6$: it sends two different inputs to each of $0$, $2$ and $4$, so the value of $2x$ does not determine $x$. Cancellation $ab = ac \implies b = c$ is lawful only in \emph{integral domains} (rings with no nontrivial zero divisors, like $\Z$) and \emph{fields} (rings where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse, like $\Q$ and $\R$).
  \item \textbf{The Non-Normal Quotient Collapse}: A \emph{subgroup} $H \le G$ is a subset that forms a group in its own right under the same operation, and a \emph{coset} $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$ is a shifted copy of $H$. It is tempting to assume that for any subgroup $H \le G$, the set of cosets $G/H$ forms a quotient group with multiplication $(aH)(bH) = (ab)H$. But unless $H$ has a special property called \emph{normality} (defined later in this chapter), this rule is not even a well-defined function: the answer can depend on which representatives $a$ and $b$ you pick.

  For example, take the \emph{symmetric group} $S_3$: the group of all six permutations of $\{1, 2, 3\}$, with composition as the operation. We write permutations in \emph{cycle notation}: $(1\;2)$ swaps $1$ and $2$ and leaves $3$ alone; $(1\;3\;2)$ sends $1 \mapsto 3$, $3 \mapsto 2$, and $2 \mapsto 1$; and $e$ is the permutation that moves nothing. Let $H = \{e, (1\;2)\}$, a two-element subgroup. Consider the coset $C = (2\;3)H = \{(2\;3), (1\;3\;2)\}$. (We compose permutations right to left, like functions: $(2\;3)(1\;2)$ means ``apply $(1\;2)$ first, then $(2\;3)$.'' Tracking each symbol gives $1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 3$, $2 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 1$, and $3 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 2$, so $(2\;3)(1\;2) = (1\;3\;2)$.) If coset multiplication were legitimate, the square $C \cdot C$ would be a single coset. Compute it using two different representatives of the same coset:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Choosing $(2\;3) \in C$: $(2\;3)(2\;3) = e \in H$, giving the coset $H$.
    \item Choosing $(1\;3\;2) \in C$: $(1\;3\;2)(1\;3\;2) = (1\;2\;3) \notin H$, giving the coset $(1\;2\;3)H$.
  \end{itemize}
  Since $H \neq (1\;2\;3)H$, the product $C \cdot C$ gives two contradictory results depending on the representative, so this coset multiplication is not well-defined.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Kernel: Measuring Lost Information}

When you move between algebraic systems, you need maps that respect their internal rules. A \textbf{homomorphism} is a structure-preserving function between groups: a map $\phi \colon G \to H$ satisfying
\[
\phi(a \cdot b) = \phi(a) \cdot \phi(b) \quad \text{for all } a, b \in G.
\]
In words: multiplying two elements in $G$ and then applying $\phi$ gives the same result as applying $\phi$ first and multiplying the images in $H$. A homomorphism can send several elements to the same place, but it respects products everywhere.

Because $\phi$ respects multiplication, it automatically maps the identity to the identity. Apply $\phi$ to $e_G = e_G \cdot e_G$:
\[
\phi(e_G) = \phi(e_G \cdot e_G) = \phi(e_G) \cdot \phi(e_G).
\]
Multiplying both sides on the left by $\phi(e_G)^{-1}$ gives $e_H = \phi(e_G)$. The map also preserves inverses:
\begin{align*}
\phi(g)\,\phi(g^{-1}) &= \phi(gg^{-1}) && \text{(homomorphism rule)}\\
&= \phi(e_G)\\
&= e_H, && \text{(identity goes to identity)}
\end{align*}
so $\phi(g^{-1})$ undoes $\phi(g)$, that is, $\phi(g^{-1}) = \phi(g)^{-1}$.

The elements in $G$ that map to the target identity $e_H \in H$ form the \textbf{kernel}:
\[
\ker(\phi) = \{g \in G : \phi(g) = e_H\}.
\]
The kernel measures the \emph{information lost} by the map. If $\ker(\phi) = \{e_G\}$, nothing is collapsed except the identity itself, which means $\phi$ is injective (one-to-one, Chapter~32) and an exact copy of $G$ sits inside $H$. When the kernel contains other elements, $\phi$ sends each coset $gN$ of the kernel $N = \ker(\phi)$ to a single point, as Figure~\ref{36:fig-kernel-coset-homomorphism} shows.

The kernel is never an arbitrary subset of $G$. It is always a subgroup, and it has the extra property called \textbf{normality}, defined in the next section.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check:} Consider the determinant map $\det \colon \mathrm{GL}_2(\R) \to \R^\times$. Here $\mathrm{GL}_2(\R)$ is the group of invertible $2 \times 2$ matrices with real entries (Chapter~21), and $\R^\times$ is the set of non-zero real numbers. The group operation on $\mathrm{GL}_2(\R)$ is matrix multiplication, and the operation on $\R^\times$ is ordinary multiplication.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Why is $\det$ a group homomorphism?
  \item What is the identity element of the target group $(\R^\times, \cdot)$?
  \item What is the kernel $\ker(\det)$?
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Answer:} (a) The determinant product formula guarantees $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$. (b) The identity in $(\R^\times, \cdot)$ is the number $1$. (c) The kernel consists of all matrices with determinant $1$:
\[
\ker(\det) = \{A \in \mathrm{GL}_2(\R) : \det(A) = 1\} = \mathrm{SL}_2(\R).
\]
This is the \emph{special linear group}: the linear transformations of the plane that preserve area and orientation.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Normal Subgroups: Why Coset Multiplication Must Work}

If we want to form a quotient group $G/N$ whose elements are the cosets $gN$, we need a rule to multiply them:
\[
(aN) \cdot (bN) \stackrel{?}{=} (ab)N.
\]
This definition refers to specific representatives $a$ and $b$. What if we picked different elements from the same cosets, say $a' \in aN$ and $b' \in bN$? Would the product $(a'b')N$ land in the same coset $(ab)N$?

In general, \textbf{no}. Coset multiplication is well-defined if and only if the subgroup $N$ is \textbf{normal}.

To see why, suppose $a' = an_1$ and $b' = bn_2$ for some elements $n_1, n_2 \in N$. We need their product $a'b'$ to belong to the coset $(ab)N$. We want to write it in the form $(ab)n_3$ for some $n_3 \in N$. To bring $b$ next to $a$, insert $e = bb^{-1}$ after $a$:
\begin{align*}
a'b' &= (an_1)(bn_2)\\
&= a\,(bb^{-1})\,n_1 b\, n_2 && \text{(insert } e = bb^{-1}\text{)}\\
&= (ab)\,(b^{-1}n_1b)\,n_2. && \text{(associativity)}
\end{align*}
Since $n_2 \in N$, this product belongs to $(ab)N$ if and only if the element $b^{-1}n_1b$ belongs to $N$. An element of the form $gng^{-1}$ is called the \emph{conjugate} of $n$ by $g$, and forming it is called \emph{conjugation}; think of it as ``undo $g$, do $n$, redo $g$,'' read right to left. Because $b$ was an arbitrary element of $G$ and $n_1$ an arbitrary element of $N$, coset multiplication works if and only if conjugating any element of $N$ by any element of $G$ lands back in $N$. (Writing $g = b^{-1}$, the element $b^{-1}n_1b$ is exactly $gn_1g^{-1}$, the form used in the definition below.)

\begin{formalbox}
A subgroup $N \le G$ is \textbf{normal} (written $N \triangleleft G$; Chapter~32 used this definition inside a proof) if conjugation by every element of $G$ maps $N$ onto itself:
\[
gNg^{-1} = N \quad \text{for all } g \in G,
\]
where $gNg^{-1} = \{gng^{-1} : n \in N\}$.
Equivalently, for every $n \in N$ and $g \in G$, the element $gng^{-1} \in N$. In terms of cosets, normality means that each left coset $gN$ equals the corresponding \emph{right coset} $Ng = \{ng : n \in N\}$, for all $g \in G$.
\end{formalbox}

The subgroup $H = \{e, (1\;2)\}$ of $S_3$ from the previous section fails this test. Figure~\ref{36:fig-s3-left-right-cosets} lists its left and right cosets: they cut $S_3$ into pieces in two different ways, so $gH \neq Hg$ for $g = (1\;3)$ and $g = (2\;3)$. That mismatch is exactly what broke the coset multiplication in Figure~\ref{36:fig-non-normal-collapse}.

Every kernel is automatically a normal subgroup. If $n \in \ker(\phi)$ and $g \in G$, we test whether $gng^{-1}$ is in the kernel by applying $\phi$:
\begin{align*}
\phi(gng^{-1}) &= \phi(g)\,\phi(n)\,\phi(g^{-1}) && \text{(homomorphism rule)}\\
&= \phi(g)\, e_H\, \phi(g)^{-1} && (n \in \ker(\phi)\text{; inverses preserved})\\
&= \phi(g)\,\phi(g)^{-1}\\
&= e_H.
\end{align*}
Therefore $gng^{-1} \in \ker(\phi)$, so $\ker(\phi) \triangleleft G$.

Because kernels are normal, the quotient $G/\ker(\phi)$ is a group, and the \textbf{First Isomorphism Theorem for groups} (proved in Chapter~32) applies:
\[
G/\ker(\phi) \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}(\phi).
\]
Here $\operatorname{im}(\phi) = \{\phi(g) : g \in G\}$ is the image of $\phi$, and $\cong$ means ``isomorphic'': there is a bijective homomorphism between the two groups. The theorem says:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Every homomorphism $\phi$ partitions $G$ into the cosets of its kernel.
  \item The quotient group $G/\ker(\phi)$ is isomorphic to the image subgroup $\operatorname{im}(\phi) \le H$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What Abstract Algebra Explains---and Its Limits}

The structural approach of Noether and van der Waerden reshaped much of modern mathematics and theoretical physics:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Galois Theory and Insolvability}: Galois theory showed that the general quintic (for instance, $x^5 - 4x + 2 = 0$) cannot be solved by radicals because of its structure, not because nobody has been clever enough: the alternating group $A_5$ of even permutations of five roots is simple and non-abelian, so its symmetry cannot be broken down into abelian stages (Chapter~21). The same ideas about fields settled old Greek geometry problems, proving that trisecting an arbitrary angle or doubling the cube with straightedge and compass is impossible (squaring the circle needed one more ingredient: Ferdinand von Lindemann's 1882 proof that $\pi$ is transcendental).
  \item \textbf{The Symmetry Language of Physics}: In physics, continuous symmetries are described by \emph{Lie groups} (groups of smooth transformations, such as all rotations of space). Emmy Noether's 1915 theorem proved that every continuous symmetry of a physical system yields a conservation law: time translation yields conservation of energy, spatial translation yields conservation of momentum, and rotational symmetry yields conservation of angular momentum. In the Standard Model of particle physics, fundamental particles are sorted into families according to how they transform under one particular Lie group, written $\mathrm{SU}(3) \times \mathrm{SU}(2) \times \mathrm{U}(1)$ (built from groups of complex matrices).
  \item \textbf{Modern Cryptography}: In \emph{public-key} cryptography, anyone can encrypt a message using a published key, but only the holder of a matching secret key can decrypt it. Systems such as Diffie--Hellman key exchange and elliptic-curve cryptography rely on a one-way property of certain finite groups. Computing a power $g^k$ is fast: \emph{repeated squaring} computes $g, g^2, g^4, g^8, \dots$ and multiplies the ones needed (Figure~\ref{36:fig-repeated-squaring}), so it takes milliseconds even for huge $k$. But recovering the exponent $k$ from $g$ and $g^k$ (the \emph{discrete logarithm problem}) has no known method that finishes in a feasible time for large groups. (RSA, named after Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman, rests instead on the difficulty of factoring large integers, though it too computes in the multiplicative group of integers modulo $n$.)
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
Algebra also has limits:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Algorithmic Undecidability (The Word Problem)}: Although group axioms are short, computing with them can be provably impossible. A \emph{finitely presented} group is one described by a finite list of \emph{generators} (basic elements whose products give every element) and a finite list of \emph{relations} (equations the generators satisfy, such as $r^4 = e$). The Novikov--Boone theorem (Pyotr Novikov 1955, William Boone 1958) proved that there is a finitely presented group for which no algorithm can decide whether an arbitrary product of generators equals the identity element.
  \item \textbf{No Built-In Notion of Distance or Continuity}: Purely algebraic axioms talk only about exact equality ($a \cdot b = c$). On their own, they have no vocabulary for ``close,'' ``approaching,'' or ``continuous.'' To tell a group of separate, isolated symmetries apart from a smooth family of symmetries such as all rotations of a circle, you must combine algebra with topology.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{36:fig-algebraic-hierarchy} summarizes the ladder of algebraic structures, showing how each additional axiom restores familiar computational moves, from groups up to fields. In the examples listed there, $S_n$ is the group of permutations of $n$ objects, $M_n(\R)$ is the set of all $n \times n$ real matrices, $k[x]$ and $\R[x]$ are polynomials in $x$ with coefficients from a field $k$ or from $\R$, and $\mathbb{F}_p = \Z_p$ is the integers modulo a prime $p$.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mental Warm-Up: Cancellation and Inverses.}
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item In the additive group $(\Z_{12}, +)$, solve the equation $4 + x \equiv 1 \pmod{12}$ by adding the additive inverse of $4$.
    \item In the multiplicative system $(\Z_{12}, \cdot)$, find all solutions $x \in \{0, 1, \dots, 11\}$ to the equation $3x \equiv 6 \pmod{12}$. Explain why you cannot ``divide both sides by $3$'' to obtain a unique solution.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Socks-and-Shoes Property.} In any group $G$, prove that for all $a, b \in G$,
  \[
  (ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}.
  \]
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Verify algebraically that $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1}) = e$ and $(b^{-1}a^{-1})(ab) = e$, identifying where associativity is used.
    \item Explain why reversing the composition of successive actions (such as putting on socks then shoes) illustrates why the order of inverses must reverse: $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Subgroups of Index 2 Are Always Normal.} Let $G$ be a group and let $H \le G$ be a subgroup of index $2$, meaning there are exactly two left cosets: $H$ and $G \setminus H$.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item What are the two right cosets of $H$ in $G$?
    \item For any $g \in G$, show that the left coset $gH$ equals the right coset $Hg$ by considering two cases: $g \in H$ and $g \notin H$.
    \item Conclude that $H \triangleleft G$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Determinant Homomorphism and $\mathrm{SL}_n(\R)$.} Consider the determinant mapping $\det \colon \mathrm{GL}_n(\R) \to \R^\times$ on invertible $n \times n$ real matrices.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Prove that $\det$ is surjective: for any non-zero real number $r \in \R^\times$, exhibit an invertible $n \times n$ matrix with determinant $r$.
    \item Apply the First Isomorphism Theorem to deduce that $\mathrm{GL}_n(\R)/\mathrm{SL}_n(\R) \cong \R^\times$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Alternating Group via the First Isomorphism Theorem.} Let $n \ge 2$, and consider the sign homomorphism $\operatorname{sgn} \colon S_n \to \{+1, -1\}$ on the symmetric group of permutations, which sends each even permutation (one that can be built from an even number of swaps of two elements) to $+1$ and each odd permutation to $-1$.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item What is the kernel $\ker(\operatorname{sgn})$? (This is called the alternating group $A_n$.)
    \item What is the size $|A_n|$ relative to $|S_n| = n!$?
    \item Apply the First Isomorphism Theorem to deduce that $S_n/A_n \cong \Z_2$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
