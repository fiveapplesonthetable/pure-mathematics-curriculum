\section{Topology Preview: Continuity Without Rulers}
\chapterline

\subsection{The origin: from Königsberg bridges to the three-body problem}

In Chapter~21, we saw how abstract algebra steps back from raw numbers to study symmetry and operations. A group tracks transformations like rotating an equilateral triangle by $120^\circ$ or flipping it over an axis. But those algebraic operations were rigid: the triangle always retained its exact side lengths, straight edges, and sharp angles.

What happens if you abandon rigidity altogether?

In elementary geometry and introductory calculus, every statement leans on a ruler. You measure the distance $|x - y|$ between points, you compute angles between vectors, and you declare a function continuous when small changes in input guarantee small changes in output:
\[
|x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \eps.
\]
Both $\eps$ and $\delta$ are measurements with a ruler: $\delta$ is the radius of a tiny input window, and $\eps$ is the tolerance band on the output.

The earliest hint that geometry could survive without a ruler arrived in 1736, when Leonhard Euler solved the puzzle of the seven bridges of K\"onigsberg. Seven bridges spanned the Pregel River, connecting two islands to each other and to the two riverbanks. Could a walker plan a route that crossed every bridge exactly once and returned home? Euler saw that the distances between the bridges, the curves of the riverbanks, and the areas of the islands did not matter. He represented each landmass as a single dot (a \emph{node}) and each bridge as a line joining two dots (an \emph{edge}), as in Figure~\ref{top:fig-konigsberg}. A walk that enters a landmass must also leave it, so on a closed walk that uses every bridge once, every landmass must touch an even number of bridges. In K\"onigsberg all four landmasses touch an odd number of bridges (5, 3, 3, 3), so no such walk exists. Only the pattern of connections decided the answer. Euler called this kind of question \emph{geometria situs} (the geometry of position).

The problem that pushed topology into a real subject arose about 150 years later, in the work of Henri Poincar\'e. In 1885, King Oscar II of Sweden announced a prize competition on a question in celestial mechanics: is the solar system stable? Can three bodies pulling on each other by gravity (such as the Sun, Earth, and Moon) keep orbiting forever under Newton's laws, or will one eventually escape or crash into another? Poincar\'e won the prize in 1889.

Poincar\'e found that there is no formula of the usual kind that gives the positions of all three bodies at every future time. The standard method of approximating an orbit by adding smaller and smaller corrections does not settle down to an answer, and tiny changes in the starting positions can produce wildly different orbits. To decide whether orbits stay in a bounded region, he turned to the \emph{qualitative shape} of the space of all possible states of the system, where each point records the positions and velocities of all three bodies at once. In his 1895 paper \emph{Analysis Situs}, he introduced tools that classify such spaces by their holes and loops. One tool, the \emph{fundamental group}, records which closed loops drawn in a space can be shrunk to a point without snagging on a hole. Another, \emph{homology}, counts the holes of each dimension: gaps a loop can wrap around, hollow cavities a surface can enclose, and so on.

\subsection{Geometry on a pliable surface}

Now consider transformations that permit bending, stretching, or compressing without tearing or gluing. Distances and angles change: a circle can deform into an ellipse, and a straight segment can deform into a curve.

What geometric properties survive this kind of deformation?

Distance, straightness, and area are not preserved. What survives is \emph{nearness} and connectedness. If two points are joined by an unbroken path, a deformation without tearing keeps that path unbroken. If a region has a hole, the deformation cannot close the hole without gluing points together. And if a point lies inside a region with some room around it, after the deformation it still lies inside with some (differently shaped) room around it.

Topology is the study of these properties that survive bending and stretching. It is geometry with the ruler thrown away. Figure~\ref{top:fig-rubber-sheet} shows the idea.

\subsection{The definition of an open set}

To describe nearness without distances, mathematicians settled on one basic notion: the \textbf{open set}, a region where every point has some room around it that is still inside the region.

To build intuition we first use ordinary distance in $\R^n$, the space of lists $(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ of $n$ real numbers (the plane is $\R^2$). The straight-line distance between points $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ and $y = (y_1, \dots, y_n)$ is written $\|y - x\|$:
\[
\|y - x\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - x_i)^2}.
\]
For $n = 2$ this is the Pythagorean distance, and for $n = 1$ it is $|y - x|$. The \textbf{open ball} with center $x$ and radius $r > 0$ is the set of all points strictly closer to $x$ than $r$:
\[
B_r(x) = \{y \in \R^n : \|y - x\| < r\}.
\]
On the real line ($n = 1$) the ball $B_r(x)$ is the open interval $(x - r, x + r)$; in the plane it is a disc without its rim. A subset $U \subseteq \R^n$ is called \textbf{open} if every point of $U$ is the center of some ball, of positive radius, lying completely inside $U$:
\[
\forall x \in U, \; \exists r > 0 \text{ such that } B_r(x) \subseteq U.
\]
If you stand at any point $x$ of an open set $U$, you can take a small enough step in \emph{any} direction and still be in $U$. A point is a \textbf{boundary point} of $U$ if every ball around it, however small, contains both points of $U$ and points outside $U$; the set of boundary points is written $\partial U$. An open set contains none of its boundary points. Figure~\ref{top:fig-interior-boundary} shows the three kinds of point.

\subsection{The topological axioms}

What happens when we throw away the distance $\|\cdot\|$ entirely? We keep only the collection $\mathcal{T}$ (a script letter T) of subsets that we agree to call ``open''. For these sets to behave like the open sets of $\R^n$, the collection must obey three rules, called the \emph{axioms} of a topology.

\begin{definition}
A \textbf{topology} on a set $X$ is a collection $\mathcal{T}$ of subsets of $X$ satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Both the empty set $\emptyset$ and the full space $X$ belong to $\mathcal{T}$.
\item Any union (finite or infinite) of sets in $\mathcal{T}$ belongs to $\mathcal{T}$.
\item Any intersection of \emph{finitely many} sets in $\mathcal{T}$ belongs to $\mathcal{T}$.
\end{enumerate}
The pair $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is called a \textbf{topological space}, and the sets in $\mathcal{T}$ are called the \textbf{open sets} of the space.
\end{definition}

Why do unions permit infinitely many sets, while intersections only permit finitely many?

Consider open intervals in $\R$:
\begin{itemize}
\item If you take the union of any collection of open intervals, every point in the union belongs to at least one of the intervals, and that interval already gives the point room around it. The same room works inside the union.
\item But consider an infinite sequence of shrinking open intervals centered at $0$ (Figure~\ref{top:fig-shrinking-intervals}):
\[
\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \left(-\frac{1}{n}, \frac{1}{n}\right) = \{0\}.
\]
Each interval $(-1/n, 1/n)$ is open. Yet for any $x \neq 0$, the Archimedean property (Chapter~20) gives an integer $n > 1/|x|$, so $|x| > 1/n$ and $x \notin (-1/n, 1/n)$. Thus $0$ is the only point in every interval, and the infinite intersection is the one-point set $\{0\}$. No open interval around $0$ fits inside $\{0\}$, so $\{0\}$ is not open in $\R$: an infinite intersection of open sets need not be open.
\end{itemize}

Restricting the intersection rule to finitely many sets avoids this problem. In $\R^n$, if a point lies in finitely many open sets with room of radii $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_k > 0$, the smallest radius $r = \min(r_1, \dots, r_k)$ is still strictly positive and works for all of them at once (Figure~\ref{top:fig-finite-intersection}). With infinitely many sets the radii can shrink towards $0$, as the radii $1/n$ did above, and then no single positive radius works for all of them.

\begin{example}[Topologies on a three-point set]
To see how these axioms work on a finite set, consider $X = \{a, b, c\}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The indiscrete topology (the fewest possible open sets):}
\[
\mathcal{T}_{\text{indisc}} = \{\emptyset, X\}.
\]
Only the empty set and the whole space are declared open. No open set can contain one point while leaving out another, so the topology cannot tell points apart.
\item \textbf{The discrete topology (the most possible open sets):}
\[
\mathcal{T}_{\text{disc}} = \pow(X) = \{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{b\}, \{c\}, \{a, b\}, \{a, c\}, \{b, c\}, X\}.
\]
Every subset is open, so $\mathcal{T}_{\text{disc}}$ is the power set $\pow(X)$ from Chapter~10. In particular, every one-point set $\{x\}$ is open.
\item \textbf{An intermediate topology:}
\[
\mathcal{T} = \{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{a, b\}, X\}.
\]
Check the rules. Unions and intersections involving $\emptyset$ or $X$ give back one of the sets already listed, so only one pair needs checking:
\begin{align*}
\{a\} \cup \{a, b\} &= \{a, b\} \in \mathcal{T}, \\
\{a\} \cap \{a, b\} &= \{a\} \in \mathcal{T}.
\end{align*}
Both $\emptyset$ and $X$ are included. All three axioms hold, so $\mathcal{T}$ is a topology on $X$. The open sets are nested inside one another, as the left half of Figure~\ref{top:fig-three-point} shows.
\item \textbf{A non-example (violating the axioms):}
Consider $\mathcal{T}_{\text{bad}} = \{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{b\}, X\}$.
Here, $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$ both belong to $\mathcal{T}_{\text{bad}}$, but their union $\{a\} \cup \{b\} = \{a, b\}$ is not in $\mathcal{T}_{\text{bad}}$.
The union axiom fails, so $\mathcal{T}_{\text{bad}}$ is not a topology (right half of Figure~\ref{top:fig-three-point}).
\end{itemize}
\end{example}

\subsection{Continuity reimagined: pulling open sets backward}

In calculus, we say $f$ is continuous if nearby inputs give nearby outputs. Topology says the same thing without mentioning distance, by looking at which inputs land in an open set of outputs (Figure~\ref{top:fig-continuity-preimage}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering

\begin{definition}
Let $(X, \mathcal{T}_X)$ and $(Y, \mathcal{T}_Y)$ be topological spaces. A function $f: X \to Y$ is \textbf{continuous} if for every open set $V \in \mathcal{T}_Y$, the inverse image
\[
f^{-1}(V) = \{x \in X : f(x) \in V\}
\]
is an open set in $X$.
\end{definition}

Recall from Chapter~14 that $f^{-1}(V)$ denotes the set-theoretic \textbf{preimage}---the set of all domain elements that land in $V$. It does \emph{not} require $f$ to have an inverse function.

Why does this definition test the \emph{inverse} image $f^{-1}(V)$ rather than the forward image $f(U)$?

First, pushing open sets forward fails whenever a function squeezes space together. Consider the constant function $f: \R \to \R$ given by $f(x) = 5$ for all $x$. A constant function has no jumps at all, so any sensible definition must call it continuous. Yet if you take the open interval $U = (0, 1)$, its forward image is
\[
f(U) = \{5\}.
\]
This image is a single point. No interval around $5$ fits inside $\{5\}$, so it is not open. Testing forward images would declare constant functions discontinuous.

Pulling backward, by contrast, gives the right answer (Figure~\ref{top:fig-constant}):
\begin{itemize}
\item If $5 \in V$, every real input lands in $V$, so $f^{-1}(V) = \R$, which is open.
\item If $5 \notin V$, no input lands in $V$, so $f^{-1}(V) = \emptyset$, which is open.
\end{itemize}

Preimages also fit the axioms well. As in Chapter~14, for any sets $V_1, V_2 \subseteq Y$,
\begin{align*}
f^{-1}(V_1 \cup V_2) &= f^{-1}(V_1) \cup f^{-1}(V_2), \\
f^{-1}(V_1 \cap V_2) &= f^{-1}(V_1) \cap f^{-1}(V_2),
\end{align*}
and the first identity holds for unions of any number of sets. Forward images do not behave this well for intersections.

Second, pulling backward detects discontinuities directly. Consider the step function:
\[
f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } x < 0, \\ 1 & \text{if } x \ge 0. \end{cases}
\]
Choose the open target interval $V = (0.5, 1.5) \subseteq \R$ surrounding the output value $1$ and excluding $0$. The inputs that map into $V$ are the non-negative numbers:
\[
f^{-1}(V) = [0, \infty).
\]
Is $[0, \infty)$ open in $\R$? No. At the endpoint $x = 0$, every candidate interval $(-\delta, \delta)$ contains negative numbers, where $f(x) = 0 \notin V$. So the set $[0, \infty)$ contains no open ball centered at $0$, and the topological definition detects the jump without taking any limits. Figure~\ref{top:fig-discontinuity-detector} shows the test.

\begin{theorem}[Equivalence with calculus continuity in $\R$]
Let $f: \R \to \R$ be a function. Then $f$ is continuous under the $\eps$-$\delta$ definition if and only if for every open set $V \subseteq \R$, the preimage $f^{-1}(V)$ is an open set in $\R$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We prove both directions. Figure~\ref{top:fig-eps-delta} shows the picture behind both.

($\implies$) Suppose $f$ is continuous in the $\eps$-$\delta$ sense, and let $V \subseteq \R$ be any open set. We must show that $f^{-1}(V)$ is open. If $f^{-1}(V) = \emptyset$, it is open by axiom~1. Otherwise, choose any point $c \in f^{-1}(V)$. By definition of the preimage, $f(c) \in V$. Because $V$ is open in $\R$, there is a radius $\eps > 0$ such that the open interval $(f(c) - \eps, f(c) + \eps)$ lies inside $V$. By $\eps$-$\delta$ continuity of $f$ at $c$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that
\[
|x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \eps.
\]
In set notation, this means every point of the open interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$ is sent into the $\eps$-interval around $f(c)$:
\[
f\bigl((c - \delta, c + \delta)\bigr) \subseteq (f(c) - \eps, f(c) + \eps) \subseteq V.
\]
Therefore $(c - \delta, c + \delta) \subseteq f^{-1}(V)$. We have found an open interval around $c$ inside $f^{-1}(V)$. Since $c$ was an arbitrary point of $f^{-1}(V)$, the set $f^{-1}(V)$ is open.

($\impliedby$) Conversely, suppose the preimage of every open set in $\R$ is open. Fix any point $c \in \R$ and let $\eps > 0$ be given. The open interval $V = (f(c) - \eps, f(c) + \eps)$ is an open set in $\R$. By our hypothesis, its preimage $U = f^{-1}(V)$ is an open set in $\R$. Also $c \in U$, because $f(c) \in V$. By the definition of an open set in $\R$, there exists a radius $\delta > 0$ such that the open ball $(c - \delta, c + \delta) \subseteq U$. Translating this back into points:
\[
|x - c| < \delta \implies x \in U = f^{-1}(V) \implies f(x) \in V \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \eps.
\]
Thus $f$ satisfies the $\eps$-$\delta$ definition of continuity at $c$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Topological continuity says: choose any open target $V$ around an output; then the inputs that land inside $V$ form an open set, so each of them has room to move without leaving it. A sudden jump would break this.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{historybox}
The path to geometry without rulers had its share of false starts. For decades after Euler, mathematicians took his formula for polyhedra (solids with flat polygon faces), $V - E + F = 2$, as a law of all such solids; here $V$ counts the corners, $E$ the edges, and $F$ the faces. In the early nineteenth century, solids with tunnels (like a picture frame) and solids with hollow inner cavities turned up, giving counts of zero or four instead of two. As Imre Lakatos describes in \emph{Proofs and Refutations}, mathematicians argued for decades between ``monster-barring'' (refusing to call such shapes polyhedra) and ``monster-adjusting'' (repairing the definition). The resolution was the \emph{Euler characteristic} $\chi = V - E + F$, which for a surface with $g$ tunnels through it equals $2 - 2g$. The number $g$ is called the \emph{genus}. A sphere has $g=0$ ($\chi = 2$), while a torus, the surface of a donut, has $g=1$ ($\chi = 0$).

Poincar\'e himself stumbled on the subtlety of higher-dimensional spaces. In 1900 he claimed that any closed three-dimensional space (finite in extent and without an edge, as the surface of a ball is a closed two-dimensional space) with the same homology as the three-dimensional sphere (the points at distance 1 from the origin in $\R^4$, one dimension up from the surface of a ball) must be that sphere. In 1904 he found his own counterexample, now called the Poincar\'e homology sphere, built by gluing opposite faces of a solid dodecahedron (a solid with twelve five-sided faces) with a slight twist. It has the same hole counts as the sphere, yet it contains loops that can never be shrunk to a point. Seeing that homology was too coarse a test, Poincar\'e asked instead whether a closed three-dimensional space in which every loop can be shrunk to a point must be the three-dimensional sphere. This question, the Poincar\'e Conjecture, stayed open for a century until Grigori Perelman proved it in 2002--2003, using a method of Richard Hamilton called Ricci flow, which gradually smooths out the shape of a space, somewhat as heat spreads out until the temperature is even.

Early topology still relied on distances. In 1914, Felix Hausdorff defined spaces using only the neighborhoods of each point, with no distance function. In 1922, Kazimierz Kuratowski gave another distance-free system of axioms, and over the following decades mathematicians settled on the open-set axioms used in this chapter (closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections).

Some questions remain open. The Poincar\'e Conjecture has analogues in every dimension: Stephen Smale proved them for dimensions five and higher in 1961, and Michael Freedman for dimension four in 1982. A sharper version, the \textbf{smooth 4-dimensional Poincar\'e Conjecture}, is still unsolved. Call a deformation \emph{smooth} if it creates no corners or creases. The question asks whether every four-dimensional space that can be deformed into the 4-sphere without tearing can also be deformed into it smoothly, or whether there are ``exotic'' 4-spheres that match the 4-sphere as topological spaces but cannot be smoothed into it. In some higher dimensions exotic spheres are known to exist (John Milnor found the first, in dimension seven, in 1956); in dimension four, nobody knows.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Qualitative geometry---and its natural boundaries}

Describing space through open sets isolates the properties that survive stretching. Two spaces are called \textbf{homeomorphic} when there is a \textbf{homeomorphism} between them: a continuous bijection whose inverse is also continuous. A homeomorphism deforms one space into the other without tearing or gluing. A \textbf{topological invariant} is a property or number that every homeomorphism leaves unchanged; the Euler characteristic and the fundamental group are examples. If a surface has a hole, no homeomorphism can erase it. With these tools mathematicians have listed every closed surface (finite, without an edge, like a sphere or the surface of a donut) up to homeomorphism. Analysis uses the same language for spaces whose points are functions, where different notions of ``getting close'' correspond to different choices of open sets.

The trade-off is that dropping distance also drops every measurement. To a topologist, a round sphere, a stretched ellipsoid, and the surface of a cube are all homeomorphic, while the surface of a donut is not homeomorphic to any of them (Figure~\ref{top:fig-homeomorphic}). Open sets alone cannot measure shortest paths, angles, or curvature, nor can they define velocity vectors or differential equations.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Mental check: valid and invalid topologies.}
Let $X = \{1, 2, 3\}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Consider the collection $\mathcal{T}_1 = \{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2\}, X\}$. Explain why $\mathcal{T}_1$ is not a topology on $X$. Which specific set is missing?
\item Is $\mathcal{T}_2 = \{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{1, 2\}, X\}$ a valid topology on $X$? Test all three axioms: the empty set and whole space, all possible unions, and all possible finite intersections.

\item \textbf{Open and non-open sets in $\R$.}
For each subset of $\R$ (with the usual open sets, defined by open balls), determine whether the set is open. Prove your claim directly using the definition of an open ball:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item The open ray $U = (2, \infty)$.
\item The half-open interval $A = [0, 5)$.
\item The set of rational numbers $\Q \subset \R$. (Hint: Does any open ball $(q - r, q + r)$ around a rational $q$ contain only rational numbers?)
\item The punctured real line $\R \setminus \{0\}$.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Continuity of composed functions via preimages.}
Let $X, Y, Z$ be topological spaces, and let $f: X \to Y$ and $g: Y \to Z$ be continuous functions. Prove that the composite function $g \circ f: X \to Z$ is continuous.
(Hint: Recall the preimage identity $(g \circ f)^{-1}(W) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(W))$ from Chapter~14. Apply the definition of topological continuity to $g$, then to $f$.)

\item \textbf{The extremes: discrete and indiscrete topologies.}
Let $X$ and $Y$ be topological spaces, and let $f: X \to Y$ be an arbitrary function.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Suppose $X$ carries the discrete topology ($\mathcal{T}_X = \pow(X)$, every subset is open). Prove that $f$ is automatically continuous, regardless of what $Y$ is.
\item Suppose $Y$ carries the indiscrete topology ($\mathcal{T}_Y = \{\emptyset, Y\}$). Prove that $f$ is automatically continuous, regardless of what $X$ is.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Catching a discontinuity with a preimage.}
Consider the sign function $\operatorname{sgn}: \R \to \R$ defined by
\[
\operatorname{sgn}(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x > 0, \\ 0 & \text{if } x = 0, \\ -1 & \text{if } x < 0. \end{cases}
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Compute $\operatorname{sgn}^{-1}\bigl((0.5, 1.5)\bigr)$ and explain why this preimage \emph{is} open in $\R$ (unlike the step function in Figure~\ref{top:fig-discontinuity-detector}, where $f(0) = 1$).
\item Find an open interval $V \subseteq \R$ (hint: include the output $0 = \operatorname{sgn}(0)$, such as $V = (-0.5, 0.5)$ or $V = (-0.5, 1.5)$) such that $\operatorname{sgn}^{-1}(V)$ is \emph{not} open in $\R$. Which point in $\operatorname{sgn}^{-1}(V)$ has no open interval around it lying inside $\operatorname{sgn}^{-1}(V)$?
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
