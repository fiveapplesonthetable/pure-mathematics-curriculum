\section{Topology Preview: Continuity Without Rulers}
\chapterline

\subsection{The origin: from Königsberg bridges to the three-body problem}

In Chapter~21, we saw how abstract algebra steps back from raw numbers to study symmetry and operations. A group tracks transformations like rotating an equilateral triangle by $120^\circ$ or flipping it over an axis. But those algebraic operations were rigid: the triangle always retained its exact side lengths, straight edges, and sharp angles.

What happens if you abandon rigidity altogether?

In elementary geometry and introductory calculus, every statement leans on a ruler. You measure the distance $|x - y|$ between points, you compute angles between vectors, and you declare a function continuous when small changes in input guarantee small changes in output:
\[
|x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \eps.
\]
Both $\eps$ and $\delta$ are measurements with a ruler: $\delta$ is the radius of a tiny input window, and $\eps$ is the tolerance band on the output.

The earliest hint that geometry could survive without a ruler arrived in 1736, when Leonhard Euler resolved the puzzle of the seven bridges of K\"onigsberg. Seven bridges spanned the Pregel River, connecting two large islands to each other and to the opposite riverbanks. Could an afternoon walker plan a route that crossed every single bridge exactly once and returned home? Euler observed that the exact distances between the bridges, the curves of the riverbanks, and the land areas of the islands were completely irrelevant to the puzzle. By representing each landmass as a single dot (a node) and each bridge as a connecting line (an edge), Euler proved that crossing every bridge once was impossible because four landmasses met an odd number of bridges. Only the pattern of connectivity---the topology of the network---determined the outcome. Euler called this qualitative inquiry \emph{geometria situs} (the geometry of position).

Yet the problem that prompted the development of modern topology arose over 150 years later, in the work of Henri Poincar\'e. In 1889, King Oscar II of Sweden sponsored a mathematical competition to address a question in celestial mechanics: is the solar system stable? Can three gravitational bodies (such as the Sun, Earth, and Moon) orbit each other indefinitely under Newton's laws, or will one eventually escape or collide with another body?

Poincar\'e discovered that the three-body problem is non-integrable: there is no algebraic formula or closed-form expression for the positions of all three bodies over time. Analytical perturbation series---approximating paths by adding progressively smaller corrections---diverge, and small changes to initial positions produce complex orbits that defy explicit calculation. Poincar\'e realized that to determine whether orbits remained bounded, mathematicians needed to study the \emph{qualitative shape of the phase space}---the space where each point represents the positions and velocities of all bodies at once. In his 1895 monograph \emph{Analysis Situs}, Poincar\'e introduced key tools of algebraic topology: the \emph{fundamental group} (which records which closed loops on a space can be shrunk to a point without snagging), alongside \emph{homology} and \emph{Betti numbers} (algebraic tools that count independent holes, tunnels, and cavities across various dimensions) to classify multidimensional spaces by their holes and loops.

\subsection{Geometry on a pliable surface}

Now consider transformations that permit bending, stretching, or compressing without tearing or gluing. Distances and angles change: a circle can deform into an ellipse, and a straight segment can deform into a curve.

What geometric properties survive this kind of deformation?

Distance, straightness, and area are not preserved. But qualitative relations remain: \emph{nearness} and connectivity. If two points are connected by a continuous path, continuous deformation does not break that path. If a region has a hole, continuous deformation cannot close the hole without gluing points together. And if a point lies in the interior of a region, it still has an open neighborhood after deformation.

Topology is the study of these qualitative geometric properties. It is geometry with the ruler discarded.

\subsection{The definition of an open set}

To formalize nearness without distances, mathematicians realized that the fundamental building block is the concept of an \textbf{open set}---a region where every point is protected by a cushion of wiggle room.

In ordinary Euclidean space $\R^n$, we can still use the standard Euclidean norm $\|y - x\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i)^2}$ (the straight-line ruler distance) to build our intuition. An open ball around a center $x$ of radius $r > 0$ is the set of all points strictly closer than $r$:
\[
B_r(x) = \{y \in \R^n : \|y - x\| < r\}.
\]
A subset $U \subseteq \R^n$ is called \textbf{open} if every point inside $U$ has a buffer zone of positive radius completely contained inside $U$:
\[
\forall x \in U, \; \exists r > 0 \text{ such that } B_r(x) \subseteq U.
\]
If you stand at any point $x$ in an open set, you can take a small step in \emph{any} direction without falling off the edge. Points on the boundary $\partial U$ (the outer rim where the inside meets the outside) do not belong to the set.

\subsection{The topological axioms}

What happens when we discard the metric $\|\cdot\|$ entirely? We keep only the collection $\mathcal{T}$ of subsets that we agree to call ``open''. To behave like genuine neighborhoods, this collection must satisfy three simple rules.

\begin{definition}
A \textbf{topology} on a set $X$ is a collection $\mathcal{T}$ of subsets of $X$ satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Both the empty set $\emptyset$ and the full space $X$ belong to $\mathcal{T}$.
\item Any union (finite or infinite) of sets in $\mathcal{T}$ belongs to $\mathcal{T}$.
\item Any intersection of \emph{finitely many} sets in $\mathcal{T}$ belongs to $\mathcal{T}$.
\end{enumerate}
The pair $(X, \mathcal{T})$ is called a \textbf{topological space}, and the sets in $\mathcal{T}$ are called the \textbf{open sets} of the space.
\end{definition}

Why do unions permit infinitely many sets, while intersections only permit finitely many?

Consider open intervals in $\R$:
\begin{itemize}
\item If you take the union of any collection of open intervals, every point in the union belongs to at least one interval, where it already has breathing room. That room remains valid in the union.
\item But consider an infinite sequence of shrinking open intervals centered at $0$:
\[
\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \left(-\frac{1}{n}, \frac{1}{n}\right) = \{0\}.
\]
Each individual interval $(-1/n, 1/n)$ is open. Yet their infinite intersection is the singleton set $\{0\}$. No open interval around $0$ is contained in $\{0\}$, so $\{0\}$ is not open in $\R$: an infinite intersection of open sets need not be open.
\end{itemize}
Limiting the intersection axiom to finitely many sets preserves the property: if $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_k > 0$ are finitely many radii, the minimum radius $r = \min(r_1, \dots, r_k)$ is still strictly positive.

\begin{example}[Topologies on a three-point set]
To see how these axioms work on a finite set, consider $X = \{a, b, c\}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The indiscrete topology (the coarsest topology):}
\[
\mathcal{T}_{\text{indisc}} = \{\emptyset, X\}.
\]
Only the empty set and the whole space are declared open. No two distinct points can be separated by disjoint open sets.
\item \textbf{The discrete topology (the finest topology):}
\[
\mathcal{T}_{\text{disc}} = \pow(X) = \{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{b\}, \{c\}, \{a, b\}, \{a, c\}, \{b, c\}, X\}.
\]
Every subset is open. In particular, every singleton $\{x\}$ is an open set.
\item \textbf{An intermediate topology:}
\[
\mathcal{T} = \{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{a, b\}, X\}.
\]
Check the rules:
The union $\{a\} \cup \{a, b\} = \{a, b\} \in \mathcal{T}$.
The intersection $\{a\} \cap \{a, b\} = \{a\} \in \mathcal{T}$.
Both $\emptyset$ and $X$ are included. All three axioms hold, so $\mathcal{T}$ is a topology on $X$.
\item \textbf{A non-example (violating the axioms):}
Consider $\mathcal{T}_{\text{bad}} = \{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{b\}, X\}$.
Here, $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$ both belong to $\mathcal{T}_{\text{bad}}$, but their union $\{a\} \cup \{b\} = \{a, b\}$ is not in $\mathcal{T}_{\text{bad}}$.
The union axiom fails, so $\mathcal{T}_{\text{bad}}$ is not a topology.
\end{itemize}
\end{example}

\subsection{Continuity reimagined: pulling open sets backward}

In calculus, we say $f$ is continuous if nearby inputs yield nearby outputs. In topology, we translate this without mentioning distance.

\begin{definition}
Let $(X, \mathcal{T}_X)$ and $(Y, \mathcal{T}_Y)$ be topological spaces. A function $f: X \to Y$ is \textbf{continuous} if for every open set $V \in \mathcal{T}_Y$, the inverse image
\[
f^{-1}(V) = \{x \in X : f(x) \in V\}
\]
is an open set in $X$.
\end{definition}

Recall from Chapter~14 that $f^{-1}(V)$ denotes the set-theoretic \textbf{preimage}---the set of all domain elements that land in $V$. It does \emph{not} require $f$ to have an inverse function!

Why does this definition test the \emph{inverse} image $f^{-1}(V)$ rather than the forward image $f(U)$?

First, pushing open sets forward fails whenever a function compresses space. Consider the constant function $f: \R \to \R$ given by $f(x) = 5$ for all $x$. Intuitively, a constant function is as continuous as a function can possibly be. Yet if you take the open interval $U = (0, 1)$, its forward image is:
\[
f(U) = \{5\},
\]
which is a solitary point---a closed set with zero wiggle room, hence not open! Testing forward images would declare constant functions discontinuous.

Pulling backward, by contrast, behaves consistently:
\begin{itemize}
\item If $5 \in V$, every real input lands in $V$, so $f^{-1}(V) = \R$, which is open.
\item If $5 \notin V$, no input lands in $V$, so $f^{-1}(V) = \emptyset$, which is open.
\end{itemize}
The inverse image preserves unions, intersections, and complements.

Second, pulling backward detects discontinuities directly. Consider the step function:
\[
f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } x < 0, \\ 1 & \text{if } x \ge 0. \end{cases}
\]
Choose the open target interval $V = (0.5, 1.5) \subset \R$ surrounding the output value $1$ and excluding $0$. The inputs that map into $V$ are the non-negative numbers:
\[
f^{-1}(V) = [0, \infty).
\]
Is $[0, \infty)$ open in $\R$? No. At the endpoint $x = 0$, every candidate interval $(-\delta, \delta)$ contains negative numbers, where $f(x) = 0 \notin V$. The set $[0, \infty)$ contains no open ball centered at $0$. The topological definition therefore detects the discontinuity without taking numerical limits.

\begin{theorem}[Equivalence with calculus continuity in $\R$]
Let $f: \R \to \R$ be a function. Then $f$ is continuous under the $\eps$-$\delta$ definition if and only if for every open set $V \subseteq \R$, the preimage $f^{-1}(V)$ is an open set in $\R$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We prove both directions.

($\implies$) Suppose $f$ is continuous in the $\eps$-$\delta$ sense, and let $V \subseteq \R$ be any open set. We must show that $f^{-1}(V)$ is open. If $f^{-1}(V) = \emptyset$, it is open by axiom~1. Otherwise, choose any point $c \in f^{-1}(V)$. By definition of the preimage, $f(c) \in V$. Because $V$ is open in $\R$, there exists an error tolerance $\eps > 0$ such that the open interval $(f(c) - \eps, f(c) + \eps) \subseteq V$. By $\eps$-$\delta$ continuity of $f$ at $c$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that:
\[
|x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \eps.
\]
In set notation, this means the entire open interval $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$ maps into $(f(c) - \eps, f(c) + \eps) \subseteq V$. Therefore, $(c - \delta, c + \delta) \subseteq f^{-1}(V)$. We have found a positive buffer around $c$ contained inside $f^{-1}(V)$. Since $c$ was arbitrary, $f^{-1}(V)$ is an open set.

($\impliedby$) Conversely, suppose the preimage of every open set in $\R$ is open. Fix any point $c \in \R$ and let $\eps > 0$ be given. The open interval $V = (f(c) - \eps, f(c) + \eps)$ is an open set in $\R$. By our hypothesis, its preimage $U = f^{-1}(V)$ is an open set in $\R$. Notice that $c \in U$ because $f(c) \in V$. By the definition of an open set in $\R$, there exists a radius $\delta > 0$ such that the open ball $(c - \delta, c + \delta) \subseteq U$. Translating this back into points: if $|x - c| < \delta$, then $x \in U = f^{-1}(V)$, which means $f(x) \in V$, or equivalently $|f(x) - f(c)| < \eps$. Thus $f$ satisfies the $\eps$-$\delta$ definition of continuity at $c$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Topological continuity says: if you choose any target neighborhood $V$ around an output, all inputs that land inside $V$ form a cushioned neighborhood with room to move. There are no sudden leaps or tears.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{historybox}
The path to understanding qualitative geometry without rulers was filled with surprising false starts. For over a century, mathematicians believed Euler's formula for polyhedra, $V - E + F = 2$---where $V$ counts the corner vertices, $E$ counts the straight edges, and $F$ counts the flat polygon faces---was an unassailable law of three-dimensional solids. But in the nineteenth century, shapes with tunnels (like picture frames) and solids with hollow inner cavities began to emerge, yielding counts of zero or four instead of two. As Imre Lakatos chronicled in \emph{Proofs and Refutations}, mathematicians spent decades caught between ``monster-barring'' (banning shapes with holes from being called polyhedra) and ``monster-adjusting'' (repairing the definition). The resolution came when mathematicians discovered the Euler characteristic: $\chi = V - E + F = 2 - 2g$, where the genus $g$ counts the number of donut-like through-holes. A sphere has genus $g=0$ ($\chi = 2$), while a torus has $g=1$ ($\chi = 0$).

Even Henri Poincar\'e, the father of modern topology, stumbled on the subtlety of higher-dimensional spaces. In his initial 1895 \emph{Analysis Situs}, Poincar\'e conjectured that any closed three-dimensional space with the same loop-counting homology as a three-dimensional sphere must actually be a sphere. Nine years later, in 1904, he discovered his own counterexample: the Poincar\'e homology sphere, built by gluing opposite faces of a solid twelve-sided dodecahedron with a slight twist. It shared the homology of a sphere, yet an ant walking along its paths could trace loops that could never be shrunk to a point without snagging. Realizing that homology was too blunt an instrument, Poincar\'e invented the \emph{fundamental group} and formulated the celebrated Poincar\'e Conjecture: any closed three-dimensional space where every loop can be shrunk to a single point must be topologically a 3-sphere. This stood unresolved until Grigori Perelman proved it in 2002--2003 using Richard Hamilton's Ricci flow.

Early developments in topology relied on metric distances. In 1914, Felix Hausdorff removed the requirement of a metric, defining topological spaces purely through neighborhoods of points. In 1922, Kazimierz Kuratowski formulated the modern axioms of open sets (closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections), establishing topology as the study of nearness without metric measurement.

Yet the frontier of topology remains active with open problems. While Perelman resolved the 3-dimensional Poincar\'e Conjecture, Stephen Smale resolved dimensions five and higher in 1961, and Michael Freedman resolved dimension four in 1982 for topological manifolds, one famous problem remains unsolved: the \textbf{Smooth 4-Dimensional Poincar\'e Conjecture}. It asks whether every smooth four-dimensional space that is homeomorphic to the 4-sphere is also diffeomorphic to it (meaning it shares the same smooth differential structure), or whether there exist ``exotic'' 4-spheres that are topologically identical yet carry an incompatible smooth structure. Dimension four is the only dimension where this question remains open.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Qualitative geometry---and its natural boundaries}

Describing space through open sets isolates the qualitative properties that survive continuous stretching. Two spaces are called \textbf{homeomorphic} when there is a \textbf{homeomorphism} between them---a continuous bijection whose inverse is also continuous, deforming one space into the other without tearing or gluing. Topological invariants such as the Euler characteristic and the fundamental group stay unchanged under any homeomorphism: if a surface has a hole, no continuous deformation can erase it. That viewpoint lets mathematicians classify compact two-dimensional surfaces completely, and it gives analysis a natural language for infinite-dimensional function spaces where different notions of convergence come from different topologies.

The trade-off is that dropping distance also drops every quantitative measurement. To a topologist, a round sphere, a stretched ellipsoid, and the surface of a cube are all homeomorphic. Open sets alone cannot measure shortest paths, angles, or curvature, nor can they define velocity vectors or differential equations.

Later, Chapter~37 provides our \textbf{Second Pass: Topology as the Study of Stable Nearness}.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Mental check: valid and invalid topologies.}
Let $X = \{1, 2, 3\}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Consider the collection $\mathcal{T}_1 = \{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2\}, X\}$. Explain why $\mathcal{T}_1$ is not a topology on $X$. Which specific set is missing?
\item Is $\mathcal{T}_2 = \{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{1, 2\}, X\}$ a valid topology on $X$? Test all three axioms: the empty set and whole space, all possible unions, and all possible finite intersections.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Open and non-open sets in $\R$.}
For each subset of $\R$ under the standard Euclidean topology, determine whether the set is open. Prove your claim directly using the definition of an open ball:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item The open ray $U = (2, \infty)$.
\item The half-open interval $A = [0, 5)$.
\item The set of rational numbers $\Q \subset \R$. (Hint: Does any open ball $(q - r, q + r)$ around a rational $q$ contain only rational numbers?)
\item The punctured real line $\R \setminus \{0\}$.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Continuity of composed functions via preimages.}
Let $X, Y, Z$ be topological spaces, and let $f: X \to Y$ and $g: Y \to Z$ be continuous functions. Prove that the composite function $g \circ f: X \to Z$ is continuous.
(Hint: Recall the preimage identity $(g \circ f)^{-1}(W) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(W))$ from Chapter~14. Apply the definition of topological continuity to $g$, then to $f$.)

\item \textbf{The extremes: discrete and indiscrete topologies.}
Let $X$ and $Y$ be topological spaces, and let $f: X \to Y$ be an arbitrary function.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Suppose $X$ carries the discrete topology ($\mathcal{T}_X = \pow(X)$, every subset is open). Prove that $f$ is automatically continuous, regardless of what $Y$ is.
\item Suppose $Y$ carries the indiscrete topology ($\mathcal{T}_Y = \{\emptyset, Y\}$). Prove that $f$ is automatically continuous, regardless of what $X$ is.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Catching a discontinuity with a preimage.}
Consider the sign function $\operatorname{sgn}: \R \to \R$ defined by:
\[
\operatorname{sgn}(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x > 0, \\ 0 & \text{if } x = 0, \\ -1 & \text{if } x < 0. \end{cases}
\]
Find an open target interval $V \subset \R$ containing $1$ such that $\operatorname{sgn}^{-1}(V)$ is not an open set in $\R$. Which boundary point fails to have a protective buffer ball?
\end{enumerate}
