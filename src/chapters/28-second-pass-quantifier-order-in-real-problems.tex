\section{Second Pass: Quantifier Order in Real Problems}
\chapterline

In Chapter~27, propositional logic treated sentences as indivisible units. To do mathematics, we must inspect the inner structure where variables interact through quantifiers: ``for every'' ($\forall$) and ``there exists'' ($\exists$).

\subsection{Cauchy's 1821 slip: how a hidden quantifier swap fooled mathematicians}

In 1821, the French mathematician Augustin-Louis Cauchy published his textbook \emph{Cours d'Analyse}. He wanted to rid calculus of vague infinitesimals---quantities imagined as smaller than any positive fraction yet strictly greater than zero---and to build limits on careful deductions instead. In the book he presented a theorem that seemed self-evident: if an infinite sequence of continuous functions $f_1, f_2, f_3, \dots$ converges at every point of an interval, the limit function must also be continuous. Here, a \textbf{continuous function} is one whose graph is an unbroken curve, drawn without lifting your pen from the page (Chapter~20 gave the exact $\eps$-$\delta$ definition).

For five years, mathematicians accepted Cauchy's proof. But in 1826, the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel pointed out a counterexample: a \textbf{Fourier series}---an infinite sum of sine and cosine waves---could add up to a discontinuous \textbf{sawtooth wave}, which ramps upward smoothly and then drops in a vertical jump.

To see the problem in its simplest form, consider the functions $f_n(x) = x^n$ on the closed unit interval $[0, 1]$, the set of all real numbers $x$ with $0 \le x \le 1$ (``closed'' means both endpoints are included).
For every integer $n \ge 1$, the function $f_n(x) = x^n$ is continuous. For $x = 1/2$, the powers $(1/2)^n$ decrease rapidly toward $0$. For any fixed $x$ strictly less than $1$, repeated multiplication shrinks the value down to zero: as $n \to \infty$, $f_n(x) \to 0$. But at the right endpoint $x = 1$, the value never moves: $1^n = 1$ for all $n$.
The \textbf{pointwise limit} is the function you get by fixing each $x$ on its own and taking the limit of the numbers $f_1(x), f_2(x), f_3(x), \dots$. Here it works out to
\[
f(x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x) = \begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } 0 \le x < 1, \\
1 & \text{if } x = 1.
\end{cases}
\]
Every curve in the sequence is unbroken, every point has a limit, and yet the limit function has a jump at $x = 1$. Figure~\ref{fig:qo-xn-curves} shows several of the curves and their limit.

How can a sequence of unbroken curves produce a limit with a jump, and where did Cauchy's proof go wrong? To prove that the limit $f$ is continuous at an arbitrary point $x_0$, Cauchy needed to show that $|f(x) - f(x_0)|$ can be made as small as we please whenever $x$ is close enough to $x_0$. He split the gap into three hops using the \textbf{triangle inequality} from Chapter~20, here for three terms, $|a + b + c| \le |a| + |b| + |c|$: the distance from start to finish is at most the sum of the lengths of the hops in between. With the stops $f_n(x)$ and $f_n(x_0)$ in the middle, this gives
\[
|f(x) - f(x_0)| \le |f(x) - f_n(x)| + |f_n(x) - f_n(x_0)| + |f_n(x_0) - f(x_0)|.
\]
Figure~\ref{fig:qo-three-hops} draws the three hops.
The second term $|f_n(x) - f_n(x_0)|$ is small because each individual function $f_n$ is continuous. The third term $|f_n(x_0) - f(x_0)|$ is small because $f_n(x_0)$ converges to $f(x_0)$.

The trap was the first term: $|f(x) - f_n(x)|$. Cauchy chose an index $n$ large enough to make the error tiny at $x_0$, and assumed that the very same $n$ would keep the error tiny at whatever nearby test point $x$ an adversary might inspect. For $f_n(x) = x^n$ around $x_0 = 1$: at $x_0 = 1$, $f_n(1) = f(1) = 1$ for every $n$, so fixing $n = 20$ works at $x_0$. But at the nearby point $x = 0.999$ we have $f(0.999) = 0$, so
\[
|f(0.999) - f_{20}(0.999)| = |0 - 0.999^{20}| \approx 0.980.
\]
As $x$ approaches $1$, the exponent needed to push $x^n$ below a given tolerance grows without bound. For a tolerance $\eps$ between $0$ and $1$ and a point $0 < x < 1$, the condition $x^n < \eps$ holds exactly when
\[
n > \frac{\ln \eps}{\ln x}
\]
(take logarithms of both sides; dividing by $\ln x$, which is negative, flips the inequality). Figure~\ref{fig:qo-stage-needed} plots this threshold for $\eps = 0.25$: the stage $n$ must exceed $2$ at $x = 0.5$, about $13.2$ at $x = 0.9$, and about $1386$ at $x = 0.999$.

To write the two claims precisely, fix a tolerance $\eps > 0$ and let $N$ be a stage after which the error $|f_n(x) - f(x)|$ stays below $\eps$.
Cauchy had assumed that one stage serves every point:
\[
\exists N, \; \forall x, \; (|f_n(x) - f(x)| < \eps \text{ for all } n \ge N).
\]
Pointwise convergence gave him only a stage for each point separately:
\[
\forall x, \; \exists N, \; (|f_n(x) - f(x)| < \eps \text{ for all } n \ge N).
\]
The arithmetic and algebra were fine. The mistake was an invalid \textbf{quantifier swap}: exchanging $\forall x$ and $\exists N$ as if their order did not matter. (The first, stronger property is \emph{uniform convergence}, met in Chapters~4 and~20; with it, Cauchy's proof is correct.)

\subsection{Adaptive response versus universal choice}

In arithmetic, many operations are \textbf{commutative}: swapping the order does not change the result, as in $2 + 3 = 3 + 2$ and $x \cdot y = y \cdot x$. In logic, $P \land Q$ (``$P$ and $Q$'') means the same as $Q \land P$. These habits make it easy to assume that the order of symbols rarely changes whether a statement is true.

Quantifiers break this habit: reversing the order of a $\forall$ and an $\exists$ can completely change the meaning. This is the issue of \textbf{quantifier order}.

Consider these two abstract statements:
\begin{align*}
\text{Statement 1}: &\quad \forall x \in A, \; \exists y \in B, \; P(x, y), \\
\text{Statement 2}: &\quad \exists y \in B, \; \forall x \in A, \; P(x, y).
\end{align*}
They use the same sets $A$ and $B$ and the same predicate $P(x, y)$. Yet Statement 2 is much stronger: it implies Statement 1 (Exercise~6), but Statement 1 does not imply it. Reversing the order of $\forall$ and $\exists$ decides whether the response $y$ may depend on the input $x$ or must be chosen in advance for all inputs.

Figure~\ref{fig:qo-grid} draws this with a grid when $A$ and $B$ are small finite sets: one row for each $x \in A$, one column for each $y \in B$, and a dot in a cell when $P(x, y)$ is true. Statement~1 says every row contains at least one dot. Statement~2 says some single column is full of dots.

\subsection{The game-theoretic perspective}

A reliable way to keep quantifier order straight is to read every quantified statement as a turn-based game between two players, as in Chapter~4:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The Adversary} controls all universal quantifiers ($\forall$). The Adversary tries to break the claim by picking the hardest, most awkward element it can find.
\item \textbf{The Prover} controls all existential quantifiers ($\exists$). The Prover defends the claim by producing a witness: an element that makes the claim true.
\end{itemize}

The rule that matters is about timing: \textbf{a player can base a move only on choices already revealed in earlier rounds.}

\subsubsection{Analyzing the ``for all \dots\ there exists'' order}
Here, Round 1 belongs to the Adversary, who chooses $x$. Round 2 belongs to the Prover, who sees the specific $x$ chosen and responds with $y$.
Because the Prover acts second, the chosen $y$ is allowed to depend on $x$; it can be given by a rule
\[
y = g(x).
\]
Every input $x$ can have its own customized response $y$.

\subsubsection{Analyzing the ``there exists \dots\ for all'' order}
Now the roles are reversed: Round 1 belongs to the Prover, who must put a single element $y$ on the table. Only in Round 2 does the Adversary reveal $x$.
The Prover has to select $y$ without knowing which $x$ will be tested. The same $y$ must work for every $x \in A$ at once.

\begin{example}
Consider the relationship $x < y$ on the real numbers $\R$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\forall x \in \R, \; \exists y \in \R, \; x < y$: This is \textbf{true}. If the Adversary chooses $x = 100$, the Prover responds with $y = 101$. If the Adversary chooses $x = 10^{12}$, the Prover responds with $y = 10^{12} + 1$. The Prover always wins because $y$ can adapt to $x$.
\item $\exists y \in \R, \; \forall x \in \R, \; x < y$: This is \textbf{false}. The Prover must commit to a single real number $y$ that is strictly greater than every real number. Whatever $y$ the Prover announces, the Adversary chooses $x = y + 1$ and wins.
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

Figure~\ref{fig:qo-real-line} shows the two games side by side on the number line.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: What happens if we test multiplication: $x \cdot y = 1$?
\begin{itemize}
  \item Does $\forall x \in \R, \; \exists y \in \R, \; (x \cdot y = 1)$ hold? No. The Adversary plays $x = 0$, and no choice of $y$ can satisfy $0 \cdot y = 1$.
  \item What if we test the non-zero real numbers $\R \setminus \{0\}$? Here, $\forall x \in \R \setminus \{0\}, \; \exists y \in \R \setminus \{0\}, \; (x \cdot y = 1)$ is true, because the Prover can inspect $x$ and choose $y = 1/x$.
  \item But now reverse the quantifiers: does $\exists y \in \R \setminus \{0\}, \; \forall x \in \R \setminus \{0\}, \; (x \cdot y = 1)$ hold? No. A single real number $y$ cannot serve as the reciprocal of every nonzero number at the same time.
\end{itemize}
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Pointwise versus uniform continuity}

The same swap separates two kinds of continuity, a distinction at the centre of a first course in real analysis.

\begin{definition}
Let $D$ be a set of real numbers (the domain) and $f: D \to \R$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $f$ is \textbf{continuous} on $D$ if:
\[
\forall x_0 \in D, \; \forall \eps > 0, \; \exists \delta > 0, \; \forall x \in D, \; (|x - x_0| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(x_0)| < \eps).
\]
\item $f$ is \textbf{uniformly continuous} on $D$ if:
\[
\forall \eps > 0, \; \exists \delta > 0, \; \forall x_0 \in D, \; \forall x \in D, \; (|x - x_0| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(x_0)| < \eps).
\]
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The only change is that in uniform continuity the quantifier $\forall x_0 \in D$ has moved \emph{after} $\exists \delta > 0$. In the first definition, $\delta$ may depend on both $x_0$ and $\eps$; this is why ordinary continuity is also called \textbf{pointwise continuity}. In the second, $\delta$ may depend only on $\eps$, and that one $\delta$ must work at every point of $D$. Figure~\ref{fig:qo-continuity-game} shows the two definitions as games.

Why does this matter geometrically? Consider the function $f(x) = 1/x$ on the open interval $(0, 1)$ (all $x$ with $0 < x < 1$; the endpoints are left out). Figure~\ref{fig:qo-one-over-x} compares two points on its graph.
\begin{itemize}
\item Near $x_0 = 1/2$, the curve has slope $-4$. If $\eps = 0.1$, a window of $\delta \approx 0.02$ keeps output changes below $\eps$.
\item As $x_0$ approaches $0$, the graph shoots upward toward vertical. Near $x_0 = 0.001$, the slope is $-10^6$. To keep the vertical change $|f(x) - f(x_0)|$ under $0.1$, the horizontal window $\delta$ must shrink to roughly $10^{-7}$.
\item For any fixed $x_0 \in (0, 1)$, a valid $\delta(x_0, \eps)$ exists, so $f$ is continuous at every point.
\item But as $x_0 \to 0$, the required $\delta(x_0, \eps) \to 0$. There is no single $\delta > 0$ that works on the whole interval $(0, 1)$ at once. So $f(x) = 1/x$ is not uniformly continuous on $(0, 1)$.
\end{itemize}
The largest window that works at $x_0$ can be computed exactly. Solving $1/x = 1/x_0 + \eps$ for $x$ gives the left edge of the window, and the left side is the tighter one, so
\[
\delta(x_0, \eps) = x_0 - \frac{x_0}{1 + \eps x_0} = \frac{\eps x_0^2}{1 + \eps x_0}.
\]
For $\eps = 0.1$ this is about $0.024$ at $x_0 = 1/2$ and about $10^{-7}$ at $x_0 = 0.001$, matching the numbers above. Figure~\ref{fig:qo-delta-needed} plots it next to the window for the straight line $g(x) = 2x$, which is $\eps/2$ at every point.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Why linear functions are uniformly continuous}:
Consider a linear function $f(x) = mx + b$ on the entire real line $\R$. Because the slope is constant everywhere, the change in output is proportional to the change in input:
\[
|f(x) - f(y)| = |m| \cdot |x - y|.
\]
Given any tolerance $\eps > 0$, the Prover chooses $\delta = \eps / (|m| + 1)$ (or $\delta = \eps/|m|$ when $m \ne 0$). Then whenever $|x - y| < \delta$,
\begin{align*}
|f(x) - f(y)| &= |m| \cdot |x - y| \\
&\le |m|\,\delta && \text{(since } |x - y| < \delta\text{)} \\
&= \frac{|m|}{|m| + 1}\,\eps \\
&< \eps.
\end{align*}
Because the steepness is the same across the entire domain, this single $\delta$ works everywhere at once. The Prover has no need to know which numbers $x$ and $y$ the Adversary will test.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Everyday and engineering analogies}

The table pairs each ``for every \dots\ there exists'' claim with its swapped ``there exists \dots\ for every'' version. In each row the left claim is weaker and usually true; the right claim is much stronger and often false.

\begin{longtable}{p{0.18\linewidth}p{0.37\linewidth}p{0.37\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Context} & \textbf{Pointwise ($\forall \dots \exists \dots$)} & \textbf{Uniform ($\exists \dots \forall \dots$)} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Wardrobe} & For every person, there is a coat that fits them. & There is one coat that fits every person. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Software Testing} & For every possible software bug, there exists a unit test (a small automated check of one piece of code) that detects it. & There exists one unit test that catches every possible software bug. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Control Systems} & For every disturbance frequency (how fast an outside force shakes the system), the controller can be retuned to keep the system steady. & There is a single fixed controller setting that keeps the system steady at all frequencies. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Algorithms} & For every valid input $x$ of length $n$, the program stops after some finite number of steps $T(x)$. & There is a single bound $T$ such that the program stops within $T$ steps on every input of length $n$. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\begin{historybox}
The controversy over Cauchy's 1821 theorem persisted for nearly thirty years because nineteenth-century mathematicians had no symbols for quantifiers. Cauchy wrote his claims in long French sentences: when $n$ increases indefinitely, the remainder of the series becomes arbitrarily small for all values of $x$. That phrasing does not say whether the stage $n$ may depend on $x$, so the order of the choices was invisible.

In 1847 and 1848, the German mathematician Philipp Ludwig von Seidel and the British physicist Sir George Gabriel Stokes independently clarified the distinction. They showed that Cauchy's proof was valid if the convergence was uniform, but failed when convergence was only pointwise. In the following decades, Karl Weierstrass taught the $\eps$-$\delta$ and $\eps$-$N$ definitions used today, in which the order of the quantifiers is written out explicitly.

The difference between pointwise and uniform behaviour still drives research today:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In \textbf{harmonic analysis}---the study of breaking complicated signals into sums of pure sine waves---Andrey Kolmogorov constructed in 1926 a function whose Fourier series diverges (fails to converge) at \emph{every} point. His function belongs to the class $L^1$ of functions whose absolute value has a finite area under its graph, in a generalized sense of area due to Henri Lebesgue. By contrast, Lennart Carleson proved in 1966 that for every function in the smaller class $L^2$ (functions whose \emph{square} has finite area), including every continuous periodic function (one whose graph repeats at regular intervals), the Fourier series converges pointwise \emph{almost everywhere}: at all points except a set of total length zero. Uniform convergence needs stronger smoothness assumptions on the function, and the fine behaviour of Fourier series is still studied.
  \item In \textbf{reverse mathematics}---the program in mathematical logic that works backward from theorems to determine the bare minimum axioms required to prove them---uniform continuity marks a measurable jump in the axioms needed. Proving that every continuous function on a closed bounded interval $[a, b]$ is uniformly continuous requires an extra axiom called Weak K\"onig's Lemma. A \emph{binary tree} is a branching diagram in which each node splits into at most two children; the lemma says that every infinite binary tree has an infinite path from the root. Without this axiom, the theorem cannot be proved from the weaker base system that logicians use as a starting point.
  \item In \textbf{computational complexity} (the study of how much time and memory problems need), the \textbf{polynomial hierarchy} ($\mathbf{PH}$, met in Chapter~4) sorts problems into levels by how many alternations between ``there exists'' and ``for all'' are needed to state them. Whether each extra alternation always gives harder problems, or whether the levels stop growing after some point, is a major open question in theoretical computer science.
\end{itemize}
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The role of quantifier order---and the question of definitions}

Untangling quantifier order put real analysis on firmer ground. For example, uniform convergence is the condition that lets you integrate an infinite sum of functions one term at a time and get the right answer. In numerical work, the same distinction separates methods that reach a given accuracy at the same speed everywhere on a region from methods that slow down badly near its edges.

Quantifier order gives the rules for reasoning, but it does not say what the objects being reasoned about are. Nineteenth-century mathematics still relied on intuitive geometric descriptions of points, curves, and lines. Then came objects that intuition got wrong: geometries in which Euclid's parallel postulate fails (non-Euclidean geometries), and curves that are continuous but have no tangent line at any point (continuous nowhere-differentiable curves). These showed that mathematics needed exact definitions built from stated axioms.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Warm-Up: The Arithmetic Swap.}
  For each statement below, determine whether it is true or false in the specified number system. Justify your answer with the game (give the Prover's winning response or the Adversary's winning counter-move):
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $\forall x \in \R, \; \exists y \in \R, \; x + y = 0$.
    \item $\exists y \in \R, \; \forall x \in \R, \; x + y = 0$.
    \item $\forall x \in \N, \; \exists y \in \N, \; y < x$ \quad (taking $\N = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$).
    \item $\exists y \in \N, \; \forall x \in \N, \; y \le x$.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Mental Check: Translating Everyday Guarantees.}
  Consider the everyday claim: ``There is a universal key that opens every door in this building.''
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Translate this sentence into symbolic notation using quantifiers, letting $K$ be the set of keys, $D$ the set of doors, and $O(k, d)$ the predicate ``key $k$ opens door $d$.''
    \item Now swap the order of the quantifiers. Translate the resulting symbolic statement back into natural English.
    \item Explain why a building with a master key satisfies the first statement, whereas a building where every door has its own private key, and no key opens them all, satisfies only the second statement.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Direct Proof: Uniform Continuity of a Linear Map.}
  Let $f: \R \to \R$ be defined by $f(x) = 4x - 7$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Show from the definition that $f$ is uniformly continuous on all of $\R$. That is, given $\eps > 0$, construct an explicit $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x, y \in \R$,
    \[
    |x - y| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(y)| < \eps.
    \]
    \item Why does this chosen $\delta$ have no dependence on $x$ or $y$? What geometric property of straight lines ensures uniform continuity?
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Cauchy's Trap: The Shrinking Polynomial Sequence.}
  Consider the sequence of functions $f_n: [0, 1] \to \R$ defined by $f_n(x) = x^n$ for each integer $n \ge 1$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Compute the pointwise limit function $f(x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x)$ for each $x \in [0, 1]$. At which point is $f$ discontinuous?
    \item To see why uniform convergence fails, fix the tolerance $\eps = 1/2$. For each $n \ge 1$, find an explicit point $x_n \in [0, 1)$ such that $f_n(x_n) = 1/2$.
    \item Deduce that $|f_n(x_n) - f(x_n)| = 1/2$. Explain why this prevents the existence of any single stage $N$ that keeps the error $|f_n(x) - f(x)| < 1/2$ simultaneously for all $x \in [0, 1]$.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{The Squaring Function: Local Continuity versus Global Non-Uniformity.}
  Let $f: \R \to \R$ be defined by $f(x) = x^2$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Prove that $f$ is continuous at every point $x_0 \in \R$. Show that for a fixed $x_0$ and $\eps > 0$, choosing
    \[
    \delta(x_0, \eps) = \min\left\{1, \, \frac{\eps}{2|x_0| + 1}\right\}
    \]
    keeps $|f(x) - f(x_0)| < \eps$ whenever $|x - x_0| < \delta$. (\emph{Hint}: factor as below, and use $|x - x_0| < 1$ to bound the second factor.)
    \[
    |x^2 - x_0^2| = |x - x_0| \cdot |x + x_0|, \qquad |x + x_0| \le |x - x_0| + 2|x_0| < 2|x_0| + 1.
    \]
    \item Prove that $f$ is \emph{not} uniformly continuous on the whole real line $\R$. (\emph{Hint}: If someone claims a single $\delta > 0$ works everywhere, consider the test points $x = n$ and $y = n + \delta/2$ as $n \to \infty$.)
    \item Is $f(x) = x^2$ uniformly continuous on the closed bounded interval $[0, 10]$? Explain why restricting the domain to a bounded interval prevents the required $\delta$ from shrinking to zero.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{The Valid Implication: From Universal to Pointwise.}
  Let $P(x, y)$ be an arbitrary predicate on sets $A$ and $B$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Prove the logical implication:
    \[
    \bigl(\exists y \in B, \; \forall x \in A, \; P(x, y)\bigr) \implies \bigl(\forall x \in A, \; \exists y \in B, \; P(x, y)\bigr).
    \]
    Write your proof in the two-player game format: state what the premise gives you, what the adversary hands you, and what witness you play in response.
    \item Provide a concrete predicate $P(x, y)$ on the real numbers where the reverse implication:
    \[
    \bigl(\forall x \in A, \; \exists y \in B, \; P(x, y)\bigr) \implies \bigl(\exists y \in B, \; \forall x \in A, \; P(x, y)\bigr)
    \]
    fails.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
