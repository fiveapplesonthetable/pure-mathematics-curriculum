\section{Examples, Non-Examples, and Counterexamples}
\chapterline

In elementary mathematics, examples are often treated as practice exercises---routine repetitions to build speed after a formula has been introduced.

In pure mathematics, examples play a much deeper role. They are the testing laboratory in which definitions and theorems are investigated. When a mathematician encounters a new definition, their first move is not to stare passively at the symbols. They immediately probe the landscape:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Find a friendly exemplar}: What is the simplest, most transparent object that satisfies this definition?
\item \textbf{Construct a non-example}: What is an object that misses the definition by just one condition, revealing why that condition was included?
\item \textbf{Hunt for counterexamples}: If someone proposes a plausible conjecture, what minimal object breaks it?
\end{enumerate}

Look closely at the three roles highlighted in the diagram:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Standard Exemplars ($f(x) = x^2$)}: Sitting securely in the intersection of both properties, exemplars confirm that the definition is not empty. They provide a safe baseline for intuition.
\item \textbf{Counterexamples ($f(x) = |x|$)}: Sitting inside the hypothesis $H$ (continuous) but outside the conclusion $P$ (differentiable), this single function shatters the universal claim ``$\forall f$, Continuous $\implies$ Differentiable.''
\item \textbf{Non-examples ($f(x) = \operatorname{sgn}(x)$)}: Sitting outside the hypothesis, this function reminds you where the theorem's jurisdiction ends. It cannot be used as a counterexample because it fails the entrance criteria of the premise.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The art of the minimal counterexample}

When trying to test whether a mathematical statement is true, beginners often try to construct complex, exotic counterexamples. But mathematical truth is usually fragile: if a statement fails, it usually fails on the smallest, simplest possible object.

Consider the claim:
\begin{quote}
``For all real numbers $x$ and $y$, if $x^2 = y^2$, then $x = y$.''
\end{quote}
You do not need a complicated transcendental number to break this claim. You test small integers:
\[
x = 1, \quad y = -1 \implies (1)^2 = (-1)^2 = 1, \quad \text{yet } 1 \neq -1.
\]
A two-second test with the number $-1$ completely disproves the claim.

Here is a checklist of standard mathematical pressure points to test against any algebraic or analytic conjecture:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Numbers}: $0$, $1$, $-1$, negative numbers, fractions between $0$ and $1$, irrationals like $\sqrt{2}$.
\item \textbf{Sets}: the empty set $\emptyset$, singletons $\{a\}$, sets with identical elements, infinite sets.
\item \textbf{Functions}: the constant function $f(x) = 0$, the identity function $f(x) = x$, absolute value $f(x) = |x|$, piecewise jumps, $1/x$.
\item \textbf{Matrices}: the zero matrix $O$, the identity matrix $I$, projection matrices, nilpotent matrices such as $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stress-testing theorems: removing assumptions}

A powerful habit for developing mathematical maturity is to take an established theorem, delete one of its hypotheses, and construct a function or space that demonstrates why the theorem immediately fails.

Consider the \textbf{Extreme Value Theorem}:
\begin{theorem}
Let $f : [a, b] \to \R$ be a continuous function on a closed and bounded interval. Then $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum.
\end{theorem}

Notice the three distinct hypotheses: (1) closed interval, (2) bounded interval, (3) continuous function. What happens if you drop each requirement one by one?

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Drop ``closed''}: Consider the open interval $(0, 1)$ and the continuous function $f(x) = x$. As $x \to 1$, the values approach $1$, but $f(x)$ never equals $1$ because $1 \notin (0, 1)$. The function has no maximum value on $(0, 1)$!
\item \textbf{Drop ``bounded''}: Consider the unbounded interval $[0, \infty)$ and $f(x) = x$. The function grows without bound and attains no maximum.
\item \textbf{Drop ``continuous''}: Consider $f : [0, 1] \to \R$ defined by $f(x) = x$ for $x \in [0, 1)$ and $f(1) = 0$. The values climb toward $1$, but at the endpoint the function drops back to $0$. The supremum $1$ is never attained.
\end{enumerate}

Every single word in the hypothesis was an indispensable structural pillar. Removing any one of them causes the conclusion to collapse.

\begin{historybox}
In 1752, Leonhard Euler announced a mesmerizing geometric law: for any polyhedron, the number of vertices ($V$), edges ($E$), and faces ($F$) always satisfies $V - E + F = 2$. For a cube, $8 - 12 + 6 = 2$; for a tetrahedron, $4 - 6 + 4 = 2$. In 1813, Augustin-Louis Cauchy published what seemed like an airtight proof by removing one face and flattening the remaining surface onto a flat plane.

Yet over the following century, unexpected geometric ``monsters'' shattered Euler's formula. Johannes Kepler's self-intersecting star polyhedra yielded $12 - 30 + 12 = -6$. In 1813, Louis Lhuilier showed that a picture-frame polyhedron with a central tunnel gives $V - E + F = 0$, while a hollow cube nested inside another cube gives $V - E + F = 4$. 

In his 1976 masterwork \emph{Proofs and Refutations}, the philosopher Imre Lakatos showed that mathematical discovery does not proceed in a straight line from unquestioned axioms to permanent theorems. Instead, it advances through a living dialogue of bold conjectures and surprising counterexamples. Early mathematicians tried ``monster-barring''---declaring that hollow or self-intersecting shapes were ``not real polyhedra'' to defend the original equation. The true breakthrough came from inspecting where the proof had broken: Cauchy had quietly assumed that every polyhedron could be inflated like a balloon into a round sphere. By incorporating this missing topological hypothesis (that the surface has zero donut holes, or genus $g = 0$), mathematicians uncovered the deeper invariant: $V - E + F = 2 - 2g$. Counterexamples were not annoying mistakes; they were the essential chisels that shaped modern topology.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{What counterexamples unlocked---and the need for a universal container}

The discipline of seeking exemplars, non-examples, and minimal counterexamples transforms the reader from a passive memorizer of formulas into an active researcher. By testing boundary values ($0, 1, -1, \emptyset$) and stripping hypotheses one by one, you discover the exact structural joints that keep a theorem standing.

Yet testing examples on numbers, functions, and geometric polyhedra reveals a deeper need: \textbf{what is the universal substrate that holds all these objects?} What does a real number, a geometric shape, and an algebraic operation actually consist of? Without a common foundational language, every mathematical subfield operates in its own isolated dialect. 

In Chapter~10 (\emph{Sets: The Grammar of Modern Mathematics}), we introduce that universal lingua franca: set theory. We explore how Georg Cantor built a unified world out of simple collections, and how Bertrand Russell discovered a paradox that shook the foundations of human thought.

\subsection{Exercises}

For each false claim below, provide a minimal counterexample and explain which condition fails:
\begin{enumerate}
\item For all real numbers $a$ and $b$, if $ab = 0$, then $a = 0$ and $b = 0$.
\item For all sets $A, B, C$, if $A \cup B = A \cup C$, then $B = C$.
\item For all sets $A, B, C$, if $A \cap B = A \cap C$, then $B = C$.
\item For every real number $x$, if $x^2 > 4$, then $x > 2$.
\item If a real function $f$ satisfies $f'(x) = 0$ everywhere on its domain, then $f$ is constant. (Hint: What if the domain is disconnected, like $(-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)$?)
\item Every sequence of rational numbers that converges has a rational limit. (Hint: How do decimal approximations of $\sqrt{2}$ behave?)
\end{enumerate}
