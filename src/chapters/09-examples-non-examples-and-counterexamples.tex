\section{Examples, Non-Examples, and Counterexamples}
\chapterline

When an exploratory proof stalls, how do you decide whether you are stuck on a technical step or whether the claim is false? Instead of continuing to push a proof forward, mathematicians test the statement against concrete objects to see whether it holds.

In elementary mathematics, examples are often treated as routine practice exercises after a formula has been taught. In pure mathematics, examples serve a different purpose: they are the testing ground where definitions and conjectures are investigated.

In this testing, mathematical objects do three different jobs:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Exemplars}: The simplest, clearest objects satisfying a definition, confirming that the concept describes something concrete.
\item \textbf{Non-examples}: Objects that fail one condition of a definition on purpose, showing why that condition is needed.
\item \textbf{Counterexamples}: Objects that satisfy every hypothesis of a proposed statement but fail its conclusion, proving the statement false.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The geography of mathematical objects}

To see how these three categories interact, consider a historical question. A \textbf{continuous function} is one whose graph has no sudden tears or breaks, so you can sketch it without lifting your pencil from the paper. A \textbf{differentiable function} is one that is smooth enough to have a well-defined tangent line and definite slope at every point. In the early nineteenth century, many mathematicians, including Andr\'e-Marie Amp\`ere, believed that a continuous function can fail to have a slope only at a few isolated points, such as sharp corners. Start with the cruder claim ``every continuous function is differentiable.''

A proposed mathematical theorem of this kind has the logical form:
\[
\forall x, \quad \bigl(H(x) \implies C(x)\bigr).
\]
In words, for every object $x$ in our universe (the collection of all objects under discussion), if $x$ satisfies hypothesis $H$, then $x$ has the target property $C$. Picture the objects as points: the statement claims that the region of objects satisfying $H$ lies entirely inside the region of objects having $C$. As in Chapter~7, this is written $H \subseteq C$ and read ``$H$ is a subset of $C$.''

Every differentiable function on $\R$ is automatically continuous (the box below explains why), so $C \subseteq H$. The claim is therefore asking whether the two regions are the same: is continuity alone enough to force smoothness? Figure~\ref{09:fig-geography} places three test functions in the picture.

Compare the three regions in Figure~\ref{09:fig-geography}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Exemplars ($f(x) = x^2$)}: Sitting inside the target region $C$ (and therefore inside $H$), exemplars show that the concept describes actual objects, and give you a concrete case to picture.
\item \textbf{Counterexamples ($f(x) = |x|$)}: Sitting inside the hypothesis $H$ (continuous) but outside the target $C$ (differentiable), this single function disproves the universal claim. At $x = 0$, the left-hand slope is $-1$ while the right-hand slope is $+1$, so no tangent line exists (the middle panel of Figure~\ref{09:fig-three-graphs}). The function lies in $H \setminus C$ (read ``$H$ minus $C$'': the objects in $H$ that are not in $C$), and one object there is enough to show the statement is false.
\item \textbf{Non-examples of the hypothesis ($f(x) = \operatorname{sgn}(x)$)}: The sign function $\operatorname{sgn}(x)$ equals $+1$ for $x > 0$, $-1$ for $x < 0$, and $0$ at the origin. Because it jumps abruptly at $0$, it is not continuous: it sits completely outside $H$. It cannot be used to test whether $H \implies C$, because it does not satisfy the hypothesis in the first place.
\end{itemize}

The function $|x|$ does not refute Amp\`ere's weaker belief, which allowed a few isolated corners. That belief fell in 1872, when Karl Weierstrass constructed a function that is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere: a counterexample to the weaker claim, and a far stranger object than $|x|$. His function is an infinite sum of cosine waves, each one faster and smaller than the last:
\[
W(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n \cos(b^n \pi x),
\]
where $a$ is a number between $0$ and $1$ (it shrinks the heights) and $b$ is an odd whole number (it speeds up the waves). Weierstrass required $ab > 1 + \tfrac{3}{2}\pi$, for example $a = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $b = 13$; in 1916 G.~H.~Hardy showed that $ab \ge 1$ is enough, so $a = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $b = 3$ also work. Because the heights shrink, the sum settles down to a continuous function; because the waves speed up faster than they shrink, new wiggles appear at every scale and the graph has a corner-like zigzag near every point. Figure~\ref{09:fig-weierstrass} plots the first few terms of such a sum.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Could a function ever sit inside $C$ (differentiable) but outside $H$ (continuous)? No. A standard theorem of calculus says that if a function $f$ is differentiable at a point $x_0$, then
\begin{align*}
\lim_{x \to x_0} \bigl(f(x) - f(x_0)\bigr)
  &= \lim_{x \to x_0} \left( \frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0} \cdot (x-x_0) \right) \\
  &= f'(x_0) \cdot 0 \qquad \text{(the fraction tends to } f'(x_0)\text{)} \\
  &= 0,
\end{align*}
which forces $f(x) \to f(x_0)$, so $f$ is continuous at $x_0$. In our picture, the part of $C$ outside $H$ is empty.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{The art of the minimal counterexample}

When testing whether a mathematical statement is true, beginners often try to construct elaborate counterexamples. In practice, however, if a statement is false, it often fails on a simple object.

Consider the claim:
\begin{quote}
``For all real numbers $x$ and $y$, if $x^2 = y^2$, then $x = y$.''
\end{quote}
You do not need anything complicated to test this claim. Try two small numbers with opposite signs, $x = 1$ and $y = -1$:
\[
x^2 = 1^2 = 1 = (-1)^2 = y^2, \quad \text{yet} \quad x = 1 \neq -1 = y.
\]
This one pair disproves the claim. Figure~\ref{09:fig-square-pairs} shows why: the graph of $y = x^2$ is a parabola that is symmetric about the vertical axis, so every positive output is reached by two different inputs.

Similarly, consider the statement ``Every prime number is odd.'' The minimal counterexample is the number $2$. It is the only even prime, which is why so many theorems about primes begin ``for every odd prime $p$.''

\subsubsection*{The danger of trusting early patterns}
Why is a proof required rather than twenty successful examples? Consider Leonhard Euler's polynomial:
\[
P(n) = n^2 + n + 41.
\]
Test the first few whole numbers:
\begin{align*}
P(0) &= 0 + 0 + 41 = 41 && \text{(prime)}, \\
P(1) &= 1 + 1 + 41 = 43 && \text{(prime)}, \\
P(2) &= 4 + 2 + 41 = 47 && \text{(prime)}, \\
P(3) &= 9 + 3 + 41 = 53 && \text{(prime)}.
\end{align*}
If you continue testing by hand, you will find that for every integer from $n = 0$ to $n = 39$, the polynomial gives a prime number: forty successes in a row. In an experimental science, forty confirmations and no failures would count as strong evidence. (Generalizing from observed cases in this way is called \emph{inductive reasoning}.)

Yet at $n = 40$, the pattern fails:
\begin{align*}
P(40) &= 40^2 + 40 + 41 \\
      &= 40 \cdot (40 + 1) + 41 && \text{(factor $40$ out of the first two terms)} \\
      &= 40 \cdot 41 + 41 \\
      &= 41 \cdot (40 + 1) && \text{(factor out $41$)} \\
      &= 41 \cdot 41 = 1681.
\end{align*}
So $1681$ is composite (a product of two whole numbers greater than $1$), not prime. Forty successes did not guarantee the forty-first.

\subsubsection*{Common test cases}
When testing a conjecture, standard test cases often expose false statements quickly:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Numbers}: $0$ (which flattens products to zero), $1$ and $-1$ (which preserve squares while flipping signs), negative numbers (which reverse inequalities when multiplied), fractions strictly between $0$ and $1$ (where powers shrink rather than grow: $(1/2)^2 = 1/4 < 1/2$; see Figure~\ref{09:fig-shrinking-powers}), and irrational numbers like $\sqrt{2}$ (numbers that cannot be written as a fraction of integers).
\item \textbf{Sets}: the empty set $\emptyset$ (the set containing no elements; as in Chapter~5, an if-then statement about the elements of $\emptyset$ has nothing to check, so it is vacuously true), singletons $\{a\}$ (sets with exactly one member), and disjoint sets (sets that share no elements).
\item \textbf{Functions}: the constant zero function $f(x) = 0$, the identity function $f(x) = x$, absolute value $f(x) = |x|$ (which introduces a sharp corner), step functions (which jump from one constant value to another; such a break in the graph is called a \emph{jump discontinuity}), and the reciprocal $f(x) = 1/x$ (which shoots to infinity at zero).
\item \textbf{Matrices}: the zero matrix $O$ (every entry $0$), the identity matrix $I$, projection matrices (matrices satisfying $P^2 = P$, so applying one twice does the same as applying it once), and nilpotent matrices (non-zero matrices that square to the zero matrix). Two small examples, which Figure~\ref{09:fig-matrix-tests} draws as moves of points in the plane:
\[
P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
N^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The projection $P$ keeps the $x$-coordinate of a point and sets the $y$-coordinate to $0$, sending $(x, y)$ to $(x, 0)$. The nilpotent $N$ sends $(x, y)$ to $(y, 0)$, so applying it twice sends every point to $(0, 0)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stress-testing theorems: removing assumptions}

A useful practice for understanding theorems is to remove one hypothesis and construct an object showing why the theorem then fails. In a well-stated theorem each hypothesis is there for a reason, and such an object shows you the reason.

Consider the \textbf{Extreme Value Theorem}:
\begin{theorem}[Extreme Value Theorem]
Let $f : [a, b] \to \R$ be a continuous function on a closed and bounded interval. Then $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum.
\end{theorem}

Before manipulating this statement, unpack the vocabulary:
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{closed interval} $[a, b]$ is one that includes both of its boundary endpoints $a$ and $b$.
\item A \textbf{bounded interval} is one that has finite length, rather than stretching off to infinity.
\item A function \textbf{attains an absolute maximum} if there is an actual point $c \in [a, b]$ where $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$---meaning the function takes on its maximum value at some point in the domain, rather than merely approaching an upper bound. An \textbf{absolute minimum} is defined the same way with $f(c) \le f(x)$.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{09:fig-evt-holds} shows an exemplar: a continuous function on a closed, bounded interval, with its highest and lowest points marked.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Drop ``closed''}: Replace $[0, 1]$ with the open interval $(0, 1)$, which leaves out both endpoints, and choose the identity function $f(x) = x$. As $x$ approaches $1$ from the left, the outputs get arbitrarily close to $1$. But $1 \notin (0, 1)$. For any point $x \in (0, 1)$, the midpoint $(x+1)/2$ is also in $(0, 1)$ and gives a strictly higher value. The number $1$ is the \textbf{supremum} of the values (their least upper bound: the smallest number that no value exceeds), but it is not attained by any input in $(0, 1)$. The function has no maximum value.
\item \textbf{Drop ``bounded''}: Replace $[0, 1]$ with the unbounded ray $[0, \infty)$ and keep $f(x) = x$. The function grows without bound and attains no maximum.
\item \textbf{Drop ``continuous''}: Keep the closed and bounded domain $[0, 1]$, but introduce a single jump discontinuity at the right boundary:
\[
f(x) = \begin{cases} x & \text{if } 0 \le x < 1, \\ 0 & \text{if } x = 1. \end{cases}
\]
On $[0, 1)$, the values climb steadily toward $1$. But at $x = 1$ itself, the output drops to $0$. The supremum $1$ is not attained.
\end{enumerate}

Each hypothesis was necessary: removing any one of them allows the conclusion to fail.

\begin{historybox}
In 1752, Leonhard Euler announced a geometric law: for any \textbf{polyhedron}---a solid three-dimensional shape with flat polygonal faces, straight edges, and sharp corner vertices, such as a cube or pyramid---the number of vertices ($V$), edges ($E$), and faces ($F$) satisfies:
\[
V - E + F = 2.
\]
For a cube, $8 - 12 + 6 = 2$; for a tetrahedron, $4 - 6 + 4 = 2$. In 1813, Augustin-Louis Cauchy published what appeared to be an airtight proof by removing one face and flattening the remaining network of edges onto a flat two-dimensional plane (Figure~\ref{09:fig-cauchy-flatten} shows this for a cube).

Solids that break the formula soon turned up. Johannes Kepler had already described self-intersecting star polyhedra (solids whose faces pass through one another), which give $12 - 30 + 12 = -6$. In 1813, the Swiss mathematician Simon Lhuilier showed that a picture-frame polyhedron with a square tunnel through the middle gives $V - E + F = 0$, while a hollow cube floating inside another cube gives $V - E + F = 4$.

In his 1976 book \emph{Proofs and Refutations}, the philosopher Imre Lakatos used this history to argue that mathematics often advances through rounds of conjecture, counterexample, and repair, rather than in a straight line to finished theorems. Some mathematicians responded with ``monster-barring'': declaring that solids with tunnels or self-intersections were ``not real polyhedra,'' to save Euler's equation. Real progress came from finding the unstated assumption in Cauchy's proof: he had assumed that every polyhedron could be inflated like a balloon into a sphere.

Adding the missing condition gives the repair. The \textbf{genus} $g$ of a solid counts the donut-like tunnels passing through it (a cube has $g = 0$, a picture frame $g = 1$). For every polyhedron whose surface is one connected piece,
\[
V - E + F = 2 - 2g.
\]
(The cube inside a cube has two separate surfaces, each contributing $2$, which is why it gives $4$.) The quantity $V - E + F$ is the simplest example of a \textbf{topological invariant}: a number that does not change when a shape is stretched, bent, or compressed without tearing or gluing.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Why set theory provides the shared framework}

By testing extreme cases ($0, 1, -1, \emptyset$) and removing hypotheses one by one, you discover why each condition in a theorem is needed.

The objects tested in this chapter were numbers, functions, matrices, and solids, and each claim was about which objects lie inside which region. What common language connects these different kinds of mathematical objects? In Chapter~10 (\emph{Sets: The Grammar of Modern Mathematics}), we introduce set theory, the framework used throughout modern mathematics to define and organize objects.

\subsection{Exercises}

\paragraph{Quick mental checks.} For each statement below, find a counterexample:
\begin{enumerate}
\item For all real numbers $a$ and $b$, if $ab = 0$, then $a = 0$ and $b = 0$.
\item For every real number $x$, if $x^2 > 4$, then $x > 2$.
\item If a polygon in the plane has four equal sides, it must be a square.
\item For all positive integers $a, b, c$, if $a$ divides the product $bc$, then $a$ divides $b$ or $a$ divides $c$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Routine practice.} Provide a concrete counterexample for each false claim and identify precisely which condition or hypothesis fails. (In Exercises 5 and 6, the \emph{union} $A \cup B$ is the set of elements in $A$ or $B$ or both, and the \emph{intersection} $A \cap B$ is the set of elements in both $A$ and $B$.)
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item For all sets $A, B, C$, if $A \cup B = A \cup C$, then $B = C$.
\item For all sets $A, B, C$, if $A \cap B = A \cap C$, then $B = C$.
\item If a differentiable function $f : D \to \R$ satisfies $f'(x) = 0$ for every $x \in D$, then $f$ is a constant function. (Hint: What if the domain $D$ consists of two disconnected pieces, such as $(-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)$?)
\item Every sequence of rational numbers that converges in $\R$ must have a rational limit. (Hint: Consider decimal truncations of an irrational number, such as $1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, \ldots \to \sqrt{2}$.)
\item For all real numbers $x$, if $x^2 \ge x$, then $x \ge 1$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Deeper exploration.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Euler's sum-of-powers conjecture}: In 1769, Leonhard Euler conjectured that for all integers $n > 2$, it requires at least $n$ positive $n$-th powers to sum to another $n$-th power. For $n = 3$, this asserts that two cubes cannot sum to a cube (the $n=3$ case of Fermat's Last Theorem), requiring three cubes ($3^3 + 4^3 + 5^3 = 6^3$). For $n = 5$, Euler's conjecture claimed that no four fifth powers could sum to a fifth power: $a^5 + b^5 + c^5 + d^5 = e^5$ was conjectured to be impossible. In 1966, L.~J.~Lander and T.~R.~Parkin discovered the following equality:
\[
27^5 + 84^5 + 110^5 + 133^5 = 144^5.
\]
Verify by direct calculation that this identity holds. Explain why this single numerical counterexample permanently settled a conjecture that had stood for 197 years.
\item \textbf{Patterns and proof}: In 1640, Pierre de Fermat inspected the numbers $F_n = 2^{2^n} + 1$:
\[
F_0 = 3, \quad F_1 = 5, \quad F_2 = 17, \quad F_3 = 257, \quad F_4 = 65537.
\]
Observing that all five numbers are prime, Fermat conjectured that $F_n$ is prime for every non-negative integer $n$. In 1732, Leonhard Euler factored the next term:
\[
F_5 = 2^{32} + 1 = 4{,}294{,}967{,}297 = 641 \times 6{,}700{,}417.
\]
Explain the philosophical difference between scientific induction (generalizing from observed instances) and mathematical proof: why can no finite collection of examples ever prove a statement of the form $\forall n \in \N,\ S(n)$ (where $S(n)$ is a statement about $n$)?
\end{enumerate}
