\chapterline

\subsection{Beyond the readiness plan: from templates to spoken dialect}

In Chapter~26, we used explicit proof templates for direct arguments, contrapositives, and contradictions to get clean arguments onto the page.

This Second Pass is about fluency. At a blackboard, mathematicians do not stop to consult templates or fill in sixteen-row truth tables. They read a compound statement and see directly how the truth of its parts decides the truth of the whole.

The subject of this chapter is \textbf{propositional logic}: the logic of whole statements (propositions) $P, Q, R, \dots$ joined by \textbf{connectives}, the symbols $\neg$ (not), $\land$ (and), $\lor$ (or), $\implies$ (if \dots\ then) and $\iff$ (if and only if). It looks only at how statements are combined, not at what is inside them.

Writing logic down as a fully symbolic language with explicit rules is a fairly recent idea. In 1879 the German mathematician and philosopher Gottlob Frege published \emph{Begriffsschrift} (``Concept-Script''). He wanted to remove the ambiguity of ordinary language and the unstated assumptions from mathematical proofs, and so to realize Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz's earlier dream of a symbolic language in which every step of reasoning follows an explicit rule. In his later two-volume \emph{Grundgesetze der Arithmetik} (``Basic Laws of Arithmetic'') he tried to derive all of arithmetic from logical axioms.

For a student meeting formal logic for the first time, truth tables can feel like bookkeeping: rows of $\mathbf{T}$'s and $\mathbf{F}$'s, checked column by column. Mathematicians instead picture a compound statement as a \textbf{syntax tree}. The whole statement sits at the top (the \emph{root}); each connective is a branching point; the single letters $P, Q, R$ sit at the bottom (the \emph{leaves}). The tree shows which operation is applied first and which connective governs the whole statement. When parentheses are left out, a \textbf{precedence} rule decides the grouping, just as $\times$ is done before $+$ in arithmetic: $\neg$ binds tightest, then $\land$ and $\lor$, and last $\implies$ and $\iff$. So $P \land \neg Q \implies R$ means $(P \land (\neg Q)) \implies R$. To evaluate the statement, pass truth values up the tree from the leaves to the root, the way signals pass through the logic gates of a digital circuit (small components that output a 1 or a 0 depending on their inputs). Figure~\ref{27:fig-syntax-tree} shows this for one assignment of truth values.

\subsection{Translating subtle English idioms into boolean structure}

Everyday English hides logical structure inside idioms. To read mathematics fluently you need to translate them into \emph{Boolean} form, meaning a combination of true/false statements and connectives (named after George Boole, who first treated logic as algebra). The common idioms are these:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{``$A$ unless $B$''}: This means that if $B$ does not occur, $A$ must hold:
\[
\neg B \implies A \quad (\text{or equivalently, } A \lor B).
\]
For example, ``the sequence converges unless it oscillates'' means: if the sequence does not oscillate, it must converge. The left panel of Figure~\ref{27:fig-idiom-regions} shades where ``$A$ unless $B$'' is true: everywhere except outside both circles.

\item \textbf{``$P$ if $Q$'' versus ``$P$ only if $Q$''}:
\begin{itemize}
\item ``$P$ if $Q$'' means $Q \implies P$. Here $Q$ is the hypothesis: whenever $Q$ holds, $P$ is guaranteed.
\item ``$P$ only if $Q$'' means $P \implies Q$. The direction is reversed: saying ``you can pass only if you take the exam'' means that passing cannot happen without the exam ($\text{Pass} \implies \text{Exam}$). It does not say that taking the exam guarantees passing.
\item ``$P$ if and only if $Q$'' means both implications hold simultaneously: $(P \implies Q) \land (Q \implies P)$, written compactly as $P \iff Q$.
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Sufficient versus Necessary}: In any implication $P \implies Q$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $P$ is a \textbf{sufficient condition} for $Q$: having $P$ is enough on its own to guarantee $Q$.
\item $Q$ is a \textbf{necessary condition} for $P$: without $Q$, you cannot have $P$.
\end{itemize}
For a concrete memory hook: living in Kyoto is sufficient for living in Japan; living in Japan is necessary for living in Kyoto. Figure~\ref{27:fig-implication-sets} draws this as one region inside another: think of $P$ and $Q$ as the regions where each statement is true.

\item \textbf{``Neither $P$ nor $Q$''}: Both conditions fail simultaneously:
\[

\]

\item \textbf{``$P$ but $Q$''}: In everyday English, ``but'' signals surprise or contrast. In mathematics, it means the same as ``and'' ($P \land Q$): both clauses are claimed to hold (right panel of Figure~\ref{27:fig-idiom-regions}). For example, ``the function $f$ is continuous on $[0,1]$, but not differentiable at $x = 1/2$'' is the conjunction
\[
\text{($f$ is continuous on $[0,1]$)} \land \neg\,\text{($f$ is differentiable at $1/2$)}.
\]
Watch for hidden quantifiers in such sentences: ``not differentiable everywhere on $[0,1]$'' means there is at least one $x \in [0,1]$ where the derivative $f'(x)$ fails to exist, whereas ``nowhere differentiable on $[0,1]$'' makes the much stronger claim that for every $x \in [0,1]$, $f'(x)$ fails to exist.
\end{itemize}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: A sign on a library reading room says: ``You may enter only if you display a faculty badge.'' A student observes Professor Klein displaying a valid badge, but the librarian does not let her in because the room is closed for renovations. Has the rule posted on the sign been violated?

\emph{Answer}: No. Displaying a faculty badge is a \emph{necessary} condition ($\text{Enter} \implies \text{Badge}$), not a \emph{sufficient} one. The rule guarantees that anyone who enters has a badge; it does not promise that having a badge forces the doors open.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Short-circuit evaluation: why mathematicians skip truth tables}

Consider the proposition:
\[
(A \land B \land C) \implies D.
\]
A student approaching this mechanically might write out a truth table with $2^4 = 16$ rows, one for each way of assigning $\mathbf{T}$ or $\mathbf{F}$ to the four letters. Mathematicians do not write such tables. Why not?

The reason lies in when an implication is false. An implication $P \implies Q$ is false in exactly one case: when the hypothesis $P$ is true and the conclusion $Q$ is false. When $P$ is false, the implication is true automatically; Chapter~7 called this \textbf{vacuous truth}. The hypothesis of an implication is also called its \textbf{premise} or \textbf{antecedent}. Figure~\ref{27:fig-implication-table} shows the four cases.

Programmers know this trick as \textbf{short-circuit evaluation}: when a program evaluates \texttt{A and B} and finds \texttt{A} false, it stops, because the answer is already settled and \texttt{B} cannot change it. The same thing happens here. Look at $(A \land B \land C) \implies D$. How many of the 16 rows have a false premise $A \land B \land C$?
\begin{itemize}
\item If $A = \mathbf{F}$ (8 rows), the conjunction $(A \land B \land C)$ is false immediately.
\item If $A = \mathbf{T}$ but $B = \mathbf{F}$ (4 rows), the conjunction is false.
\item If $A = \mathbf{T}$ and $B = \mathbf{T}$ but $C = \mathbf{F}$ (2 rows), the conjunction is false.
\end{itemize}
Summing these gives $8 + 4 + 2 = 14$ rows where the premise is false. In all 14 of those rows the implication is $\mathbf{T}$, whatever $D$ is.

Only $16 - 14 = 2$ rows remain: those where $A$, $B$ and $C$ are all true ($D$ true in one, false in the other). In those two rows the truth of the whole statement depends only on $D$. Figure~\ref{27:fig-short-circuit} sets this count beside the shape of the proof.

This short-circuiting is why a direct proof of $P \implies Q$ begins: \emph{``Assume $P$.''} You do not need to look at the cases where $P$ is false, because the statement is already true in all of them.

\subsection{Four lenses on a single mathematical claim}

Here is one claim, looked at in four ways: as a reader, a writer, a debugger, and an algebraist looking for structure.
\[
\forall n \in \Z, \; n^2 + n \text{ is even}.
\]

\subsubsection{1. The Reader's Lens: Unpacking grammar and target}
The outer symbol is universal: $\forall n \in \Z$. A valid proof must hold for an arbitrary, unspecified integer $n$. Checking one value such as $n = 4$ proves nothing about the other integers.
The property to prove is ``$n^2 + n$ is even.'' By definition, this means:
\[
\exists k \in \Z \text{ such that } n^2 + n = 2k.
\]
The target is now concrete: rearrange $n^2 + n$ until it is visibly $2$ times an integer.

\subsubsection{2. The Writer's Lens: Crafting clear, self-contained prose}
How do we guide the reader from the arbitrary integer to the factor of $2$?
\begin{proof}
Let $n \in \Z$ be arbitrary. We can factor the expression as:
\[
n^2 + n = n(n+1).
\]
The integers $n$ and $n+1$ are consecutive. By the division algorithm, dividing $n$ by $2$ leaves remainder $0$ or $1$, so $n$ is either even or odd:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Case 1 ($n$ is even)}: There exists $m \in \Z$ such that $n = 2m$. Substituting this gives
\[
n(n+1) = 2m(n+1) = 2\bigl(m(n+1)\bigr).
\]
Since $m(n+1)$ is an integer, $n^2 + n$ is even.
\item \textbf{Case 2 ($n$ is odd)}: There exists $m \in \Z$ such that $n = 2m + 1$. Then
\[
n + 1 = (2m + 1) + 1 = 2m + 2 = 2(m+1).
\]
Substituting this factor into the product yields
\[
n(n+1) = n \cdot 2(m+1) = 2\bigl(n(m+1)\bigr).
\]
Since $n(m+1)$ is an integer, $n^2 + n$ is even.
\end{itemize}
In both cases, $n^2 + n$ is twice an integer, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{3. The Debugger's Lens: Diagnosing flawed reasoning}
Consider this common student draft:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Attempted proof}: $n^2 + n$ is even because it has two terms, one of which is even and one of which is odd, and even plus odd is always odd, wait, no, they cancel out.
\end{quote}
\textbf{Diagnosis}:
\begin{itemize}
\item The claim that $n^2$ and $n$ have opposite parity is false: if $n = 2$, both $n = 2$ and $n^2 = 4$ are even. If $n = 3$, both $n = 3$ and $n^2 = 9$ are odd.
\item In fact, $n^2$ and $n$ \emph{always} have the same parity: an odd times an odd is odd, and an even times an even is even.
\item The sum of two numbers with the same parity is always even ($\text{odd} + \text{odd} = \text{even}$, and $\text{even} + \text{even} = \text{even}$).
\end{itemize}
Fixing the draft gives a second correct proof: $n^2$ and $n$ have the same parity, so their sum is even. Figure~\ref{27:fig-parity-table} checks this for the first few values of $n$.

\subsubsection{4. The Structural Lens: Connecting to modular arithmetic}
In algebra and number theory, statements about divisibility by $2$ are written as congruences modulo $2$ (Chapter~15). This is clock arithmetic with only two marks on the dial, $0$ and $1$: two integers count as the same if they leave the same remainder when divided by $2$. Write $[a]$ for the class of all integers with the same remainder as $a$. Since
\[
n^2 + n = n(n+1),
\]
the question becomes whether $x(x+1)$ always lands in $[0]$.
The two-element system $\Z/2\Z = \{[0], [1]\}$, with addition and multiplication done modulo $2$, is a field (Chapter~24) with finitely many elements, so it is called a \emph{finite field} and written $\mathbb{F}_2$. Figure~\ref{27:fig-f2-tables} gives its addition and multiplication tables. Evaluating $x(x+1)$ at both of its elements:
\begin{align*}
[0]([0] + [1]) &= [0] \cdot [1] = [0], \\
[1]([1] + [1]) &= [1] \cdot [0] = [0].
\end{align*}

\begin{labbox}
Pick any proposition with two connectives, such as $(P \implies Q) \lor (Q \implies P)$. Draw its syntax tree, evaluate it for all four truth assignments, and see why it comes out true in every case. A formula that is true under every assignment of truth values is called a \textbf{tautology}. Then test Peirce's law (named after the logician Charles Sanders Peirce): $((P \implies Q) \implies P) \implies P$. Use the tree to find which branch forces the value $\mathbf{T}$ even when you do not know whether $Q$ holds.
\end{labbox}

\begin{historybox}
The development of formal logic led to foundational debates in the early twentieth century. In June 1902, as the second volume of Gottlob Frege's \emph{Grundgesetze} was in press, Bertrand Russell pointed out that Frege's logical system permitted unrestricted comprehension---the assumption that any predicate defines a set. Russell introduced the collection $R = \{x : x \notin x\}$ of all sets that do not contain themselves. If $R \in R$, then by definition $R \notin R$; if $R \notin R$, then $R \in R$. This contradiction, now known as Russell's paradox, showed that naive set theory required reformulation.

To resolve these paradoxes, Russell and Alfred North Whitehead developed type theory in \emph{Principia Mathematica} (1910--1913), stratifying mathematical objects into a hierarchy of types to prevent a set from containing itself.

Other responses followed. L.E.J. Brouwer founded \emph{intuitionism}, arguing that mathematical objects must be constructively created and questioning the unrestricted use of the law of excluded middle on infinite collections. In response, David Hilbert formulated the \emph{formalist program}, aiming to prove arithmetic to be both consistent (free of contradictions) and complete (capable of proving or refuting every statement).

In 1931, Kurt G\"odel proved his Incompleteness Theorems. Take any formal system that is consistent, whose axioms can be listed by a mechanical procedure, and that can express ordinary arithmetic. Then the system is incomplete: some true arithmetic statements cannot be proven within it. Nor can such a system prove its own consistency. In 1936, Alan Turing answered Hilbert's \emph{Entscheidungsproblem} (German for ``decision problem'': is there a mechanical procedure that decides, for any statement, whether it follows from given axioms?). He defined an abstract computing machine, now called a Turing machine, and proved that no such machine can solve the Halting Problem (deciding, for every program and input, whether the program eventually stops). From this the answer to Hilbert's question is no, and the result became one of the starting points of theoretical computer science.

Formal logic is still closely tied to computer science. Proof assistants such as Lean, Coq and Isabelle (programs that check every step of a proof) let mathematicians have their proofs verified by machine, using the same methods engineers use to verify software.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Exercises}

\textbf{Part A: Mental Checks and Syntax Parsing.} For each proposition, draw its syntax tree and identify its root (outermost) connective:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $(P \land \neg Q) \implies (R \lor S)$.
\item $\neg(P \implies Q) \iff (P \land \neg Q)$.
\item $(P \implies Q) \land (Q \implies R) \implies (P \implies R)$.
\item Pass truth values up the tree from Exercise 1, from the leaves to the root, under the truth assignment $P = \mathbf{T}, Q = \mathbf{T}, R = \mathbf{F}, S = \mathbf{F}$. What is the truth value at the root?
\item \emph{Mental check}: Suppose an implication $P \implies Q$ is known to evaluate to $\mathbf{F}$. What can you deduce about the truth value of $P$? What can you deduce about the truth value of $Q$?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Part B: Translating Spoken Idioms.} Translate each English statement into precise propositional formulas using the indicated letters:
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item ``The sequence $(a_n)$ is bounded unless it diverges to infinity'' ($B$: sequence is bounded, $D$: sequence diverges to infinity).
\item ``A quadrilateral is a rectangle only if its interior angles are right angles'' ($R$: quadrilateral is a rectangle, $A$: interior angles are right angles).
\item ``A function $f$ is integrable if it is continuous'' ($I$: function is integrable, meaning the area under its graph is well defined; $C$: function is continuous).
\item ``The linear system has a unique solution if and only if the matrix determinant is non-zero'' ($U$: unique solution, $D$: determinant is non-zero; the determinant is a single number computed from the entries of a square matrix, and you do not need to know how to compute it here).
\item Identify the sufficient condition and the necessary condition in the theorem: ``Every differentiable function is continuous.''
\item Explain the logical difference between the mathematical assertion ``$f$ is continuous, but not bounded'' and the compound statement ``$f$ is continuous and not bounded.''
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Part C: Short-Circuiting and Vacuous Truth.} Examine each implication and determine whether it is vacuously true, non-vacuously true, or false:
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item ``If $x$ is a positive real number such that $x^2 + 1 = 0$, then $x + 10 = 3$.''
\item ``If $n$ is an even prime integer greater than $2$, then $n$ is a multiple of $7$.''
\item In a textbook proof of Lagrange's Theorem (``If $G$ is a finite group and $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, then the order of $H$ divides the order of $G$''; here a subgroup is a subset of $G$ that is itself a group under the same operation, $H \le G$ means ``$H$ is a subgroup of $G$'', and the order of a group is its number of elements), describe which scenarios are dismissed as vacuously true by beginning the proof with ``Let $G$ be a finite group and let $H \le G$.''
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Part D: Proofs Through the Four Lenses.} Apply the four lenses (reader, writer, debugger, structural) to each claim:
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item Prove that for every integer $n$, the product $n(n+1)(n+2)$ is divisible by $6$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Reader's Lens}: State the explicit existential definition of divisibility by $6$.
\item \emph{Writer's Lens}: Write a clean, self-contained proof using properties of consecutive integers or division by cases.
\item \emph{Debugger's Lens}: Diagnose this student attempt: ``$n(n+1)$ is divisible by $2$, and $(n+1)(n+2)$ is divisible by $3$, so multiplying them together gives divisibility by $6$.''
\item \emph{Structural Lens}: Evaluate the polynomial $x(x+1)(x+2)$ at every element of $\Z/2\Z$ and of $\Z/3\Z$ (arithmetic modulo $2$ and modulo $3$).
\end{enumerate}
\item Prove that the square of any odd integer leaves a remainder of $1$ when divided by $8$. (Hint: Write $n = 2k + 1$ and consider the parity of the consecutive product $k(k+1)$).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Part E: Deeper Structural Investigations.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Peirce's Law}: Consider the classical propositional formula $((P \implies Q) \implies P) \implies P$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Evaluate the formula when $P = \mathbf{T}$ using short-circuit reasoning.
\item Evaluate the formula when $P = \mathbf{F}$.
\item Why is this formula surprising? It contains only implications ($\implies$) and uses neither disjunction ($\lor$) nor negation ($\neg$). Yet it cannot be proved in Brouwer's intuitionistic logic, which rejects the law of excluded middle; adding it to that logic brings back the full law of excluded middle $P \lor \neg P$.
\end{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Functional Completeness of the Sheffer Stroke}: A set of connectives is \emph{functionally complete} if every truth table can be produced by some formula built from those connectives alone. The NAND (``not and'') operator, also called the Sheffer stroke and written $P \mid Q$, is defined as $\neg(P \land Q)$. (Digital circuits can be built entirely from NAND gates for exactly this reason.)
\begin{enumerate}
\item Show that $\neg P \equiv P \mid P$.
\item Show that $P \lor Q \equiv (P \mid P) \mid (Q \mid Q)$.
\item Using the fact that every truth table can be written with $\neg$ and $\lor$ alone, conclude that every propositional connective can be expressed using only the Sheffer stroke.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{walkbox}
When reading your next mathematics paper or textbook chapter, find the outermost connective of every theorem. Does the theorem say ``If $\dots$ then'', ``$\dots$ if and only if $\dots$'', or ``Neither $\dots$ nor $\dots$''? The root connective tells you what the first sentence of the proof must be.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{The limits of propositional truth---and the quantifier shift}

Once you read connectives as grammar, the shape of a statement tells you how to handle it. The main connective in the syntax tree shows how a proof must open. Vacuous truth explains why a direct proof of $P \implies Q$ starts by assuming $P$ and works toward $Q$. Switching among the viewpoints of reader, writer, debugger and algebraist helps you both build arguments and find the flaws in them.

Propositional logic, though, works only with whole statements. It treats $P$ and $Q$ as indivisible units and does not look at the objects and predicates inside them. So it cannot express statements that quantify over objects, such as ``every integer has a prime factorization'' or ``there exists a unique solution.''

Chapter~28, \textbf{Second Pass: Quantifier Order in Real Problems}, takes up statements where objects and quantifiers interact, and shows how swapping the order of $\forall$ and $\exists$ changes what a statement about convergence says.
