\section{Induction: Infinite Claims, Finite Proofs}
\chapterline

Imagine standing before an infinite line of dominoes stretching past the horizon:
\[
D_1, \; D_2, \; D_3, \; D_4, \; \dots, \; D_n, \; \dots
\]
You wish to ensure that every single domino will eventually topple. 

You could walk down the line and knock each domino over by hand. But because the line is infinite, you would never finish. No matter how many millions of dominos you tip over, an endless expanse remains untouched.

Instead, you inspect the mechanics of the line and notice two local facts:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{The Initial Push:} You physically tip over the very first domino, $D_1$.
\item \textbf{The Chain Reaction:} The dominos are spaced closely enough that whenever any arbitrary domino $D_k$ falls, its momentum is guaranteed to strike and topple the next domino, $D_{k+1}$.
\end{enumerate}

With those two finite guarantees in place, you do not need to walk to infinity. The cascade carries itself. Domino $1$ topples $2$; domino $2$ topples $3$; and by inescapable logical necessity, every domino in the infinite chain falls.

This is the principle of \textbf{mathematical induction}. It is our first mathematical machine for proving an infinite sequence of statements using a finite chain of deductions.

\begin{historybox}
The formalization of mathematical induction was achieved by Blaise Pascal in his 1654 \emph{Trait\'e du triangle arithm\'etique}. Pascal was collaborating with Pierre de Fermat on the ``problem of points''---how to fairly divide the stakes in an unfinished game of chance. To calculate probabilities, Pascal needed rigorous formulas for binomial coefficients across all rows of his triangle, but he had no way to verify an infinite table one row at a time. He formulated the principle of induction: verify the first row, prove that each row forces the truth of the next, and the entire infinite triangle is conquered.

Traces of inductive reasoning had appeared earlier in the work of the 14th-century mathematician Levi ben Gershon (Gersonides) and the Sicilian mathematician Francesco Maurolico (1575), who proved that the sum of the first $n$ odd integers equals $n^2$. But Pascal made the principle explicit and general. 

Two centuries later, Giuseppe Peano (\emph{Arithmetices principia}, 1889) recognized that induction is not just a clever proof trick; it is the fifth Peano axiom defining what the natural numbers $\N$ actually are. The natural numbers are precisely the set generated from $1$ by repeatedly applying the successor operation. Induction works because there are no ``phantom'' natural numbers hiding outside the reach of the domino cascade.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The Formal Principle of Mathematical Induction}

\begin{theorem}[Principle of Mathematical Induction]
Let $P(n)$ be a predicate parameterized by integers $n \ge n_0$. Suppose that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Base Case:} $P(n_0)$ is true.
\item \textbf{Inductive Step:} For every integer $k \ge n_0$, if $P(k)$ is true, then $P(k+1)$ is true:
\[
(\forall k \ge n_0)(P(k) \implies P(k+1)).
\]
\end{enumerate}
Then $P(n)$ is true for all integers $n \ge n_0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Beginners often ask: ``In the inductive step, aren't we assuming what we are trying to prove?''

No! You are \textbf{not} assuming $P(n)$ is true for all $n$. You are proving an \emph{if-then statement}: $P(k) \implies P(k+1)$. To prove an implication $A \implies B$, standard logic allows you to temporarily adopt $A$ as a working assumption and show that $B$ follows. You are not asserting that $P(k)$ is a universal truth; you are proving that the bridge between step $k$ and step $k+1$ is structurally sound.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{A Worked Example: Gauss's Triangular Sum}

Consider the classical formula for the sum of the first $n$ positive integers:
\[
1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
\]
Before diving into induction, pause to see the geometry. If you arrange dots in a triangular staircase of height $n$, you can fit two such staircases together to form an $n \times (n+1)$ rectangle containing $n(n+1)$ dots. Half of that rectangle gives the formula.

Now let us prove this rigorously for every $n \ge 1$ using induction.

\begin{theorem}
For all integers $n \ge 1$,
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n i = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $P(n)$ be the statement that $\sum_{i=1}^n i = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.

\textbf{Base Case ($n = 1$):}
For $n = 1$, the left-hand side is simply the single term $1$.
The right-hand side is
\[
\frac{1(1 + 1)}{2} = \frac{1(2)}{2} = 1.
\]
Both sides equal $1$, so the base case $P(1)$ is verified.

\textbf{Inductive Step:}
Let $k \ge 1$ be an arbitrary integer. Assume that $P(k)$ is true, meaning
\[
1 + 2 + \dots + k = \frac{k(k+1)}{2}. \tag{Induction Hypothesis}
\]
We must prove that $P(k+1)$ holds, which means establishing:
\[
1 + 2 + \dots + k + (k+1) = \frac{(k+1)((k+1)+1)}{2} = \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}.
\]

We begin with the left-hand side of $P(k+1)$ and isolate the sum of the first $k$ terms:
\[
\underbrace{1 + 2 + \dots + k}_{= \frac{k(k+1)}{2} \text{ by hypothesis}} + (k+1).
\]
Substituting our induction hypothesis:
\[
1 + 2 + \dots + k + (k+1) = \frac{k(k+1)}{2} + (k+1).
\]
Now we combine terms over a common denominator of $2$:
\begin{align*}
\frac{k(k+1)}{2} + \frac{2(k+1)}{2} 
&= \frac{k(k+1) + 2(k+1)}{2} \\
&= \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}.
\end{align*}
This is precisely the formula required by $P(k+1)$.

Having verified the base case and established that $P(k) \implies P(k+1)$ for all $k \ge 1$, the Principle of Mathematical Induction implies that the formula holds for all integers $n \ge 1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Induction With Inequalities}

Induction is not limited to algebraic sums; it frequently establishes asymptotic bounds.

\begin{theorem}
For all integers $n \ge 1$, $2^n \ge n + 1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{Base Case ($n = 1$):} For $n = 1$, the left side is $2^1 = 2$ and the right side is $1 + 1 = 2$. Since $2 \ge 2$, the base case holds.

\textbf{Inductive Step:} Assume $2^k \ge k + 1$ for an arbitrary integer $k \ge 1$. We must prove $2^{k+1} \ge (k+1) + 1 = k + 2$.

Notice that $2^{k+1} = 2 \cdot 2^k$. Applying the induction hypothesis:
\[
2^{k+1} = 2 \cdot 2^k \ge 2(k + 1) = 2k + 2.
\]
Because $k \ge 1$, we certainly have $2k \ge k + 1$ (or simply $2k + 2 = k + (k + 2) > k + 2$). Thus:
\[
2^{k+1} \ge 2k + 2 = k + k + 2 \ge 1 + k + 2 > k + 2.
\]
Therefore $2^{k+1} \ge (k+1) + 1$, completing the inductive step.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The Four Cardinal Mistakes of Induction:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Neglecting the base case:} Without an anchor, an inductive bridge proves nothing. (For example, $n = n + 1 \implies n+1 = (n+1) + 1$ is a valid conditional bridge, but $n = n+1$ is false for all numbers because no base case exists!)
\item \emph{Assuming $P(k+1)$:} You must assume the previous case $P(k)$ and deduce $P(k+1)$, never assume the target.
\item \emph{Proving the inductive step for only one value:} The step must hold for an \emph{arbitrary} integer $k \ge n_0$.
\item \emph{Hiding the hypothesis:} If your proof of $P(k+1)$ never uses $P(k)$, you are either doing direct algebra or your logic has a hole. Always explicitly flag where the induction hypothesis is invoked.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{P\'olya's Horse Fallacy:}
To see how a subtle flaw in the induction step can prove absurdities, consider George P\'olya's famous puzzle: ``Prove that all horses have the same color.''
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Base Case ($n=1$):} In any set containing a single horse, every horse in the set shares the same color, because there is only one horse.
\item \emph{Inductive Step:} Assume that in any set of $k$ horses, all horses have the same color. Now take any set of $k+1$ horses, numbered $\{h_1, h_2, \dots, h_{k+1}\}$. Remove horse $h_{k+1}$; the remaining $k$ horses $\{h_1, \dots, h_k\}$ all have the same color by hypothesis. Now put $h_{k+1}$ back and remove $h_1$; the remaining $k$ horses $\{h_2, \dots, h_{k+1}\}$ also have the same color. Since these two groups overlap, all $k+1$ horses must have the same color!
\end{itemize}
Where does this argument collapse? Look at the transition from $k=1$ to $k+1=2$! When $k=1$, the first set is $\{h_1\}$ and the second set is $\{h_2\}$. They do \emph{not} share an overlapping middle horse! The bridge from $1$ to $2$ never existed. A single broken link ruins the entire infinite cascade.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{What Induction Unlocked, Its Limits, and Where We Go Next}

Mathematical induction gives finite human beings a way to master infinity:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Infinite theorems in two checks:} You can prove summation formulas, divisibility laws, and algebraic inequalities across all infinitely many natural numbers in just a few lines of deductive logic.
\item \textbf{Program loop invariants:} In computer science, an induction proof on loop counter $k$ proves that an algorithm maintains its safety invariant at every iteration.
\item \textbf{Asymptotic scaling:} In analysis, induction bounds growth rates (such as $2^n > n$), establishing convergence criteria for power series.
\end{itemize}

Yet ordinary induction has an honest, severe bottleneck: it is strictly \textbf{single-step and local}. 

It requires that an object of size $k+1$ is constructed by adding a single unit onto an object of size $k$. But what happens when an object does not reduce to its immediate predecessor? 
\begin{itemize}
\item In prime factorization, $12 = 3 \times 4$. Knowing that $11$ has a prime factorization is completely useless for factoring $12$!
\item In recursive divide-and-conquer algorithms (like merge sort), a problem of size $n$ splits into two subproblems of size $n/2$, not $n-1$.
\item In geometry, triangulating a polygon of $k+1$ vertices cuts the shape into two smaller polygons of arbitrary sizes, neither of which is necessarily of size $k$.
\end{itemize}

When subproblems jump backward by more than one step, ordinary induction fails. We need an induction machine that does not balance on a single rung, but rests on the entire cumulative bedrock of everything proven so far. In Chapter~18, we introduce \textbf{Strong Induction and Recursive Thinking}, unlocking the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic and the correctness of recursive computation.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Prove that the sum of the first $n$ odd positive integers equals $n^2$:
\[
1 + 3 + 5 + \dots + (2n - 1) = n^2 \quad \text{for all } n \ge 1.
\]
(Draw a geometric square of dots to see why adding each subsequent L-shaped border of odd size creates the next square!)
\item Prove by induction that $3 \mid (4^n - 1)$ for all integers $n \ge 1$.
\item Prove that $n! > 2^n$ for all integers $n \ge 4$. Where does the base case begin?
\item Prove Bernoulli's Inequality: for every real number $x > -1$ and all integers $n \ge 1$,
\[
(1 + x)^n \ge 1 + nx.
\]
\end{enumerate}
