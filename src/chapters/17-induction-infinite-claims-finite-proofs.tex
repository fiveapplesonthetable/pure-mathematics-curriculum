\section{Induction: Infinite Claims, Finite Proofs}
\chapterline

Imagine an infinite line of dominoes:
\[
D_1, \; D_2, \; D_3, \; D_4, \; \dots, \; D_n, \; \dots
\]
You want to be sure that every domino will eventually fall.

You could walk down the line and knock each domino over by hand. But the line is infinite, so you would never finish. However many millions you tip over, infinitely many are still standing.

Instead, you look at how the line is set up and check two facts, each about only one or two dominoes:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{The Initial Push:} You tip over the first domino, $D_1$, by hand.
\item \textbf{The Chain Reaction:} The dominoes are spaced closely enough that whenever any domino $D_k$ falls, it strikes and topples the next domino, $D_{k+1}$.
\end{enumerate}

With those two guarantees in place, every domino in the infinite chain falls: domino $1$ topples $2$, domino $2$ topples $3$, and the pattern continues for every positive integer (Figure~\ref{17:fig-domino-cascade}).

This is the principle of \textbf{mathematical induction}. It is our main tool for proving an infinite list of statements (one for each $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$) with a proof of finite length.

\begin{historybox}
Blaise Pascal used and described mathematical induction in his 1654 \emph{Trait\'e du triangle arithm\'etique} (``Treatise on the arithmetical triangle''). Pascal was working with Pierre de Fermat on the ``problem of points'': how to divide the stakes fairly in a game of chance that is stopped before it ends. To calculate probabilities, Pascal needed proven formulas for the binomial coefficients (the counts $\binom{n}{k}$ of ways to choose $k$ items from $n$, which form the rows of his triangle) in every row, but he had no way to check an infinite table one row at a time. He formulated the principle of induction: verify the first row, prove that each row forces the truth of the next, and the entire table is established.

Traces of inductive reasoning had appeared earlier in the work of the 14th-century mathematician Levi ben Gershon (Gersonides) and the Sicilian mathematician Francesco Maurolico (1575), who proved that the sum of the first $n$ odd integers equals $n^2$. But Pascal made the principle explicit and general. 

Two centuries later, Giuseppe Peano (\emph{Arithmetices principia}, 1889) listed a few basic rules, called axioms, that are accepted without proof and define the natural numbers $\N$. Induction is the fifth of them. In Peano's description, the natural numbers are exactly the numbers you reach from $1$ by repeatedly applying the successor operation, the step from $n$ to $n+1$. Induction works because there are no natural numbers outside the list $1, 2, 3, \dots$ produced this way.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The Formal Principle of Mathematical Induction}

To turn the domino picture into a proof method, we replace dominoes with mathematical statements.

Recall from Chapter~2 that a \textbf{predicate} is an open statement containing a variable slot, such as $P(n): \text{``}2^n \ge n+1\text{''}$. By itself, a predicate is neither true nor false; it becomes a statement with a truth value only when a specific number is plugged into the slot.

In the theorem below, $n_0$ is the starting integer: the first value of $n$ the claim is about (often $n_0 = 1$, but it can be any integer).

\begin{theorem}[Principle of Mathematical Induction]
Let $P(n)$ be a predicate about integers $n \ge n_0$. Suppose that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Base Case:} $P(n_0)$ is true.
\item \textbf{Inductive Step:} For every integer $k \ge n_0$, if $P(k)$ is true, then $P(k+1)$ is true:
\[
(\forall k \ge n_0)\,\bigl(P(k) \implies P(k+1)\bigr).
\]
\end{enumerate}
Then $P(n)$ is true for all integers $n \ge n_0$.
\end{theorem}

The assumption ``$P(k)$ is true'' that you make inside the inductive step is called the \textbf{induction hypothesis}.

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Mental Check:} Suppose you verify the base case at $n_0 = 4$, and you prove that $P(k) \implies P(k+1)$ for all $k \ge 4$. Does this theorem guarantee that $P(2)$ is true?
\emph{Answer:} No. The dominoes fall only forward from the base case. Dominoes before the starting push ($n < n_0$) are untouched, and nothing has been proved about them (Figure~\ref{17:fig-start-at-four}).
\end{walkbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
A common question when first learning induction is: ``In the inductive step, aren't we assuming what we set out to prove?''

No. You are not claiming that $P(n)$ is true for every $n$. You are proving an implication: if $P(k)$ holds, then $P(k+1)$ holds.

In the inductive step, you assume $P(k)$ for an arbitrary $k \ge n_0$, as a ``suppose'', and use that assumption to deduce $P(k+1)$. What you have proved is the conditional statement $P(k) \implies P(k+1)$, not $P(k)$ itself. The base case supplies the one case you know outright, $P(n_0)$, and the implication then carries the truth forward to every later integer.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{A Worked Example: Gauss's Triangular Sum}

Consider the classical formula for the sum of the first $n$ positive integers, named after Carl Friedrich Gauss, who by a well-known story found it as a schoolboy:
\[
1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
\]
There is a picture behind it. Write $S_n$ for the sum $1 + 2 + \dots + n$. Arrange $S_n$ dots in a staircase of $n$ rows, with $i$ dots in row $i$ (such counts are called \emph{triangular numbers}, because the dots form a triangle). Two such staircases fit together to form a rectangle of $n$ rows and $n+1$ columns, containing $n(n+1)$ dots. One staircase is half of that rectangle (Figure~\ref{17:fig-gauss-staircase}).

The picture shows why the formula is true for $n = 5$. Induction proves it for every integer $n \ge 1$. (Below, $\sum_{i=1}^n i$ is the sum $1 + 2 + \dots + n$, as in Chapter~3.)

\begin{theorem}
For all integers $n \ge 1$,
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n i = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $P(n)$ be the statement that $\sum_{i=1}^n i = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.

\textbf{Base Case ($n = 1$):}
For $n = 1$, the left-hand side is the single term $1$.
The right-hand side is
\[
\frac{1 \cdot (1 + 1)}{2} = \frac{1 \cdot 2}{2} = 1.
\]
Both sides evaluate to $1$, so the base case $P(1)$ is verified.

\textbf{Inductive Step:}
Let $k \ge 1$ be an arbitrary integer. Assume that $P(k)$ is true, meaning
\[
1 + 2 + \dots + k = \frac{k(k+1)}{2}. \tag{Induction Hypothesis}
\]
We must prove that $P(k+1)$ holds, which means establishing:
\[
1 + 2 + \dots + k + (k+1) = \frac{(k+1)((k+1)+1)}{2} = \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}.
\]

We begin with the sum of the first $k+1$ terms and separate off the sum of the first $k$ terms, which the induction hypothesis tells us about. Then, instead of multiplying everything out, we take out the common factor $(k+1)$:
\begin{align*}
1 + 2 + \dots + k + (k+1)
&= \underbrace{(1 + 2 + \dots + k)}_{\text{first } k \text{ terms}} + (k+1) \\
&= \frac{k(k+1)}{2} + (k+1) && \text{(induction hypothesis)} \\
&= (k+1)\left(\frac{k}{2} + 1\right) && \text{(take out the factor } k+1\text{)} \\
&= (k+1) \cdot \frac{k+2}{2} \\
&= \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}.
\end{align*}
This is the formula demanded by $P(k+1)$.

Having verified the base case and established that $P(k) \implies P(k+1)$ for all $k \ge 1$, we conclude by the Principle of Mathematical Induction that the formula holds for all integers $n \ge 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Mental Check:} In the inductive step above, why did we peel off the last term $(k+1)$ before doing any algebra?
\emph{Answer:} The only thing we know is the induction hypothesis, and it speaks only about the sum of the first $k$ terms. Separating those $k$ terms from the rest creates a place where the hypothesis can be used. In the staircase picture, the staircase for $k+1$ is the staircase for $k$ with one new bottom row of $k+1$ dots (Figure~\ref{17:fig-gauss-step}).
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{Induction With Inequalities}

Induction also proves inequalities, such as statements that one quantity grows at least as fast as another.

When proving an equality by induction, both sides must match exactly. With inequalities, the inductive step often produces a quantity larger than needed, and that is fine: the goal is to keep every estimate pointing in the right direction ($\ge$ stays $\ge$), not to keep equality.

Figure~\ref{17:fig-two-power-growth} plots both sides of the next theorem, $2^n$ and $n + 1$, for $n = 1, \dots, 6$. They are equal at $n = 1$. After that, each step doubles the left side but adds only $1$ to the right side, so the gap keeps widening. The inductive step turns this observation into a proof.

\label{17:fig-two-power-growth}

\begin{theorem}
For all integers $n \ge 1$, $2^n \ge n + 1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $P(n)$ be the inequality $2^n \ge n + 1$.

\textbf{Base Case ($n = 1$):} For $n = 1$, the left side is $2^1 = 2$ and the right side is $1 + 1 = 2$. Since $2 \ge 2$, the base case $P(1)$ holds.

\textbf{Inductive Step:} Let $k \ge 1$ be an arbitrary integer. Assume that $P(k)$ is true:
\[
2^k \ge k + 1. \tag{Induction Hypothesis}
\]
We must prove $P(k+1)$: that $2^{k+1} \ge (k+1) + 1 = k + 2$.

We start with the left side of our target inequality and write $2^{k+1}$ in terms of $2^k$. Because $2 > 0$, multiplying both sides of the induction hypothesis by $2$ keeps the inequality. Then we split $2k + 2$ as $k + k + 2$ to bring out the target $k + 2$; because $k \ge 1$, the extra term $k$ is at least $1$:
\begin{align*}
2^{k+1} &= 2 \cdot 2^k \\
&\ge 2(k + 1) && \text{(induction hypothesis, multiplied by } 2\text{)} \\
&= k + k + 2 \\
&\ge 1 + k + 2 && \text{(since } k \ge 1\text{)} \\
&= k + 3 \\
&> k + 2.
\end{align*}
Since $2^{k+1} > k + 2$, it follows that $2^{k+1} \ge k + 2$. This completes the inductive step.

By the Principle of Mathematical Induction, $2^n \ge n + 1$ for all integers $n \ge 1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Induction on Divisibility Statements}

A third common use of induction is proving that an expression is always divisible by some number.

Recall the definition from Chapter~6: for integers $a$ and $b$, we say $a \mid b$ ($a$ divides $b$) if there exists an integer $m$ such that $b = a \cdot m$.

To prove by induction that an expression is divisible by $d$ for every $n$, a reliable strategy is:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \textbf{State the Hypothesis:} Write the $k$-th expression as $d \cdot m$ for some integer $m$.
\item \textbf{Expand the Target:} Write out the $(k+1)$-th expression.
\item \textbf{Isolate the Hypothesis:} Regroup the expanded terms to separate out the $k$-th expression.
\item \textbf{Substitute and Factor:} Replace the $k$-th expression with $d \cdot m$, and factor $d$ out of the remaining terms.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{theorem}
For all integers $n \ge 1$, $3$ divides $n^3 + 2n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $P(n)$ be the predicate that $3 \mid (n^3 + 2n)$.

\textbf{Base Case ($n = 1$):}
For $n = 1$, the expression evaluates to:
\[
1^3 + 2 \cdot 1 = 1 + 2 = 3 = 3 \cdot 1.
\]
Because $3$ is an integer multiple of $3$, the base case $P(1)$ is verified.

\textbf{Inductive Step:}
Let $k \ge 1$ be an arbitrary integer. Assume $P(k)$ is true: there exists an integer $m$ such that
\[
k^3 + 2k = 3m. \tag{Induction Hypothesis}
\]
We must prove $P(k+1)$: that $(k+1)^3 + 2(k+1)$ is an integer multiple of $3$.

We expand $(k+1)^3 + 2(k+1)$. Splitting $5k = 2k + 3k$ then isolates the expression $k^3 + 2k$ from our induction hypothesis and leaves multiples of $3$ behind. Finally we replace $k^3 + 2k$ by $3m$:
\begin{align*}
(k+1)^3 + 2(k+1)
&= (k^3 + 3k^2 + 3k + 1) + (2k + 2) && \text{(expand)} \\
&= k^3 + 3k^2 + 5k + 3 \\
&= \underbrace{(k^3 + 2k)}_{=\,3m} + (3k^2 + 3k + 3) && \text{(split } 5k = 2k + 3k\text{)} \\
&= 3m + 3(k^2 + k + 1) && \text{(induction hypothesis)} \\
&= 3\bigl(m + k^2 + k + 1\bigr).
\end{align*}
Because $m$ and $k$ are integers, $q = m + k^2 + k + 1$ is an integer. Thus $(k+1)^3 + 2(k+1) = 3q$, proving that $3 \mid ((k+1)^3 + 2(k+1))$.

By the Principle of Mathematical Induction, $3 \mid (n^3 + 2n)$ for all integers $n \ge 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Common Mistakes in Induction Proofs:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Omitting or mishandling the base case:} Without a verified base case, an inductive implication proves nothing. For instance, the implication $n = n + 1 \implies n+1 = (n+1) + 1$ is true (add $1$ to both sides), yet $n = n+1$ is false for every integer: there is no base case to start the chain.
\item \emph{Assuming the target $P(k+1)$:} You must assume the earlier case $P(k)$ and deduce $P(k+1)$. Assuming $P(k+1)$ at the outset is circular: it assumes what you are trying to prove.
\item \emph{Proving the inductive step for only a specific number:} Proving $P(1) \implies P(2)$ and $P(2) \implies P(3)$ does not prove the theorem. The inductive step must be established for an \emph{arbitrary} integer $k \ge n_0$.
\item \emph{Hiding or ignoring the induction hypothesis:} If your proof of $P(k+1)$ never uses $P(k)$, then either you have a direct proof (which did not need induction in the first place) or your argument has a gap. Always say explicitly where the induction hypothesis is used.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{P\'olya's Horse Fallacy}

A small flaw in the inductive step can ``prove'' absurd things. The Hungarian mathematician George P\'olya gave a well-known example: ``Prove that all horses have the same color.''
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Base Case ($n=1$):} In any set of one horse, all the horses have the same color, because there is only one horse.
\item \emph{Inductive Step:} Assume that in any set of $k$ horses, all horses have the same color. Now consider any set of $k+1$ horses, numbered $\{h_1, h_2, \dots, h_{k+1}\}$. 
Remove horse $h_{k+1}$; the remaining $k$ horses $\{h_1, h_2, \dots, h_k\}$ have the same color by our induction hypothesis. 
Now put $h_{k+1}$ back and remove $h_1$; the remaining $k$ horses $\{h_2, \dots, h_{k+1}\}$ also have the same color. 
Because the two groups share horses, all $k+1$ horses must have the same color.
\end{itemize}

Where does this argument break? Look at the step from $k=1$ to $k+1=2$, shown in Figure~\ref{17:fig-horse-fallacy}.
\end{trapbox}

When $k = 1$, the first group is $\{h_1\}$ and the second group is $\{h_2\}$. Their intersection is empty:
\[

\]

For $k \ge 2$ the two groups do share horses and that step is fine, but the chain of dominoes is broken between the first and second, so induction proves nothing beyond $P(1)$.

\subsection{The Reach and Limits of Induction}

Mathematical induction proves statements about all natural numbers:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Infinitely many statements from two proofs:} A base case and an inductive step together prove summation formulas, divisibility facts, and inequalities for every natural number.
\item \textbf{Algorithm correctness via loop invariants:} A \emph{loop invariant} is a condition that is true before a program's loop starts and stays true after each pass through the loop (for example, ``after $k$ passes, the variable \texttt{total} holds the sum of the first $k$ numbers''). Proving that it stays true is an induction on the pass number $k$.
\item \textbf{Growth bounds:} Inequalities such as $2^n > n$, proved by induction, are basic tools in analysis (the study of limits, Chapter~20), where they are used to compare how fast sequences grow.
\end{itemize}

Ordinary induction has one limitation: each case may use only the case \emph{immediately} before it.

The inductive step assumes $P(k)$ and nothing else, so it fits problems where the case $k+1$ is built from the case $k$ by one small step. But what happens when a case depends on cases further back?
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Multiplicative Factorization:} When factoring $12 = 3 \times 4$, neither $3$ nor $4$ is $11$. Knowing that $11$ can be factored into primes gives no information about how to factor $12$ (Figure~\ref{17:fig-one-step-vs-many}).
\item \textbf{Divide-and-Conquer Algorithms:} A divide-and-conquer algorithm solves a problem by splitting it into smaller copies of itself. Merge sort sorts a list by sorting its two halves and then merging them; binary search finds an entry in a sorted list by checking the middle and continuing in one half. In both, an input of size $n$ leads to subproblems of size about $n/2$, not size $n-1$.
\item \textbf{Recursive Sequences:} In the Fibonacci sequence $F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1}$, calculating the next value requires the two previous values, not just one.
\item \textbf{Cutting Polygons into Triangles:} A diagonal of a polygon is a segment joining two corners that are not next to each other. Cutting a polygon with $k+1$ corners along a diagonal that lies inside it gives two polygons, each with fewer than $k+1$ corners, but neither need have exactly $k$.
\end{itemize}

When a case depends on cases more than one step back, ordinary induction does not apply directly. We need an induction principle that assumes all previous cases $P(1), P(2), \dots, P(k)$.

Chapter~18, \textbf{Strong Induction and Recursive Thinking}, sets up that principle. With it we prove the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (every integer greater than $1$ is a product of primes in only one way, apart from order) and analyze algorithms that call themselves on smaller inputs.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Mental Check (Base Case Location):} Test the inequality $2^n > n^2$ for small positive integers:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Evaluate both sides for $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$. For which values does the inequality hold, and for which does it fail?
\item Identify the smallest integer $n_0$ where the inequality holds permanently.
\item Prove by induction that $2^n > n^2$ for all integers $n \ge 5$. (Where in your inductive step do you use $k \ge 5$? Hint: compare $2k^2 = k^2 + k^2$ with $(k+1)^2 = k^2 + 2k + 1$ by checking that $k^2 \ge 5k > 2k + 1$ for $k \ge 5$.)
\end{enumerate}

\]
(Draw a $k \times k$ square of dots and see how adding an L-shaped outer border of $2k + 1$ dots expands it into a $(k+1) \times (k+1)$ square, as in Figure~\ref{17:fig-odd-squares}.)

\item \textbf{Divisibility Practice:} Prove by induction that $3 \mid (4^n - 1)$ for all integers $n \ge 1$. (Follow the four-step strategy: state the hypothesis $4^k - 1 = 3m$, write $4^{k+1} - 1 = 4 \cdot 4^k - 1$, and hunt for the expression $4^k - 1$.)

\item \textbf{Factorial Growth:} Recall from Chapter~13 that $n! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots n$ (``$n$ factorial''); for example $4! = 24$. Prove by induction that $n! > 2^n$ for all integers $n \ge 4$.

\item \textbf{Bernoulli's Inequality:} Let $x$ be a real number with $x > -1$ and $x \neq 0$. Prove by induction that for all integers $n \ge 2$,
\[
(1 + x)^n > 1 + nx.
\]
Where in your proof do you rely on the assumption that $1 + x > 0$?

\item \textbf{Geometric Challenge (L-Tromino Tiling):} An L-tromino is a tile made of three $1 \times 1$ squares joined in an L-shape. A $2^n \times 2^n$ grid of squares has exactly one $1 \times 1$ square removed at an arbitrary position. Prove by induction on $n \ge 1$ that the remaining $2^{2n} - 1$ squares can always be completely covered without overlap by L-trominoes.
\emph{Hint:} Divide the $2^{k+1} \times 2^{k+1}$ grid into four $2^k \times 2^k$ quadrants. Exactly one quadrant contains the missing square. Place a single L-tromino at the central intersection of the other three quadrants so that it covers one corner of each (Figure~\ref{17:fig-tromino-hint}).
\end{enumerate}
