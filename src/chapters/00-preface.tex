\section*{How This Book Should Be Read}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{How This Book Should Be Read}

This book does not skim over advanced topics. It works through the mechanics of proof one step at a time.

We spend a long time on the foundations---statements, variables, quantifiers (the phrases ``for all'' and ``there exists''), negations, definitions, and sets---because everything later is built from them. Without a secure grasp of these tools, real analysis (the careful study of limits and continuity, the ideas behind calculus) can seem obscure, abstract algebra (the study of symmetry and of operations like addition and multiplication in general) can appear disconnected from familiar arithmetic, and topology (the study of properties of shapes that survive stretching and bending without tearing) can seem purely abstract.

When the foundations are clear, each of these subjects grows out of asking precise questions about mathematical objects. Figure~\ref{00:fig-two-approaches} contrasts the two ways of working that this book moves between: guessing from calculations, and proving from definitions.

\subsection*{The anatomy of a mathematical concept}

In this text, we resist the urge to hurry into formal proofs before the picture is settled in your mind. Whenever a major concept arrives, you will encounter it through a deliberate sequence:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The geometric or tactile picture}: What does this look like if you draw it, walk through it, or treat it as a game?
\item \textbf{The minimal formal definition}: What exact requirements are packed into the vocabulary, and why is every single word necessary?
\item \textbf{Examples and deliberate non-examples}: What lives inside the concept, and what sits just outside its boundary?
\item \textbf{Notation decoded}: What is the shorthand saying in plain human terms, and what common reading traps does it set?
\item \textbf{Complete, unhurried proofs}: Every algebraic and logical step is written out and explained; nothing is skipped.
\item \textbf{Walking questions}: Prompts designed to be turned over in your head while away from your desk.
\end{itemize}

\begin{focusbox}
Measure your progress through this book by your ability to work with the concepts independently: after reading an explanation, close the book, take a blank sheet of paper, and verify whether you can state the definitions precisely and explain the reasoning behind the proofs.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection*{The working rule for this text}

When you encounter a new definition, test it immediately against concrete examples. Find a simple object that satisfies it, find a close non-example that fails one condition, and ask what would change if a condition were omitted.

The theorems of advanced mathematics describe how definitions interact, so the better you know the definitions, the easier proofs become to follow and to find.

Figure~\ref{00:fig-working-rule} runs these three tests on the definition of an even integer, which the box below examines more closely.

It helps to picture a definition as a machine that inspects each candidate and either accepts or rejects it (Figure~\ref{00:fig-definition-machine}).

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Consider the definition of an \emph{even integer}: an integer $n$ (written $n \in \Z$, meaning $n$ belongs to the set of integers $\Z = \{\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\}$) for which there exists an integer $k$ such that $n = 2k$. Test this machine on three borderline cases before reading further:
\begin{itemize}
\item Is $0$ even? Check the machine: does an integer $k$ exist with $0 = 2k$? Yes, $k = 0 \in \Z$, so $0$ passes.
\item Is $-6$ even? Check the machine: $-6 = 2(-3)$, and $-3 \in \Z$, so $-6$ passes.
\item Is $2.4$ even? The definition requires $n \in \Z$; $2.4$ is not an integer, so the condition fails immediately.
\end{itemize}
A formal definition settles questions that everyday intuition might leave uncertain.
\end{recoverybox}

The machine for ``even'' works in two stages: first it checks that the candidate is an integer at all, then it checks the condition $n = 2k$.

\subsection*{From measurement to deductive proof}

Using rules found by trial and measurement, together with arithmetic, Egyptian and Babylonian scribes computed canal volumes, granary capacities, and calendar cycles with notable practical accuracy.

Beginning around the sixth century BCE, Greek mathematicians pursued a different goal: explaining why geometric relationships must hold in general. Consider an isosceles triangle (a triangle with two sides of equal length). A surveyor can measure its base angles with a protractor, verify their equality across many drawings, and observe that the base angles match.

Why, then, did Euclid construct a formal \emph{proof}?

Because a protractor inspects a single physical drawing. It is subject to eyesight and the limits of the instrument. It can confirm that the base angles match within a fraction of a degree, but it cannot establish that the difference is strictly zero. More fundamentally, measuring a thousand triangles cannot guarantee that every possible isosceles triangle shares the same property.

Deductive proof shifts the focus from measurement to structure (Figure~\ref{00:fig-measurement-vs-proof}). Name the corners of the triangle $A$, $B$, $C$, with the two equal sides $AB = AC$ meeting at the top corner $A$. The base is $BC$, and the base angles are $\angle B$ and $\angle C$ (the symbol $\angle B$ means the angle at corner $B$; $\angle BAD$ means the angle at $A$ between the lines to $B$ and to $D$). Instead of measuring the base angles, draw the line from $A$ that bisects $\angle A$, that is, splits it into two equal halves, and let $D$ be the point where it meets the base. Then $\angle BAD = \angle CAD$. Now compare the left triangle $\triangle ABD$ and the right triangle $\triangle ACD$: they have $AB = AC$ (given), $\angle BAD = \angle CAD$ (by construction), and the shared side $AD = AD$. Two triangles are \emph{congruent}, written $\triangle ABD \cong \triangle ACD$, when they have the same shape and size, so that one can be placed exactly on top of the other. The Side-Angle-Side rule says that two triangles that match in two sides and the angle between those sides are congruent. So the two halves are exact mirror images, and matching corners have equal angles. The equality $\angle B = \angle C$ does not depend on measurement precision; it follows deductively from those three structural matches.

\begin{historybox}
The limits of physical measurement became sharp with the Pythagorean discovery of incommensurability (quantities that share no common measuring unit). The early Pythagoreans believed every geometric relationship was a ratio of whole numbers. When measuring the diagonal of a unit square against its side, they expected a fine enough unit to divide evenly into both. Instead, deductive proof revealed that no such unit exists: the ratio is $\sqrt{2}$, an irrational number. (A \emph{rational} number is one that can be written as a fraction $a/b$ of integers; an \emph{irrational} number is one that cannot.) Legend says Hippasus of Metapontum was drowned at sea for revealing this result. Physical measurement produces approximations like $1.414$ or $1.41421356$, but only deductive proof could show that the ratio is not a fraction.

Euclid's breakthrough in the \emph{Elements} (c.~300 BCE) was to replace physical measurement with an axiomatic system: a short list of \emph{axioms} (statements accepted without proof as starting points) from which everything else is deduced. Starting from a few definitions and five geometric axioms, which he called postulates (such as ``a straight line can be drawn between any two points''), each proposition (proved statement) was deduced step by step.

Even so, Euclid's system left gaps. His synthetic geometry (geometry built directly from axioms without coordinates) relied implicitly on diagrams---for instance, assuming without proof that two circles must intersect if their centers are close enough. In the nineteenth century, mathematicians discovered that Euclid's Fifth Postulate, the parallel postulate (through a point not on a given line there is exactly one line parallel to it), can be replaced by a different rule without producing any contradiction; the resulting systems are called non-Euclidean geometries. In 1899, David Hilbert supplied a full set of axioms for Euclid's geometry that fills the gaps. In 1931, Kurt G\"odel showed that no axiomatic system for arithmetic whose axioms can be listed by a mechanical procedure and that is \emph{consistent} (never proves a contradiction) can prove every true statement of arithmetic.

Figure~\ref{00:fig-timeline} places these events in order.
\end{historybox}

The proof that the diagonal of a unit square (a square with sides of length $1$) cannot be expressed as a ratio of whole numbers shows the difference between physical approximation and deduction (Figure~\ref{00:fig-incommensurability-diagonal}). By the Pythagorean theorem the diagonal $d$ satisfies $d^2 = 1^2 + 1^2 = 2$, so $d = \sqrt{2}$. The argument is a \emph{proof by contradiction}: assume the opposite of what you want to show, and deduce something impossible. Here we assume $\sqrt{2} = p/q$ for integers $p$ and $q$, written in lowest terms. Lowest terms means $p$ and $q$ share no common factor greater than $1$, written $\gcd(p, q) = 1$ (their greatest common divisor is $1$); any fraction can be put in lowest terms by cancelling common factors. The arrow $\implies$ reads ``implies.'' The key step is about parity (whether an integer is even or odd). Squaring any odd integer $2k + 1$, where $k$ is an integer, gives
\begin{align*}
(2k + 1)^2 &= 4k^2 + 4k + 1 \\
&= 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1,
\end{align*}
which is twice an integer plus one, so it is odd. Figure~\ref{00:fig-odd-square} shows the same computation as areas. Therefore, whenever a square $p^2$ is even, the integer $p$ itself must be even. The diagram uses this fact twice, once for $p$ and once for $q$.

\subsection*{What beginner-friendly means here}

Here, beginner-friendly means that every logical step is explained, while the mathematics stays fully precise. Each concept starts with a concrete motivation and a picture, and then leads to a precise definition and to deductions you can check line by line.

In Chapter~1 (\emph{The Change in Game: From Getting Answers to Giving Reasons}), we make this transition concrete. We will examine a claim that holds for every number up to about nine hundred million before abruptly failing, and see why numerical calculation cannot substitute for deductive proof.

\subsection*{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Laser measurement versus deductive proof.} Suppose you build a physical triangle out of machined steel rods whose lengths agree to within one micrometer ($10^{-6}\,\text{m}$). A laser sensor measures the three angles and reports $59.999^\circ$, $60.001^\circ$, and $60.000^\circ$. Have you proven that an equilateral triangle has equal angles? What does a deductive proof provide that the physical sensor can never deliver?

\item \textbf{The machine test: why $1$ is not prime.} A student argues: ``Since $1$ is only divisible by $1$ and itself, $1$ must be a prime number.'' Mathematicians define a prime number as an integer $p > 1$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$. Why is the requirement $p > 1$ deliberately included in the definition machine? (Hint: Consider what happens to the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic---which states that every integer greater than $1$ factors into prime numbers in exactly one way---if $1$ were counted as a prime.)

\item \textbf{Boundary testing on a definition machine.} Consider the definition: ``An integer $n$ is \emph{composite} if there exist integers $a > 1$ and $b > 1$ such that $n = ab$.'' Test each candidate number against this machine and state whether it is accepted, rejected, or outside the domain of definition:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $n = 12$
\item $n = 7$
\item $n = 1$
\item $n = 0$
\item $n = -6$
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Testing claims on numbers.} Consider the claim: ``For every real number $x$, if $x > 0$, then $x^2 > x$.''
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Find two numbers that satisfy this claim.
\item Test the boundary value $x = 1$. Does $1^2 > 1$?
\item Test the value $x = 1/2$. Does $(1/2)^2 > 1/2$?
\item Describe the exact collection of positive real numbers for which the claim $x^2 > x$ is true.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Incommensurability and proof.} In your own words, explain why the discovery that the diagonal of a unit square cannot be written as a fraction of integers ($a/b$) could never have been made using bronze calipers or numerical tables alone. What role did deductive contradiction play in uncovering this fact?
\end{enumerate}

\begin{walkbox}
Consider a mathematical formula you frequently use, such as the quadratic formula or the Pythagorean theorem. Can you derive it from basic definitions and facts, or do you rely on it as a memorized procedure? What definitions and intermediate steps are required to turn that formula into a complete deductive proof?
\end{walkbox}
