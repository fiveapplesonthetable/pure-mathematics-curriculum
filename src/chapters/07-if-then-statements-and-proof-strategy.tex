\section{If-Then Statements and Proof Strategy}
\chapterline

In the previous chapter, we treated definitions as precise classification tools: given an object, they tell us whether it belongs to a class. In mathematics, we connect these conditions into chains of consequence:
\[
P \implies Q.
\]
We call this an \textbf{implication}: a conditional promise that whenever condition $P$ is satisfied, outcome $Q$ is guaranteed to follow. The statement $P$ is called the \textbf{hypothesis} (the starting assumption), and $Q$ is called the \textbf{conclusion} (the promised result).

When you are asked to prove an implication, beginners often make an understandable initial mistake: they look at $P$ and wonder whether it is true in the real world. But an implication never asserts that $P$ is true; it makes a conditional promise:
\begin{center}
\itshape ``In any mathematical scenario where $P$ happens to hold, $Q$ is guaranteed to hold as well.''
\end{center}
Think of an airline boarding pass: ``If you hold a ticket for Seat 14B, you may board the plane.'' The airline is not claiming that you hold such a ticket. They are stating a binding rule: hand them a ticket for 14B, and a seat on the plane is guaranteed.

\subsection{Implication as geometric containment}

A picture of sets makes an implication easy to see. Let $U$ be the universe of all possible scenarios, let $P$ be the set of scenarios where the hypothesis holds, and let $Q$ be the set of scenarios where the conclusion holds.

Recall that a set $S$ is a \textbf{subset} of a set $A$, written $S \subseteq A$, when every element of $S$ is also an element of $A$: for every object $x$, if $x \in S$, then $x \in A$. The assertion $P \implies Q$ means exactly that \textbf{$P$ is a subset of $Q$} ($P \subseteq Q$). Figure~\ref{07:fig-implication-containment} draws this as an \textbf{Euler diagram}: each condition is a region, and drawing one region entirely inside another means that every scenario in the inner region also lies in the outer one.

The diagram shows how truth behaves in each region:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Region 1 ($P$ is true, $Q$ is true)}: You stand inside $P$. Because the boundary of $P$ is nested entirely inside $Q$, you are automatically inside $Q$. For example, if $n$ is a multiple of $4$, then $n$ is automatically even. Both hypothesis and conclusion hold; the promise is fulfilled.
\item \textbf{Region 2 ($\neg P$ holds, $Q$ is true)}: You stand inside $Q$, but outside $P$. For example, the number $6$ is even, but it is not a multiple of $4$. Has the promise ``if a multiple of $4$, then even'' been broken? No. The promise said nothing about what happens to numbers that are not multiples of $4$.
\item \textbf{Region 3 ($\neg P$ holds, $\neg Q$ holds)}: You stand outside both $P$ and $Q$. For example, $7$ is neither a multiple of $4$ nor even. Again, no promise was broken.
\item \textbf{The forbidden breach ($P$ is true, $\neg Q$ holds)}: This would be a point inside $P$ but outside $Q$---for example, an integer that is a multiple of $4$ yet odd. If even a single such point existed, the implication $P \implies Q$ would be false. Saying that $P \implies Q$ is true means this region contains no points.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Vacuous truth: promises about empty conditions}

The fact that $P \implies Q$ is considered true whenever $P$ is false is known as \textbf{vacuous truth}: a conditional promise counts as true because its starting condition is never satisfied. ``Vacuous'' means ``empty''; the promise is true because there is nothing it could fail on. This rule can feel strange at first. Consider the statement:
\begin{quote}
``If an integer is both a multiple of $4$ and an odd number, then that integer is purple.''
\end{quote}
Is this statement true? In mathematical logic, the answer is \textbf{yes}.

Why? Because an integer that is simultaneously a multiple of $4$ and odd does not exist (in Figure~\ref{07:fig-number-strip}, the forbidden box has no members). The hypothesis describes the empty set $\emptyset$, the unique set containing no elements. To catch someone breaking an if-then promise, you must bring forward a real counterexample that fulfills premise $P$ while violating conclusion $Q$. Because no one can ever produce an odd multiple of $4$, no one can ever catch the promise in a breach.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose an instructor announces: ``If you score $105\%$ on the final exam, you will receive an A+.'' You take the exam, earn $88\%$, and receive a B. Did the instructor break their promise or lie to you?

\emph{Answer:} No. The promise was conditioned on scoring $105\%$. Because you never fulfilled that condition, the instructor had no obligation to award the A+, and no breach of contract occurred. In mathematics, unbroken promises are classified as true.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{historybox}
Around 300 BCE, two Greek philosophers of the Megarian school---a circle of thinkers based in the city of Megara who studied logic puzzles and paradoxes---started a debate over conditionals that lasted for more than two thousand years. Philo of Megara and his teacher Diodorus Cronus asked a direct question: what does it mean for a promise of the form ``If $P$, then $Q$'' to be true when the condition $P$ does not happen?

Philo proposed an answer that surprised his contemporaries: an implication is true in every case except when the premise $P$ is true and the outcome $Q$ is false. Under Philo's rule, whenever $P$ is false, the implication is automatically true. Modern logicians call this \emph{material implication}: the rule that judges a conditional only by whether its parts are true or false, without requiring any real-world cause connecting them. Diodorus Cronus and later Chrysippus (a leading philosopher of the Stoic school, another Greek school that studied logic) objected, arguing that a genuine conditional requires a causal or logical bond between its parts. To them, calling ``If $2 + 2 = 5$, then the Moon is made of green cheese'' true went against common sense. Much later, Bertrand Russell was challenged to show that a false premise implies anything. His reply: if $1 = 0$, adding $1$ to both sides gives $2 = 1$; the collection containing Bertrand Russell and the Pope has two members, but $2 = 1$, so the collection has one member, and Russell and the Pope are the same person.

Gottlob Frege in 1879, and later Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead in \emph{Principia Mathematica} (1910), adopted Philo's material implication as the right rule for formalizing mathematics. Because it does not depend on human feelings about whether $P$ is ``relevant'' to $Q$, proofs can be checked as mechanical manipulations of symbols. It is also what makes basic facts of set theory work: without vacuous truth, we could not prove that the empty set is a subset of every set ($\emptyset \subseteq A$).
\end{historybox}

In set theory, vacuous truth is the exact reason why the empty set $\emptyset$ is a subset of every set:
\[
\emptyset \subseteq A \quad \text{for every set } A.
\]
Recall the definition of subset: $S \subseteq A$ means that for every object $x$, if $x \in S$, then $x \in A$. When $S = \emptyset$, the hypothesis ``$x \in \emptyset$'' is false for every object in the mathematical universe. The implication is vacuously true for every object, and the containment holds without exception.

\subsection{The trap of the converse and inverse}

Once you have an implication $P \implies Q$, there are three other conditional statements that can be formed by rearranging and negating its pieces:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The Converse ($Q \implies P$)}: Swap the hypothesis and conclusion.
\item \textbf{The Inverse ($\neg P \implies \neg Q$)}: Negate both sides without swapping.
\item \textbf{The Contrapositive ($\neg Q \implies \neg P$)}: Swap both sides \emph{and} negate both sides.
\end{enumerate}

To see how these differ, let us test them on a simple arithmetic statement where $P$ is ``$x = 3$'' and $Q$ is ``$x^2 = 9$'', working with real numbers:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Original ($P \implies Q$)}: ``If $x = 3$, then $x^2 = 9$.'' \\
\emph{Status:} \textbf{True.} $3^2 = 9$.
\item \textbf{Converse ($Q \implies P$)}: ``If $x^2 = 9$, then $x = 3$.'' \\
\emph{Status:} \textbf{False.} If $x^2 = 9$, $x$ could equal $-3$.
\item \textbf{Inverse ($\neg P \implies \neg Q$)}: ``If $x \neq 3$, then $x^2 \neq 9$.'' \\
\emph{Status:} \textbf{False.} Setting $x = -3$ satisfies $x \neq 3$, yet $x^2 = 9$.
\item \textbf{Contrapositive ($\neg Q \implies \neg P$)}: ``If $x^2 \neq 9$, then $x \neq 3$.'' \\
\emph{Status:} \textbf{True.} If the square is not $9$, the number could not have been $3$.
\end{itemize}

Two statements are \textbf{logically equivalent} when they are true in exactly the same situations, so that proving one proves the other. We write $A \iff B$ (read ``$A$ if and only if $B$'') to say that $A$ and $B$ are equivalent. The table below records which of the four forms are equivalent to the original.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Statement Form} & \textbf{Symbolic Logic} & \textbf{Truth Relation to $P \implies Q$} \\
\midrule
Original Implication & $P \implies Q$ & The baseline claim \\
Contrapositive & $\neg Q \implies \neg P$ & \textbf{Logically equivalent} (identical truth value) \\
Converse & $Q \implies P$ & \textbf{Independent statement} (may be true or false) \\
Inverse & $\neg P \implies \neg Q$ & \textbf{Equivalent to converse} (independent of original) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The inverse ($\neg P \implies \neg Q$) is the contrapositive of the converse ($Q \implies P$). That is why the inverse and converse always share the same truth value. Neither of them, however, has any logical obligation to agree with the original statement.

Figure~\ref{07:fig-square-of-conditionals} arranges the four statements in a square: diagonal pairs are logically equivalent, while each single horizontal or vertical move produces a statement that need not agree with the one it came from.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The Converse Fallacy}: One of the most common errors in all of mathematics is confusing an implication with its converse. In casual conversation, people say ``If it is raining, the sidewalk is wet'' and loosely assume that a wet sidewalk implies rain. Yet a sprinkler could have caused it. Never assume that proving $P \implies Q$ gives you any license to use $Q \implies P$.
\end{trapbox}

What happens when an implication and its converse are \emph{both} true? In that case, the two statements are logically equivalent, and we write
\[
P \iff Q,
\]
read ``$P$ if and only if $Q$'' and often shortened to ``$P$ iff $Q$''. A statement of the form $P \iff Q$ is called a \textbf{biconditional}: a two-way promise. In the picture of sets, $P \implies Q$ puts $P$ inside $Q$ and $Q \implies P$ puts $Q$ inside $P$; both together force the two regions to be the same set, as Figure~\ref{07:fig-biconditional} shows.

Whenever you see a theorem framed as ``$P$ if and only if $Q$,'' you are being given two separate mathematical tasks:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Forward direction ($\implies$)}: Assume $P$, deduce $Q$.
\item \textbf{Backward direction ($\impliedby$)}: Assume $Q$, deduce $P$ (or prove its contrapositive: assume $\neg P$, deduce $\neg Q$).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} State the converse of: ``If a number is divisible by $10$, then it is divisible by $5$.'' Is the converse true?

\emph{Answer:} The converse is: ``If a number is divisible by $5$, then it is divisible by $10$.'' This converse is false: for instance, $15$ is divisible by $5$, but not by $10$. The original implication holds, but the converse fails.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Three standard proof strategies}

There are three standard ways to prove an implication $P \implies Q$, summarized in Figure~\ref{07:fig-three-strategies}. \subsection{Strategy 1: The direct route and the forward-backward method}

In a direct proof, you take premise $P$ as given and deduce conclusion $Q$ through a chain of lawful steps. But how do you find those steps when staring at a blank page?

Beginners often imagine that mathematicians discover proofs in a single forward pass, writing down line 1, then line 2, all the way to the end. In reality, finding a proof is often a two-directional process: the \textbf{forward-backward method}.

Before writing the final proof, split your scratchpad into two columns:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The Forward Column (What do I know?)}: Start with hypothesis $P$. Unpack its definitions into concrete algebraic tools. Ask: \emph{``What facts can I extract immediately from this starting point?''}
\item \textbf{The Backward Column (What do I need?)}: Look at conclusion $Q$. Unpack what it would take to justify $Q$. Ask: \emph{``What statement, if I could prove it, would be sufficient to declare $Q$ true?''}
\item \textbf{Connecting the Two Sides}: Manipulate the forward equations until they match the form required by the backward target. Once the two ends connect, write down the final proof from start to finish, presenting a clean deductive argument.
\end{enumerate}

Here is the method on an example. Recall that an integer $n$ is \textbf{even} if $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$; similarly, $n$ is \textbf{odd} if $n = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$.

\begin{theorem}
Let $n \in \Z$. If $n$ is odd, then $5n + 3$ is even.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Scratchwork and Discovery]
\textbf{Forward:} The hypothesis is that $n$ is odd. Unpacking the definition of odd gives:
\[
n = 2k + 1 \quad \text{for some integer } k.
\]
\textbf{Backward:} The target conclusion is that $5n + 3$ is even. Unpacking the definition of even tells us our destination: we must show that
\[
5n + 3 = 2m \quad \text{for some integer } m.
\]
\textbf{Connecting the two sides:} We substitute the forward expression $n = 2k + 1$ into $5n + 3$:
\begin{align*}
5n + 3 &= 5(2k + 1) + 3 && \text{(substitute } n = 2k + 1\text{)}\\
&= 10k + 5 + 3 && \text{(expand)}\\
&= 10k + 8\\
&= 2(5k + 4). && \text{(factor out } 2\text{)}
\end{align*}
Because $k$ is an integer, $5k + 4$ is an integer: sums and products of integers are always integers. (This fact is called \textbf{closure} of the integers under addition and multiplication: doing these operations never takes you outside the integers.) Setting $m = 5k + 4$ delivers the exact form $2m$ demanded by the definition of an even integer.
\end{proof}

Now we write the polished formal proof:

\begin{proof}
Assume $n$ is an odd integer.

By definition of an odd integer, there exists an integer $k$ such that $n = 2k + 1$. Substituting this expression into $5n + 3$, we have
\begin{align*}
5n + 3 &= 5(2k + 1) + 3\\
&= 10k + 8\\
&= 2(5k + 4).
\end{align*}
Let $m = 5k + 4$. Since $k \in \Z$, the quantity $m$ is an integer by closure of the integers under addition and multiplication. 

Thus, $5n + 3 = 2m$ for an integer $m$. By definition, $5n + 3$ is even.
\end{proof}

The final proof contains only the forward chain: unpack the definition of odd, do the algebra, check closure, and match the definition of even. The backward column guided the search but does not appear in the write-up.

\subsection{Strategy 2: Contrapositive proof}

Direct proof works well when the hypothesis $P$ gives concrete algebraic formulas to manipulate. But what happens when the hypothesis is awkward to unpack?

Consider the following theorem:
\begin{theorem}
Let $n \in \Z$. If $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.
\end{theorem}

Let us attempt a direct proof. We assume $n^2$ is even. Unpacking the definition gives:
\[
n^2 = 2k \quad \text{for some integer } k.
\]
Now we want to deduce that $n$ is even. To isolate $n$, we take square roots:
\[
n = \pm\sqrt{2k}.
\]
Here the direct approach stalls. What does $\sqrt{2k}$ tell us about whether $n$ is a multiple of $2$? Nothing we can see: squaring an integer is straightforward, but taking square roots leaves divisibility unclear.

So we switch to the \textbf{contrapositive}:
\[
\neg Q \implies \neg P.
\]
Recall that for any statements $P$ and $Q$, the implication $P \implies Q$ is logically equivalent to its contrapositive:
\[
(P \implies Q) \iff (\neg Q \implies \neg P).
\]
Look back at Figure~\ref{07:fig-implication-containment}. To say that $P$ is inside $Q$ is the same as saying that if you stand outside $Q$, you are guaranteed to be standing outside $P$. Writing $Q^c$ and $P^c$ for the \textbf{complements} of $Q$ and $P$ (everything in $U$ outside $Q$, and everything in $U$ outside $P$), this reads
\[
P \subseteq Q \quad \text{exactly when} \quad Q^c \subseteq P^c.
\]
If you are outside the big region, you cannot be inside the small region it contains. If a bedroom is inside an apartment, someone standing outside the apartment is guaranteed not to be in the bedroom. Figure~\ref{07:fig-contrapositive-complement} draws the two readings side by side.

Now let us apply the contrapositive to our theorem. The conclusion is ``$n$ is even,'' so its negation $\neg Q$ is ``$n$ is not even'' (meaning $n$ is odd). The hypothesis is ``$n^2$ is even,'' so its negation $\neg P$ is ``$n^2$ is odd.'' The contrapositive claim is:
\begin{center}
\itshape ``If $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd.''
\end{center}
Instead of starting with $n^2$ and taking square roots, we start with $n$ and square it. The algebraic direction is straightforward. Figure~\ref{07:fig-parity-squares} shows the pattern the proof will confirm.

\begin{proof}
We prove the theorem by contrapositive: if $n$ is not even, then $n^2$ is not even.

Dividing any integer by $2$ leaves remainder $0$ or $1$, so an integer that is not even (not of the form $2k$) is odd (of the form $2k + 1$). Assume $n$ is odd. By definition, there exists an integer $k$ such that $n = 2k + 1$. Squaring both sides gives
\begin{align*}
n^2 &= (2k + 1)^2\\
&= 4k^2 + 4k + 1 && \text{(expand)}\\
&= 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1. && \text{(factor } 2 \text{ out of the first two terms)}
\end{align*}
Because $k$ is an integer, $m = 2k^2 + 2k$ is an integer by closure under addition and multiplication. Thus $n^2 = 2m + 1$, which means $n^2$ is odd.

We have established that if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd. Because an implication is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, it follows that if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ must be even.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Choosing a proof strategy: direct, contrapositive, or contradiction}

When proving an implication, which strategy should you choose? Figure~\ref{07:fig-strategy-choice} turns the guidelines below into a sequence of questions.

\centering

    font=\small,

    answer/.style={draw=#1!80!black, fill=#1!12, rounded corners=4pt, thick, text width=3.6cm, inner sep=6pt, align=center},

\node[question] (q1) at (0, 0) {Does $P$ unpack into formulas\\that lead toward $Q$?};

\node[question] (q2) at (0, -2.3) {Is $\neg Q$ easier to unpack than $P$?\\(For example, $Q$ says ``not'', as in\\$x \neq 0$ or ``$x$ is irrational''.)};
\node[answer=sourceblue] (a2) at (7.4, -2.3) {\textbf{Contrapositive}\\\footnotesize assume $\neg Q$, deduce $\neg P$};
\node[answer=trapred, text width=6.2cm] (a3) at (0, -4.7) {\textbf{Proof by contradiction}\\\footnotesize assume $P$ and $\neg Q$, reach some $R$ and $\neg R$};

\draw[flow] (q2) -- node[above] {yes} (a2);

\label{07:fig-strategy-choice}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Use Direct Proof} when hypothesis $P$ provides clear algebraic formulas (such as $n$ is odd, $a \mid b$, or $x, y \in \Q$) that you can unpack and substitute directly into the conclusion. If the forward path moves naturally toward $Q$, direct proof is the shortest route.
\item \textbf{Use Contrapositive} when:
\begin{itemize}
\item The conclusion $Q$ is simpler to unpack than hypothesis $P$ (for example, starting from $n$ is far easier than starting from $n^2$).
\item The conclusion contains a negative assertion, such as ``$x$ is irrational'' or ``$x \neq 0$.'' Its negation $\neg Q$ is then a positive statement ($x \in \Q$ or $x = 0$) that gives you immediate formulas to manipulate.
\end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Use Proof by Contradiction} when neither direct nor contrapositive paths offer a foothold. Direct and contrapositive proofs start from what is given and deduce consequences forward. Proof by contradiction assumes both the hypothesis and the negation of the conclusion ($P \land \neg Q$), reasoning from there until reaching a contradiction ($R \land \neg R$).
\end{itemize}

Contradiction is especially useful when a theorem makes a negative claim with no algebraic hypothesis to unpack---such as proving that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, or that there are infinitely many prime numbers.

Chapter~8 (\emph{Proof by Contradiction: Power and Danger}) treats this method in detail, including its common pitfalls.

\subsection{Exercises}

\subsubsection*{Part A: Warm-Up Mental Checks}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Evaluate the truth value of each implication:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $2 + 2 = 5$, then $3 \times 3 = 9$.
\item If $2 + 2 = 4$, then $3 \times 3 = 10$.
\item If $2 + 2 = 5$, then $3 \times 3 = 10$.
\end{enumerate}
\item For each statement, write down its converse, inverse, and contrapositive:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If an integer $n$ is a multiple of $8$, then $n$ is a multiple of $4$.
\item If a real number $x$ satisfies $x > 3$, then $x^2 > 9$.
\end{enumerate}
\item Identify the logical error in the following argument: ``Every differentiable function is continuous. Since $f(x) = \lvert x \rvert$ is continuous at $x = 0$, it must be differentiable at $x = 0$.''
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part B: Routine Practice}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item State the contrapositive and the negation of each implication:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $x > 2$, then $x^2 > 4$.
\item If a triangle is equilateral (all three sides equal), then it is equiangular (all three angles equal).
\item If an integer $n$ is divisible by $6$, then $n$ is divisible by $2$ and by $3$.
\end{enumerate}
\item Prove each statement using a direct proof. Explicitly label where you unpack definitions and where you package your conclusion:
\begin{enumerate}
\item For $n \in \Z$, if $n$ is even, then $3n^2 - 5n + 6$ is even.
\item For $a, b, c \in \Z$, if $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$, then $a \mid (2b + 3c)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part C: Contrapositive Proofs}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item Prove each statement by contrapositive:
\begin{enumerate}
\item For $n \in \Z$, if $3n + 2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd.
\item For real numbers $x, y$, if $x + y > 10$, then $x > 5$ or $y > 5$.
\item For an integer $n$, if $n^2 - 1$ is not divisible by $4$, then $n$ is even.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part D: Deeper Challenges}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{A Biconditional Theorem:} Let $n \in \Z$. Prove that $n$ is even if and only if $n^2$ is even. (Explicitly present both the forward direction $\implies$ and the backward direction $\impliedby$, noting where the direct route and the contrapositive route each apply.)
\item \textbf{Rational and Irrational Numbers:} Recall that a real number $r$ is rational ($r \in \Q$) if $r = a/b$ for integers $a, b$ with $b \neq 0$, and irrational otherwise. Prove that if $x$ is irrational and $r$ is a non-zero rational number, then $r \cdot x$ is irrational. (Why does the contrapositive make working with irrational numbers more direct?)
\item If $x$ is irrational, prove that $-x$ is irrational.
\end{enumerate}
