\section{If-Then Statements and Proof Strategy}
\chapterline

The vast majority of mathematical theorems are packaged as implications:
\[
P \implies Q.
\]
We call $P$ the \textbf{hypothesis} (or premise), and $Q$ the \textbf{conclusion}.

When you are asked to prove an implication, beginners often make a dangerous initial mistake: they wonder whether $P$ is true. But the theorem does not claim that $P$ is true! It makes a conditional promise:
\begin{center}
\itshape ``In any mathematical reality where $P$ happens to hold, $Q$ is guaranteed to hold as well.''
\end{center}

\subsection{Implication as geometric containment}

The cleanest way to visualize an implication is through the geometry of sets. Let $U$ be the universe of all possible scenarios, let $P$ be the set of scenarios where the hypothesis holds, and let $Q$ be the set of scenarios where the conclusion holds.

The assertion $P \implies Q$ means precisely that \textbf{$P$ is a subset of $Q$} ($P \subseteq Q$).

Notice what this diagram reveals about truth:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Region 1 ($P$ is true, $Q$ is true)}: You stand inside $P$. Because $P$ is inside $Q$, you are automatically inside $Q$. The contract is fulfilled.
\item \textbf{Region 2 and Region 3 ($P$ is false)}: You stand outside $P$. Whether $Q$ is true (Region 2) or false (Region 3), you have never breached the contract! The implication $P \implies Q$ is \textbf{True}.
\item \textbf{The Forbidden Region ($P$ is true, $Q$ is false)}: This would be a point that sits inside $P$ but outside $Q$. The truth of $P \implies Q$ is the ironclad promise that this forbidden region contains zero points.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Vacuous truth: promises about empty conditions}

The fact that $P \implies Q$ is considered true whenever $P$ is false is known as \textbf{vacuous truth}. Beginners often find this unsettling. Consider the statement:
\begin{quote}
``If an integer is both a multiple of $4$ and an odd number, then that integer is purple.''
\end{quote}
Is this statement true? In formal logic, the answer is an unqualified \textbf{yes}. 

\begin{historybox}
Around 300 BCE, two Greek philosophers of the Megarian school---Philo of Megara and his teacher Diodorus Cronus---sparked a debate over conditional statements that echoed for more than two millennia. Working with everyday speech, they asked a deceptively simple question: what does it mean for a promise of the form ``If $P$, then $Q$'' to be true?

Philo proposed an answer that startled the ancient world: an implication is true in every single case except when the premise $P$ is true and the outcome $Q$ is false. Diodorus and later philosophers objected passionately, arguing that an authentic conditional requires an intrinsic causal or temporal connection between $P$ and $Q$. Philo's definition meant that whenever $P$ is false, the implication is automatically considered true---the puzzle of \emph{vacuous truth}. Why should ``If $2 + 2 = 5$, then the Moon is made of green cheese'' be counted as true? Bertrand Russell famously demonstrated the logic when challenged to prove that a false premise implies anything: if $1 = 0$, adding $1$ to both sides yields $2 = 1$; the collection containing Bertrand Russell and the Pope has two members, but $2 = 1$, so the collection has one member, proving that Russell and the Pope are one!

In 1910, Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead adopted Philo's material implication in \emph{Principia Mathematica}. In modern mathematics, an implication is not a statement of psychological cause and effect; it is a binding legal contract. You cannot accuse someone of breaking a promise unless you satisfy the premise and they fail to deliver the outcome. If the premise is never met, no breach of contract ever took place! Without vacuous truth, modern set theory would instantly unravel: we could not prove that the empty set is a subset of every set ($\emptyset \subseteq A$), nor could we state theorems without attaching tedious caveats verifying whether the hypothesis is possible.
\end{historybox}

Why? Because an integer that is simultaneously a multiple of $4$ and odd does not exist. The hypothesis describes the empty set $\emptyset$. To catch someone in a lie when they make a promise of the form $P \implies Q$, you must bring forward a real counterexample that fulfills $P$ while violating $Q$. Because no one can ever produce an odd multiple of $4$, no one can ever catch the promise being broken.

In set theory, this is why the empty set is a subset of every set:
\[
\emptyset \subseteq A \quad \text{for every set } A.
\]
The definition of subset demands: ``$\forall x$, if $x \in \emptyset$, then $x \in A$.'' Because $x \in \emptyset$ is always false, the condition is vacuously satisfied for every object in the universe!

When confronted with an implication $P \implies Q$, you have three primary tactical pathways:

    \scriptsize
    \textbf{Template:} ``Assume for contradiction that $P$ is true AND $Q$ is false ($P \land \neg Q$). Derive an impossible logical rupture ($R \land \neg R$). Conclude $Q$ must hold.''

\end{center}

\subsection{Why the contrapositive is an exact mirror}

The contrapositive of $P \implies Q$ is:
\[
\neg Q \implies \neg P.
\]
These two statements are logically equivalent:
\[
(P \implies Q) \iff (\neg Q \implies \neg P).
\]
Look back at the Euler diagram. To say that $P$ is inside $Q$ ($P \subseteq Q$) is geometrically identical to saying that if you stand outside $Q$, you are guaranteed to be standing outside $P$ ($Q^c \subseteq P^c$). If you are outside the big circle, you cannot possibly be inside the small circle!

\begin{theorem}
Let $n \in \Z$. If $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Attempting a direct proof is awkward: assuming $n^2 = 2k$ forces you to take square roots, yielding $n = \sqrt{2k}$, which leaves the integer divisibility of $n$ murky.

Instead, we prove the contrapositive: if $n$ is not even, then $n^2$ is not even.

For integers, not even means odd. Assume $n$ is odd. By definition, there exists $k \in \Z$ such that $n = 2k + 1$. Squaring both sides:
\[
n^2 = (2k + 1)^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1.
\]
Because $k$ is an integer, $m = 2k^2 + 2k$ is an integer. Thus $n^2 = 2m + 1$, which means $n^2$ is odd.

We have proved that if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd. By contrapositive, if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ must be even.
\end{proof}

\subsection{What strategy unlocked---and the perils of contradiction}

Mastering the geometry of implications unlocks the three fundamental battle plans of pure mathematics: Direct Proof, Contrapositive Proof, and Proof by Contradiction. Instead of attacking every theorem head-on with brute force, you learn to inspect the algebraic terrain. If the direct hypothesis is awkward or non-linear (like $n^2$ being even), rotating the claim to its contrapositive ($\neg Q \implies \neg P$) replaces mysterious square roots with clean polynomial expansion.

Yet among the three pathways, Proof by Contradiction is the most dangerous. While direct and contrapositive proofs build constructive bridges from hypothesis to conclusion, contradiction plunges into a hypothetical counter-world where you assume the opposite of what you want to believe. If used carelessly, beginners can easily derive phantom contradictions or produce proofs that obscure the underlying geometry. 

In Chapter~8 (\emph{Proof by Contradiction: Power and Danger}), we put this heavy artillery under the microscope, exploring its greatest historical triumphs---the ancient Pythagorean discovery of incommensurability and Euclid's infinitude of primes---and learning how to wield it without losing our footing.

\subsection{Exercises}

State the contrapositive and the negation of each implication:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $x > 2$, then $x^2 > 4$.
\item If a triangle is equilateral, then it is equiangular.
\item If $n$ is divisible by $6$, then $n$ is divisible by $2$ and by $3$.
\end{enumerate}

Prove each theorem, stating whether you choose a direct proof, a contrapositive proof, or a proof by contradiction:
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item For $n \in \Z$, if $3n + 2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd.
\item For real numbers $x, y$, if $x + y > 10$, then $x > 5$ or $y > 5$.
\item If $x$ is irrational, then $-x$ is irrational.
\end{enumerate}
