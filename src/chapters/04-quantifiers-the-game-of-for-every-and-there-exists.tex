\section{Quantifiers: The Game of For Every and There Exists}
\chapterline

In Chapter~3, we saw that variables in mathematics serve distinct logical roles: an \emph{arbitrary} variable represents any generic element of a domain, while a \emph{chosen} variable is a specific witness constructed to establish an existential claim.

Most mathematical statements contain more than one variable: pairs, triples, and longer chains. When several variables appear in the same claim, who chooses first? Which choices may depend on earlier ones?

The answer is governed by the two \textbf{quantifiers} met in Chapter~2, the symbols that say \emph{how many} elements of a collection satisfy a given property:
\[
\forall \quad \text{(``for every'' / ``for all'')}, \qquad\qquad \exists \quad \text{(``there exists'' / ``for at least one'')}.
\]
The order in which these symbols appear determines the logical structure of a proof. Swapping two quantifiers can turn a true theorem into a false statement.

\begin{historybox}
In the early nineteenth century, Augustin-Louis Cauchy was trying to put calculus on solid ground. Working in 1821 with informal notions of limits, Cauchy stated in his \emph{Cours d'Analyse} that if you add up infinitely many continuous functions (functions whose graphs have no jumps) and the sum settles to a definite value at every point, then the resulting function is also continuous. However, Joseph Fourier had already observed that infinite sums of sines and cosines could settle to functions with jumps, such as square waves. For decades, mathematicians debated how a sum of jump-free terms could produce a jump.

The contradiction was resolved in 1847 when Philipp Ludwig von Seidel and George Gabriel Stokes independently realized that Cauchy had mixed up two different orders of quantifiers. Write $N$ for the number of terms you add up. If you fix one point $x$, there may be an $N$ after which the partial sums stay close to their final value at that point ($\forall x \, \exists N$, called \emph{pointwise convergence}). That does not mean a single $N$ works at every point at once ($\exists N \, \forall x$, made precise by Karl Weierstrass as \emph{uniform convergence}). Cauchy's claim is true for uniform convergence and false for pointwise convergence.

When Gottlob Frege (1879) and Charles Sanders Peirce (1885) introduced symbols for quantifiers, the ambiguity of ordinary language went away: the order of $\forall$ and $\exists$ is written down and cannot be misread. Alternating quantifiers remain central in modern logic and theoretical computer science. The \emph{polynomial hierarchy} sorts computational problems into levels according to how many alternations between $\forall$ and $\exists$ are needed to state them. Whether each extra alternation always lets you state harder problems, or whether the levels eventually stop growing, is a major open problem in computational complexity theory (the study of how much time and memory problems need).
\end{historybox}

To see why quantifier order matters, it helps to read a quantified statement as a game between two players: the statement is true exactly when one of them has a way to win.

\subsection{Quantifiers as a two-player game}

Imagine a game played on a domain (the set the variables are drawn from) between two players:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The Universal Challenger ($\forall$)}: tries to break the claim. Whenever the statement says $\forall x$, the Challenger picks an element $x$ from the domain, trying to make the condition fail.
\item \textbf{The Existential Prover ($\exists$)}: tries to make the claim hold. Whenever the statement says $\exists y$, the Prover must produce an element $y$ that satisfies the condition. In the examples below, you play the Prover.
\end{itemize}
The statement is true if the Prover can always win, whatever the Challenger does, and false if the Challenger can always win.

The main rule of the game: \textbf{the order of the quantifiers is the order of play}. The quantifier written first moves first, and whoever moves second sees the first player's move before choosing.

\subsection{Pointwise dependence versus uniform choice}

Compare two games on the real numbers $\R$ with the condition $x + y = 0$.

\textbf{Game 1}:
\[
\forall x \in \R, \, \exists y \in \R \text{ such that } x + y = 0.
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item The Challenger plays first, choosing any real number $x$.
\item You play second. Given their choice of $x$, you respond with $y = -x$.
\item Check the condition: $x + (-x) = 0$. Because you can produce a valid $y$ for any $x$ the Challenger selects, the statement is \textbf{True}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Game 2}:
\[
\exists y \in \R \text{ such that } \forall x \in \R, \, x + y = 0.
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item You must play first: you must choose a single real number $y$ before the Challenger reveals their move.
\item Suppose you choose $y = 0$.
\item The Challenger now plays second, inspecting your $y$. Knowing your choice is $0$, they pick $x = 5$.
\item Check the condition: $5 + 0 = 5 \neq 0$.
\item Can any choice of $y$ work? No: for any fixed $y$, the Challenger picks $x = 1 - y$, forcing $x + y = 1 \neq 0$. The statement is \textbf{False}.
\end{itemize}

In Game 1, the choice of $y$ depends on $x$: your response can change with each $x$. In Game 2, you must pick one value of $y$ that works for every possible $x$ at once; this is called a \emph{uniform} choice.

Figure~\ref{04:fig-line-games} draws both games in the plane. The pairs $(x, y)$ with $x + y = 0$ form the line $y = -x$. Game~1 asks whether every vertical line (one fixed $x$) meets this line somewhere; it does. Game~2 asks whether some horizontal line (one fixed $y$) lies entirely on it; none does.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check:} What happens if we change the domain of Game~1 from the real numbers $\R$ to the natural numbers $\N = \{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$?
\[
\forall x \in \N, \, \exists y \in \N \text{ such that } x + y = 0.
\]
Can the Prover still win?
\emph{Answer:} No. The Challenger picks $x = 1$. The only real number satisfying $1 + y = 0$ is $y = -1$, but $-1 \notin \N$. No such natural number exists, so the statement is False. The domain of a quantifier limits the allowable choices for each variable.
\end{recoverybox}

In pictures:
\begin{itemize}
\item To satisfy $\forall x \, \exists y \, P(x, y)$, each row must contain at least one dot, even if the column changes from row to row.
\item To satisfy $\exists y \, \forall x \, P(x, y)$, one single column must be completely filled.
\end{itemize}

So $\exists y \, \forall x \, P(x, y)$ is the stronger assertion: it demands more. The implication
\[
\bigl(\exists y \, \forall x \, P(x, y)\bigr) \implies \bigl(\forall x \, \exists y \, P(x, y)\bigr)
\]
holds for every domain and every predicate $P$ (if one column is completely filled, every row has a dot in that column).

We can check this directly using the variable roles from Chapter~3. Let $D$ be the domain that both $x$ and $y$ range over.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Assume the premise:} We assume that $\exists y \, \forall x \, P(x, y)$ is true. So there is some specific witness $y^* \in D$ such that $P(x, y^*)$ holds for every $x \in D$.
\item \textbf{Set up the goal:} We need to prove $\forall x \, \exists y \, P(x, y)$. Following our game, let $x \in D$ be arbitrary (the Challenger hands us an element).
\item \textbf{Choose the response:} We must produce a winning response $y$. We choose $y = y^*$.
\item \textbf{Verify:} Does $P(x, y^*)$ hold? Yes, because $P(x, y^*)$ holds for \emph{all} elements in the domain, including the arbitrary $x$ handed to us.
\end{enumerate}
The reverse implication is not true in general: having dots in every row does not mean they line up in a single column.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check:} In a proof of $\forall x \in \R, \, \exists y \in \R, \, P(x, y)$, can your definition of $y$ mention $x$? Can it mention a variable $z$ that has never been introduced?
\emph{Answer:} Yes to $x$, because $x$ was introduced in an outer scope before $y$ was chosen. No to $z$, because $z$ does not exist in the current scope. Variable scoping in proofs is as strict as in computer programs.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{When can quantifiers swap? (Homogeneous vs.\ heterogeneous)}

Does quantifier order always matter? No. It matters only when the quantifiers \textbf{switch players}. Two quantifiers are \emph{homogeneous} if they are the same symbol ($\forall\forall$ or $\exists\exists$) and \emph{heterogeneous} if they differ ($\forall\exists$ or $\exists\forall$). Below, $A \iff B$ (read ``$A$ if and only if $B$'') means the two statements say the same thing: each implies the other. $A \not\iff B$ means they are not equivalent. Two quantifiers \emph{commute} if swapping their order gives an equivalent statement.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Homogeneous quantifiers commute:}
\[
\begin{aligned}
\forall x \, \forall y \, P(x, y) &\iff \forall y \, \forall x \, P(x, y),\\
\exists x \, \exists y \, P(x, y) &\iff \exists y \, \exists x \, P(x, y).
\end{aligned}
\]
When both quantifiers are $\forall$, the Challenger chooses both elements. It makes no difference whether they pick $x$ first or $y$ first; their goal is to find any pair $(x, y)$ that breaks the claim. We often abbreviate this as $\forall x, y \in D$. Similarly, when both quantifiers are $\exists$, you pick both witnesses together.
\item \textbf{Heterogeneous quantifiers do not commute:}
\[
\forall x \, \exists y \, P(x, y) \not\iff \exists y \, \forall x \, P(x, y).
\]
As soon as $\forall$ and $\exists$ alternate, the game is no longer symmetric. Whoever moves second has an information advantage: they see the first player's move before committing to their own.
\end{itemize}

Putting the four kinds of claim side by side ranks them from strongest to weakest. On the grid of Figure~\ref{04:fig-grid}, $\forall x \, \forall y$ needs every cell filled, $\exists y \, \forall x$ needs one full column, $\forall x \, \exists y$ needs a dot in every row, and $\exists x \, \exists y$ needs a single dot anywhere. Any grid that has one of these patterns also has the next one, so each claim implies the next, as Figure~\ref{04:fig-strength-ladder} shows. (For the first and last arrows the domain must have at least one element; on an empty grid ``every cell is filled'' is true but ``there is a dot'' is false.)

\subsection{Everyday, software, and engineering analogies}

The distinction between $\forall x \, \exists y$ and $\exists y \, \forall x$ appears throughout technology and daily life.

\textbf{1. Removing a hum from an audio signal:} A \emph{notch filter} is a circuit that removes one narrow band of frequencies and lets the rest through; its setting is the frequency it removes.
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Claim A}: For every hum frequency $f \in [20\,\text{Hz}, 20\,\text{kHz}]$, there exists a notch filter setting that removes a hum at $f$.
\end{quote}
This is a standard feature. The user turns the dial to the frequency of the hum they hear. The setting depends on the frequency: $\text{setting} = g(f)$ for some rule $g$.

Now compare:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Claim B}: There exists a single notch filter setting that removes a hum at every frequency $f \in [20\,\text{Hz}, 20\,\text{kHz}]$.
\end{quote}
This is false: one fixed setting removes one narrow band, and a hum at any frequency outside that band passes straight through. Figure~\ref{04:fig-notch} shows both claims.

\textbf{2. Software algorithms:} Here $n$ is the length of the list, and ``runs in $O(n)$ time'' means the number of steps is at most a fixed constant times $n$. A \emph{comparison-based} sort is one that learns about the list only by comparing two elements at a time.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Pointwise:} ``For every input list $L$, there exists a Python script that outputs $L$ sorted in $O(n)$ time.'' This is true for a cheap reason: once you have seen $L$, you can sort it yourself and write a script that ignores its input and prints the sorted copy, which takes time proportional to $n$. You pick a custom script after inspecting the list.
\item \emph{Uniform:} ``There exists a comparison-based Python script that sorts every input list $L$ in $O(n)$ time.'' This is impossible: a standard theorem shows that any single comparison sort needs at least a constant times $n \log n$ comparisons on some lists of length $n$ (written $\Omega(n \log n)$), and $n \log n$ grows faster than any constant times $n$ (Figure~\ref{04:fig-nlogn}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Three nested quantifiers and the bridge to negation}

The two-player game extends to three, four, or any number of quantifiers: the players take turns in the order the quantifiers are written.

Consider the definition of a \textbf{bounded function} $f \colon \R \to \R$ (the notation means $f$ takes a real number and returns a real number; ``bounded'' means its values never go beyond some fixed size). Here $|f(x)|$ is the absolute value of $f(x)$, its distance from $0$:
\[
\exists M \in \R \text{ such that } \forall x \in \R, \, |f(x)| \le M.
\]
Here, you (the Prover) must move first by proposing a bound $M$. The Challenger then sees your $M$ and looks for an $x \in \R$ that breaks the bound. For $f(x) = \sin x$, you succeed by choosing $M = 1$. For $f(x) = x^2$, whatever $M \in \R$ you pick, the Challenger counters with $x = |M| + 1$:
\begin{align*}
|f(|M| + 1)| = (|M| + 1)^2 &= |M|^2 + 2|M| + 1 \\
&> |M| && \text{(because } |M|^2 + |M| + 1 > 0\text{)} \\
&\ge M.
\end{align*}
So your bound is broken, whatever it was, and the function is unbounded. Figure~\ref{04:fig-bounded} shows both cases.

Or consider the formal definition of the \textbf{limit of a sequence}. A sequence is an infinite list of numbers $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$, with $a_n$ the $n$-th term. We write $a_n \to L$ (``$a_n$ tends to $L$'') when the terms get and stay as close to the number $L$ as you like. The Greek letter $\eps$ (epsilon) stands for an allowed error, and $|a_n - L|$ is the distance between $a_n$ and $L$:
\[
\forall \eps > 0, \, \exists N \in \N, \, \forall n \ge N, \, |a_n - L| < \eps.
\]
This corresponds to three rounds:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Round 1 (Challenger, $\forall \eps > 0$):} Names an allowed error, such as $\eps = 0.001$.
\item \textbf{Round 2 (Prover, $\exists N \in \N$):} Responds with a cutoff position $N$ in the list, computed from $\eps$.
\item \textbf{Round 3 (Challenger, $\forall n \ge N$):} Picks any position $n \ge N$ and checks whether $|a_n - L| < \eps$.
\end{enumerate}
If you have a rule for choosing $N$ that wins against every possible $\eps > 0$, the sequence converges to $L$ (has limit $L$).

Figure~\ref{04:fig-limit-game} shows the three rounds: from position $N$ on, every term lies inside a band of half-width $\eps$ around $L$.

What if a quantified claim is false? How do you disagree precisely with a claim that has several quantifiers?

If someone claims that $\forall x \in D, \, \exists y \in D, \, P(x, y)$, refuting them means taking the Challenger's side and winning. You must show that \emph{there exists an element $x \in D$} for which \emph{every possible response $y$} fails. Writing $\neg$ for ``not'' (so $\neg P(x, y)$ means ``$P(x, y)$ is false''):
\[
\neg \bigl(\forall x \in D, \, \exists y \in D, \, P(x, y)\bigr) \iff \exists x \in D, \, \forall y \in D, \, \neg P(x, y).
\]
Every $\forall$ becomes an $\exists$, every $\exists$ becomes a $\forall$, and the condition is negated. Negation swaps the roles of the two players. On the grid (Figure~\ref{04:fig-negation-grid}), the claim says every row has a dot, so its negation says some row is empty.

\subsection{Exercises}

\subsubsection*{Part A: Warm-Up Mental Checks}
State whether each statement is true or false, and justify your answer in a single sentence:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\forall x \in \R, \, \exists y \in \R \text{ such that } x + y = 10$.
\item $\exists y \in \R \text{ such that } \forall x \in \R, \, x + y = 10$.
\item $\forall x \in \Z, \, \exists y \in \Z \text{ such that } x - y \text{ is an even integer}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part B: Routine Game Practice}
Determine whether each statement is true or false. Give a winning strategy for the Prover, or a winning move for the Challenger:
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item $\forall x \in \R, \, \exists y \in \R \text{ such that } x < y$.
\item $\exists y \in \R \text{ such that } \forall x \in \R, \, x < y$.
\item $\forall x \in \R, \, \exists y \in \R \text{ such that } xy = 1$.
\item $\forall x \in (\R \setminus \{0\}), \, \exists y \in \R \text{ such that } xy = 1$. (Here $\R \setminus \{0\}$ means all real numbers except $0$.)
\item $\exists y \in \R \text{ such that } \forall x \in \R, \, xy = 0$.
\item $\forall x \in \N, \, \exists y \in \N \text{ such that } y < x$ (where $\N = \{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part C: Proof Skeletons}
For each claim below, write only the opening declaration (introducing variables and their roles) and the final verification line of a direct proof. Do not carry out the calculations:
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item $\forall x \in \R, \, \exists y \in \R \text{ such that } y^3 = x$.
\item $\exists c \in \R \text{ such that } \forall x \in \R, \, x^2 + c \ge 0$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part D: Deeper Challenges}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item Write out a careful four-step proof that for any non-empty domain $D$ and any predicate $P(x, y)$, the implication
\[
\bigl(\exists y \in D \, \forall x \in D \, P(x, y)\bigr) \implies \bigl(\forall x \in D \, \exists y \in D \, P(x, y)\bigr)
\]
is valid (true for every such $D$ and $P$). Explicitly state the role (arbitrary vs.\ chosen) of each variable you introduce.
\item Consider the three-quantifier sentence on the real numbers:
\[
\forall x \in \R, \, \exists y \in \R, \, \forall z \in \R, \, x + y + z > 0.
\]
Is this statement true or false? Play the three-round game step by step to prove your conclusion.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{walkbox}
Think of the definition of the limit of a sequence: ``For every $\eps > 0$, there exists an index $N$ such that\ldots'' Who moves first in this game? Does $N$ depend on $\eps$, or does $\eps$ depend on $N$? What would it mean if the quantifiers were reversed: ``There exists an index $N$ such that for every $\eps > 0$\ldots''? Could any sequence except an eventually constant one (one whose terms are all equal from some position on) satisfy that reversed condition?
\end{walkbox}
