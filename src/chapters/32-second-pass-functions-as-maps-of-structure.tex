\section{Second Pass: Functions as Maps of Structure}
\chapterline

In Chapter~31, we treated sets as plain containers of elements. But the sets mathematicians study usually carry extra structure: operations that combine elements, distance functions that measure how far apart points are, or symmetries of a shape. Between such sets, the functions that matter most are those that preserve that structure.

In school algebra, a function is introduced as a formula, such as $f(x) = x^2 + 1$. In higher mathematics, the question about a function changes from ``what does it compute?'' to ``what structure does it respect?'' A structure-preserving map between two structured sets is called a \emph{morphism}. A morphism that is a bijection, and whose inverse is also a morphism, is called an \emph{isomorphism}. Two structures joined by an isomorphism are the same structure with the elements renamed; we call them \emph{isomorphic}.

\begin{historybox}
In 1945, two mathematicians working at the intersection of algebra and geometry---Saunders Mac Lane and Samuel Eilenberg---published a paper titled \emph{General Theory of Natural Equivalences}. They faced a concrete obstacle: in algebraic topology (the field that studies shapes by translating them into algebraic structures), mathematicians calculated homology groups (algebraic quantities that count the independent holes, tunnels, and voids in a space). Different constructions produced isomorphic groups, but every isomorphism they could write down depended on an arbitrary choice of coordinates. They had no rigorous language to state that two geometric constructions were ``naturally'' identical without making an arbitrary choice.

To solve this, Eilenberg and Mac Lane stopped describing mathematical objects only by their elements. They formulated \emph{Category Theory}, representing mathematical structures through collections of \emph{objects} connected by directed \emph{arrows} (morphisms). Mac Lane observed that categories were introduced to define functors (maps that translate categories into categories while preserving composition), and functors were introduced to define natural transformations (systematic mappings between functors).

When category theory was first presented, some algebraists referred to it as ``generalized abstract nonsense,'' viewing arrow diagrams as purely formal bookkeeping. In the 1950s and 1960s Alexander Grothendieck rebuilt algebraic geometry on categorical foundations, describing each object through the morphisms into and out of it, and this approach led to proofs of long-open conjectures. Today categorical thinking is used across algebraic topology, mathematical physics, and the theory of programming languages. An active research area is \emph{higher category theory}, where arrows connect objects, higher arrows connect arrows, and the hierarchy continues.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Preserving Structure Across Different Mathematical Worlds}

Depending on the field, sets come with extra features: distances, symmetries, algebraic operations, or notions of openness. A structure-preserving function is one that respects those features:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Analysis (Metric Spaces)}: A metric space is a set equipped with a distance function $d$ (Chapter~29). A function $f: X \to Y$ between metric spaces is continuous if it respects nearness: whenever a sequence of points $(x_n)$ converges to $x$, the images $f(x_n)$ converge to $f(x)$. Nearby inputs produce nearby outputs.
  \item \textbf{Abstract Algebra (Groups and Rings)}: A group is a set equipped with an associative operation, an identity element, and inverses (Chapter~21). A ring is a set with two operations, an addition and a multiplication, such as the integers $\Z$. A structure-preserving map between groups is called a \emph{group homomorphism} (``homomorphism'' is Greek for ``same shape''). Writing $\cdot_G$ for the operation of $G$ and $\cdot_H$ for the operation of $H$, a group homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ (the Greek letter phi) satisfies, for all $a, b \in G$:
  \[
  \phi(a \cdot_G b) = \phi(a) \cdot_H \phi(b).
  \]
  Combining inside $G$ and then mapping gives the same result as mapping first and combining inside $H$. A ring homomorphism preserves both addition and multiplication in this way.
  \item \textbf{Topology}: A topological space replaces distance by a list of subsets that count as ``open'' (Chapter~22). A homeomorphism $f: X \to Y$ is a continuous bijection with a continuous inverse. It deforms a space without tearing or gluing; it is the isomorphism of topology.
  \item \textbf{Differential Geometry}: A manifold is a space that, near each point, looks like flat space $\R^n$ (Chapter~23). A smooth map between manifolds preserves the ability to do calculus: it carries derivatives and tangent spaces on one manifold to derivatives and tangent spaces on the other. (The tangent space at a point is the flat space of all velocity directions a curve through that point can have.)
\end{itemize}

\begin{intuitionbox}
For a homomorphism from everyday math, take the exponential function:
\[
\exp: (\R, +) \longrightarrow (\R_{>0}, \times), \qquad \exp(x) = e^x.
\]
The source $(\R, +)$ is the real line, where elements combine by addition. The target $(\R_{>0}, \times)$ is the set $\R_{>0}$ of positive real numbers, combining by multiplication.

Pick two numbers in the source, say $x = 2$ and $y = 3$. You have two ways to reach the target:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Add first, then map}: In the source, $2 + 3 = 5$. Evaluating $\exp$ gives $\exp(5) = e^5$.
  \item \textbf{Map first, then multiply}: Evaluating $\exp$ on each term first gives $\exp(2) = e^2$ and $\exp(3) = e^3$. Multiplying them in the target gives $e^2 \cdot e^3 = e^5$.
\end{enumerate}
Because $e^{x+y} = e^x \cdot e^y$ for all real $x$ and $y$, both routes always end at the same value. The exponential function turns addition of real numbers into multiplication of positive numbers.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Consider the shift map $s: \R \to \R$ given by $s(x) = x + 1$. Is $s$ a group homomorphism from $(\R, +)$ to $(\R, +)$?

Test two inputs, say $x = 1$ and $y = 2$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Adding first gives $s(1 + 2) = s(3) = 4$.
  \item Shifting first and adding gives $s(1) + s(2) = 2 + 3 = 5$.
\end{itemize}
They disagree ($4 \neq 5$). The underlying problem is that $s$ moves the identity: $s(0) = 1 \neq 0$. Every group homomorphism carries the identity to the identity (we prove this at the start of the proof of Theorem~\ref{32:thm-kernel-injective}), so $s$ is not a homomorphism.
\end{recoverybox}

A useful habit follows from this point of view: to understand an object, look at the maps into it and out of it, as well as at its elements.

\subsection{Subtle Traps: Basis-Dependent Maps and One-Way Continuity}

Thinking in terms of structure-preserving maps clears up two common traps:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Pitfall of Non-Canonical Constructions}: A common mistake is to define a relationship between two structures by choosing arbitrary coordinates. An example from linear algebra needs a few words first. A \emph{vector space} is a set of objects (vectors) that can be added and multiplied by numbers (called \emph{scalars}), like arrows in the plane. A map between vector spaces is \emph{linear} if it respects addition and scalar multiplication. A \emph{basis} of $V$ is a list of vectors such that every vector of $V$ can be written in exactly one way as a sum of scalar multiples of them, and the number of vectors in a basis is the \emph{dimension} of $V$. The \emph{dual space} $V^*$ is the set of linear functionals on $V$: linear maps that take a vector and output a scalar. Every finite-dimensional vector space $V$ has the same dimension as $V^*$ and is therefore isomorphic to it. But every such isomorphism requires choosing a basis; pick a different basis and the isomorphism changes. In contrast, each vector $v \in V$ gives a functional on $V^*$ with no choices at all, ``evaluate at $v$'': $\operatorname{eval}_v(\varphi) = \varphi(v)$. The resulting map $v \mapsto \operatorname{eval}_v$ from $V$ to its \emph{double dual} $V^{**}$ (the dual space of $V^*$) requires no choice of basis, so it is called \emph{canonical}. Morphism thinking prefers such choice-free maps, because they reflect the structure itself rather than a coordinate system.
  \item \textbf{Continuous Bijections Need Not Be Isomorphisms}: In set theory, a bijection is an invertible map. But in topology, a continuous map can be a bijection without being an isomorphism (homeomorphism). The \emph{unit circle} $S^1$ is the set of points at distance $1$ from the origin in the plane. Writing points of the plane as complex numbers, the point $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ is written $e^{i\theta}$. The map $f: [0, 1) \to S^1$ given by $f(t) = e^{2\pi i t} = (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)$ wraps the half-open interval once around the circle, starting at $(1, 0)$ and going counterclockwise. Every point on the circle has exactly one preimage, and $f$ is continuous. But its inverse is discontinuous at $(1, 0)$: points on the circle just below the positive horizontal axis have preimages near $t = 1$, while $(1, 0)$ itself has preimage $t = 0$. In terms of open sets: the interval $[0, 1/2)$ is open in the space $[0, 1)$, because it is the part of $[0, 1)$ lying inside the open interval $(-1/2, 1/2)$. Yet its preimage under $f^{-1}$, which is the forward image $f([0, 1/2))$, the upper half of the circle including $(1, 0)$ but excluding $(-1, 0)$, is not open in $S^1$. Every small arc around $(1, 0)$ contains points just below the axis that lie outside that half-circle. An isomorphism must have an inverse that also preserves the structure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Commutative Diagrams: The Language of Compatibility}

When several maps interact, mathematicians draw them as \emph{commutative diagrams}. A commutative diagram is a picture of sets (dots or letters) and functions (arrows) in which any two paths of arrows between the same pair of sets give the same composite function. We then say the diagram \emph{commutes}. Figure~\ref{32:fig-homomorphism-commute} was already a commutative diagram.

The definition of a group homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ can be expressed as a commutative diagram. In the source group $G$, the operation is a function $\mu_G: G \times G \to G$ (the Greek letter mu, for ``multiplication'') given by $\mu_G(a, b) = a \cdot_G b$. In the target group $H$, the operation is $\mu_H: H \times H \to H$. Saying that $\phi(a \cdot_G b) = \phi(a) \cdot_H \phi(b)$ means that the following diagram commutes:
\[
\begin{array}{ccc}
G \times G & \xrightarrow{\quad \mu_G \quad} & G \\
\Big\downarrow{\scriptstyle (\phi, \phi)} & & \Big\downarrow{\scriptstyle \phi} \\
H \times H & \xrightarrow{\quad \mu_H \quad} & H
\end{array}
\]
Starting at $(a, b)$ in the top-left, going across and down produces $\phi(a \cdot_G b)$. Going down and across produces $\phi(a) \cdot_H \phi(b)$, where the left arrow $(\phi, \phi)(a, b) = (\phi(a), \phi(b))$ applies $\phi$ to each coordinate. So the diagram commutes exactly when $\phi$ is a homomorphism.

\paragraph{The Canonical Decomposition of a Function.}
Consider any function $f: A \to B$. Call two inputs related when they produce the same output: $x \sim y \iff f(x) = f(y)$. This is an equivalence relation, and its equivalence classes are exactly the fibers of $f$ (the sets $f^{-1}(\{b\})$ of inputs sharing one output, Chapter~13). The set of all these classes is the quotient set $A/{\sim}$ (Chapter~15). Every function then splits into three stages, shown in Figure~\ref{32:fig-canonical-factorization}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Projection ($\pi$)}: The map $\pi: A \to A/{\sim}$ defined by $\pi(x) = [x]$. This map is surjective; it sends each element to its equivalence class.
  \item \textbf{Induced bijection ($\bar{f}$)}: The map $\bar{f}: A/{\sim} \to \operatorname{im}(f)$ defined by $\bar{f}([x]) = f(x)$. This map is both injective and surjective; it is a bijection between fibers and attained values.
  \item \textbf{Inclusion ($\iota$)}: The map $\iota: \operatorname{im}(f) \to B$ defined by $\iota(y) = y$. This map is injective; it places the image $\operatorname{im}(f)$ (the set of values $f$ actually takes) inside the codomain $B$.
\end{enumerate}

\[

\]
says the same thing as a formula. The word \emph{canonical} means that no choices are made anywhere: each of the three maps is determined by $f$ alone.

\subsection{Kernels: Measuring What a Map Forgets}

Every non-injective map collapses some information. In linear algebra, multiplying by a matrix may squash three-dimensional space onto a flat plane; the set of vectors that the matrix sends to the zero vector is called its \emph{null space}, and it records exactly which directions get flattened.

In group theory, where $G$ and $H$ are groups with identity elements $e_G$ and $e_H$, the \emph{kernel} of a homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ is defined as:
\[
\ker\phi = \{g \in G : \phi(g) = e_H\}.
\]
The kernel is the algebraic analogue of the null space: it collects every element of $G$ that maps to the identity in $H$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The size of $\ker\phi$ measures how much information $\phi$ collapses. If $\ker\phi = \{e_G\}$, no two distinct elements map to the same image, and $\phi$ is injective. If $\ker\phi$ contains non-identity elements, each fiber contains multiple elements.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorem}\label{32:thm-kernel-injective}
A group homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ is injective if and only if $\ker\phi = \{e_G\}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First, observe that every group homomorphism preserves the identity:
\[
\phi(e_G) = \phi(e_G \cdot e_G) = \phi(e_G) \cdot \phi(e_G).
\]
Multiplying both sides on the left by $\phi(e_G)^{-1}$ in $H$ turns the left side into $e_H$ and the right side into $e_H \cdot \phi(e_G) = \phi(e_G)$, so $\phi(e_G) = e_H$.

$(\implies)$ Suppose $\phi$ is injective. If $g \in \ker\phi$, then $\phi(g) = e_H = \phi(e_G)$. Because $\phi$ is injective, this forces $g = e_G$. Hence $\ker\phi = \{e_G\}$.

$(\impliedby)$ Suppose $\ker\phi = \{e_G\}$. Let $a, b \in G$ satisfy $\phi(a) = \phi(b)$. Multiply both sides on the right by $\phi(b)^{-1}$ in $H$:
\[
\phi(a)\phi(b)^{-1} = e_H.
\]
Homomorphisms preserve inverses: since
\[
\phi(b)\,\phi(b^{-1}) = \phi(b b^{-1}) = \phi(e_G) = e_H,
\]
the element $\phi(b^{-1})$ is the inverse of $\phi(b)$, that is, $\phi(b)^{-1} = \phi(b^{-1})$. Using this and the fact that $\phi$ preserves products,
\begin{align*}
e_H &= \phi(a)\,\phi(b)^{-1} \\
    &= \phi(a)\,\phi(b^{-1}) && \text{(inverses are preserved)} \\
    &= \phi(ab^{-1}) && \text{(products are preserved).}
\end{align*}
This implies $ab^{-1} \in \ker\phi$. Since the kernel contains only the identity $e_G$, we have $ab^{-1} = e_G$. Multiplying by $b$ on the right gives $a = b$. Thus $\phi$ is injective.
\end{proof}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Why does testing injectivity in group theory require checking only one element (the identity), whereas in general set theory you must check every pair of elements?

Because a group looks the same from every point: multiplying by a fixed element moves any point to any other while keeping the operation intact. If two distinct inputs collide ($\phi(a) = \phi(b)$), we can always slide the collision back to the identity by computing $ab^{-1}$: $\phi(ab^{-1}) = \phi(a)\phi(b)^{-1} = e_H$. If the identity has no collisions, no point anywhere in the group has collisions. Figure~\ref{32:fig-collision-slide} pictures this slide.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Quotient Groups and the First Isomorphism Theorem}

The kernel is the fiber over the identity $e_H$. It also controls the shape of every other fiber. Suppose two elements $x, a \in G$ produce the same output: $\phi(x) = \phi(a)$. Then:
\begin{align*}
\phi(x) = \phi(a) &\implies \phi(a)^{-1}\phi(x) = e_H && \text{(multiply on the left by $\phi(a)^{-1}$)} \\
&\implies \phi(a^{-1}x) = e_H && \text{(inverses and products are preserved)} \\
&\implies a^{-1}x \in \ker\phi.
\end{align*}
Setting $k = a^{-1}x \in \ker\phi$, we obtain $x = ak$. Conversely, for any $k \in \ker\phi$, the homomorphism property ensures:
\[
\phi(ak) = \phi(a)\phi(k) = \phi(a)e_H = \phi(a).
\]
Therefore, the full preimage of the point $\phi(a)$ is the set obtained by multiplying every element of the kernel on the left by $a$. Such a set is called a \emph{left coset} of the kernel:
\[
a\ker\phi = \{ak : k \in \ker\phi\}.
\]
Every fiber of $\phi$ is a translated copy of the kernel. If $\ker\phi$ contains $m$ elements, every fiber across the entire group contains exactly $m$ elements (the map $k \mapsto ak$ is a bijection from $\ker\phi$ onto $a\ker\phi$, since $ak = ak'$ cancels to $k = k'$). The domain $G$ is sliced into parallel coset sheets, as pictured in Figure~\ref{32:fig-first-isomorphism}. The set of all cosets is written $G/\ker\phi$. The theorem below shows that it carries a group operation, which makes it the \emph{quotient group} of $G$ by the kernel.

For a concrete case, let $\Z_3 = \{0, 1, 2\}$ with addition mod $3$, and let $\phi: (\Z, +) \to (\Z_3, +)$ send $n$ to $n \bmod 3$, the remainder of $n$ on division by $3$. Remainders add correctly, so $\phi$ is a homomorphism, and its kernel is the set $3\Z = \{\dots, -3, 0, 3, 6, \dots\}$ of multiples of $3$. Here the operation is addition, so the coset $a\ker\phi$ is written $a + 3\Z$. Figure~\ref{32:fig-mod3-fibers} shows the three fibers $3\Z$, $1 + 3\Z$ and $2 + 3\Z$: each is the kernel shifted along the number line.

\begin{theorem}[The First Isomorphism Theorem for Groups]\label{32:thm-first-isomorphism}
Let $\phi: G \to H$ be a group homomorphism. Then the map
\[
\bar{\phi}: G/\ker\phi \longrightarrow \operatorname{im}(\phi), \qquad \bar{\phi}(g\ker\phi) = \phi(g),
\]
is a well-defined group isomorphism. Consequently,
\[
G/\ker\phi \cong \operatorname{im}(\phi).
\]
\end{theorem}

The symbol $\cong$ reads ``is isomorphic to''. A map is \emph{well-defined} when its value does not depend on which representative of a coset we use to compute it; the trap box after the proof explains why this needs checking.

\begin{proof}
We check the four required properties in turn:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Well-defined}: Suppose $g\ker\phi = g'\ker\phi$. Then $g' = gk$ for some $k \in \ker\phi$. Evaluating $\bar{\phi}$ gives:
  \[
  \phi(g') = \phi(gk) = \phi(g)\phi(k) = \phi(g)e_H = \phi(g).
  \]
  The value does not depend on the choice of representative.
  \item \textbf{Homomorphism}: Coset multiplication in $G/\ker\phi$ is defined by
  \[
  (a\ker\phi)(b\ker\phi) = ab\ker\phi.
  \]
  This rule does not depend on the chosen representatives because the kernel is a \emph{normal} subgroup. A subgroup $N$ is \emph{normal} if $gkg^{-1} \in N$ for every $g \in G$ and every $k \in N$. The kernel passes this test: for every $g \in G$ and $k \in \ker\phi$,
  \[
  \phi(gkg^{-1}) = \phi(g)\,e_H\,\phi(g)^{-1} = e_H,
  \]
  so $gkg^{-1} \in \ker\phi$. Hence if $a' = ak_1$ and $b' = bk_2$ with $k_1, k_2 \in \ker\phi$, then
  \[
  a'b' = ak_1bk_2 = ab\,(b^{-1}k_1b)\,k_2 \in ab\ker\phi,
  \]
  because $b^{-1}k_1b$ lies in the kernel (normality with $g = b^{-1}$). So $\bar{\phi}$ respects multiplication:
  \begin{align*}
  \bar{\phi}\bigl((a\ker\phi)(b\ker\phi)\bigr) &= \bar{\phi}(ab\ker\phi) && \text{(rule for multiplying cosets)} \\
  &= \phi(ab) && \text{(definition of $\bar{\phi}$)} \\
  &= \phi(a)\,\phi(b) && \text{($\phi$ is a homomorphism)} \\
  &= \bar{\phi}(a\ker\phi)\,\bar{\phi}(b\ker\phi) && \text{(definition of $\bar{\phi}$).}
  \end{align*}
  \item \textbf{Injective}: Suppose $\bar{\phi}(g\ker\phi) = e_H$. Then $\phi(g) = e_H$, so $g \in \ker\phi$. But this means $g\ker\phi = \ker\phi$, which is the identity element of $G/\ker\phi$. By Theorem~\ref{32:thm-kernel-injective}, $\bar{\phi}$ is injective.
  \item \textbf{Surjective}: By definition, every element of $\operatorname{im}(\phi)$ has the form $\phi(g)$ for some $g \in G$. Since $\bar{\phi}(g\ker\phi) = \phi(g)$, every image point is reached.
\end{enumerate}
Because $\bar{\phi}$ is a bijective homomorphism, it is an isomorphism.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The Well-Definedness Trap}: Whenever you define a function whose input is an equivalence class or a coset, your formula picks a representative: $\bar{\phi}([g]) = \phi(g)$. Yet $[g]$ contains many elements. If someone else selects $g' \in [g]$, will your formula return the same answer? You must always verify:
\[
g \sim g' \implies \phi(g) = \phi(g').
\]
If this check fails, your formula assigns multiple values to the same equivalence class and thus fails to be a well-defined function.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{What Morphisms Achieve---and Their Limits}

Viewing functions as structure-preserving morphisms gave mathematics three lasting tools:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Universal Definitions}: Concepts like products ($A \times B$), quotients ($A/{\sim}$), and free groups (collections of words formed by letters with no relations other than inverses canceling) can be defined by a \emph{universal mapping property} instead of a construction recipe. Such a property describes an object by how every other object maps into or out of it. For example, the product $A \times B$ is characterized by this: a map from any set $C$ into $A \times B$ is the same thing as a pair of maps $C \to A$ and $C \to B$. Write $p_A(a, b) = a$ and $p_B(a, b) = b$ for the two projections. Given $g: C \to A$ and $h: C \to B$, the map $u: C \to A \times B$ given by $u(c) = (g(c), h(c))$ is the only map into $A \times B$ whose first coordinate is $g$ and whose second coordinate is $h$ (Figure~\ref{32:fig-product-universal}). Any two objects with the same universal property are isomorphic, by exactly one isomorphism that respects the property; mathematicians say the object is determined ``up to unique isomorphism''.
  \item \textbf{Bridges Across Disciplines}: Functors translate geometric shapes into algebraic objects such as groups, and continuous maps between shapes into homomorphisms between the groups. For example, algebraic topologists assign to any space its fundamental group (measuring how loops wind around holes) and homology groups (counting higher-dimensional cavities).
  \item \textbf{Canonical Decomposition}: Any morphism factors into a canonical projection, followed by a bijection (an isomorphism, in the case of groups), followed by an inclusion ($f = \iota \circ \bar{f} \circ \pi$).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
Thinking in arrows also has limits:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Foundational Size Paradoxes}: Category theory speaks casually of the category of ``all sets'' or ``all groups''. But just as a ``set of all sets'' leads to Russell's paradox (Chapter~10), a collection of all categories would contain itself. One fix distinguishes sets from \emph{proper classes}: collections too large to be sets, which can be described but cannot be elements of anything (this is G\"odel--Bernays set theory). Another fix uses a nested hierarchy of \emph{universes}, each a set large enough to do ordinary mathematics inside.
  \item \textbf{Loss of Computational Specifics}: Category theory proves that a solution exists and is unique up to unique isomorphism, but it rarely produces an explicit numerical formula or an algorithm to solve a differential equation. When an engineer needs the vibration frequencies of a bridge, categorical arrows must eventually yield to concrete matrix computations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mental Check (Structure Preservation)}: For each function below, decide without a full proof whether it preserves the given group operation:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $f: (\R, +) \to (\R, +)$ defined by $f(x) = 3x$.
    \item $g: (\R, +) \to (\R, +)$ defined by $g(x) = x + 3$.
    \item $h: (\R_{>0}, \times) \to (\R_{>0}, \times)$ defined by $h(x) = x^3$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item For each function below, identify the fibers (the sets of inputs mapped to the same output) and describe what geometric information is forgotten:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $f: \R^2 \to \R$ defined by $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$.
    \item $g: \R^3 \to \R^2$ defined by $g(x, y, z) = (x, y)$.
    \item $\det: \operatorname{GL}_2(\R) \to \R^\times$, mapping each invertible $2 \times 2$ matrix to its determinant:
    \[
    \det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc.
    \]
    Here $\operatorname{GL}_2(\R)$ is the group of invertible $2 \times 2$ real matrices (Chapter~21) and $\R^\times$ is the set of nonzero real numbers under multiplication.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Let $\phi: \Z \to \Z_6$ send an integer $n$ to $n \bmod 6$. Show that $\phi$ is a ring homomorphism (preserving both addition and multiplication). Here the kernel is the set of integers sent to $0$. What is $\ker\phi$? What does $\Z/\ker\phi$ look like?
  \item Suppose $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are functions with composite $h = g \circ f$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item If $h$ is injective, prove that $f$ must be injective. Give a concrete counterexample showing $g$ need not be injective.
    \item If $h$ is surjective, prove that $g$ must be surjective. Give a counterexample showing $f$ need not be surjective.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Consider the wrapping map $\phi: (\R, +) \to (S^1, \cdot)$ given by $\phi(t) = e^{2\pi i t}$, where $S^1 = \{z \in \C : |z| = 1\}$ is the unit circle and the operation $\cdot$ is multiplication of complex numbers. (You may use $e^{i\alpha} e^{i\beta} = e^{i(\alpha + \beta)}$, and $e^{2\pi i t} = 1$ exactly when $t$ is an integer.)
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Prove that $\phi$ is a group homomorphism.
    \item Prove that $\ker\phi = \Z$.
    \item Apply the First Isomorphism Theorem to deduce that $\R/\Z \cong S^1$. Describe the geometric meaning of the cosets $t + \Z$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Let $\phi: (\Z_3, +) \to (\Z_5, +)$ be a group homomorphism.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Show that for any $x \in \Z_3$, the element $\phi(x)$ must satisfy $3 \cdot \phi(x) = 0$ in $\Z_5$, where $3 \cdot \phi(x)$ means $\phi(x) + \phi(x) + \phi(x)$.
    \item Using the fact that $\gcd(3, 5) = 1$, prove that $\phi(x) = 0$ for all $x \in \Z_3$.
    \item Conclude that the only homomorphism from $\Z_3$ to $\Z_5$ is the trivial map, which sends every element to $0$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
