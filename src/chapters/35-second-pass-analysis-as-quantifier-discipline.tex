\section{Second Pass: Analysis as Quantifier Discipline}
\chapterline

In Chapter~34, we used mathematical induction to establish statements for all natural numbers: verifying a base case and stepping from $n$ to $n+1$. On the real number line, however, there is no ``next'' real number. Between any two distinct real numbers lie infinitely many others, so stepping from one number to the next cannot reach every point of the line.

To reason about limits and continuity on the real line without contradiction, mathematicians wrote the conditions as precise quantified statements. In elementary calculus, you compute limits using algebraic recipes: factor the numerator, cancel $(x-a)$, plug in numbers, or use a shortcut based on derivatives.

In real analysis, definitions are stated directly using quantifiers. For example, a function $f$ with domain $D$ (its set of allowed inputs) is continuous at a point $c \in D$ when
\[
\forall \eps > 0, \; \exists \delta > 0, \; \forall x \in D, \quad |x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(c)| < \eps.
\]
Here $\eps$ is how far the output may stray from $f(c)$, and $\delta$ is how far the input may stray from $c$. This is the definition from Chapter~20. Words like ``approaching'' and ``infinitely close'' are replaced by inequalities, and the meaning depends on the order of the quantifiers.

Figure~\ref{35:fig-epsilon-delta-game} draws the definition as a two-player game. An adversary challenges you with a vertical tolerance window $L \pm \eps$, where $L = f(c)$. To prove continuity, you must respond with a horizontal window $c \pm \delta$ such that the part of the curve above that window stays inside the adversary's corridor.

\begin{historybox}
For nearly two centuries after Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz invented calculus in the late 1600s, mathematics operated on informal foundations. Calculations relied on ``infinitesimals''---quantities treated as smaller than any positive real number, yet nonzero when dividing. In 1734, the philosopher Bishop George Berkeley published \emph{The Analyst}, pointing out the logical contradiction of treating these increments as nonzero when dividing a difference quotient and as zero immediately afterward when discarding remainder terms.

The rescue operation culminated in nineteenth-century Germany. In 1872, Richard Dedekind published \emph{Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen} (\emph{Continuity and Irrational Numbers}). Dedekind defined real numbers without geometry or motion using ``cuts'' ($A | B$): split all the rationals into a left set $A$ and a right set $B$, with every element of $A$ below every element of $B$. When no rational sits at the dividing point (as with $A = \{q : q < 0 \text{ or } q^2 < 2\}$), the cut itself defines an irrational number, here $\sqrt{2}$. Around the same time, Karl Weierstrass removed infinitesimals by rebuilding analysis on inequalities between ordinary numbers (the ``arithmetization of analysis''). He replaced phrases about motion, like ``$x$ approaches $c$,'' with the $\eps$-$\delta$ game: an adversary specifies a vertical tolerance $\eps$, and the prover must supply a horizontal window $\delta$. Eduard Heine (1872) and \'Emile Borel (1895) proved what is now called the Heine--Borel theorem: if a closed and bounded interval $[a, b]$ is covered by a collection of open intervals (possibly infinitely many), then finitely many of them already cover it. This turns a check over infinitely many pieces into a check over finitely many.

Did infinitesimals die forever? In 1960, the logician Abraham Robinson used mathematical logic to build \emph{non-standard analysis}, a larger number system (the \emph{hyperreal numbers}) that contains genuine infinitesimals and in which they can be used with full rigour. In \emph{computable analysis}, which asks what a computer can and cannot calculate about real numbers, open questions remain. One known limit: no algorithm can decide, for every real number described by a program that outputs ever-better approximations, whether that number equals zero.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The Quantifier Game: Pointwise versus Uniform}

To see how much quantifier order changes the meaning, compare two definitions of continuity for a function $f: D \to \R$ on a domain $D \subseteq \R$. Both say that small enough changes in the input keep the output within any required tolerance; they differ in what ``small enough'' may depend on:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pointwise Continuity on $D$}:
  \[
  \forall x \in D, \; \forall \eps > 0, \; \exists \delta > 0, \; \forall y \in D, \quad |x - y| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(y)| < \eps.
  \]
  \item \textbf{Uniform Continuity on $D$}:
  \[
  \forall \eps > 0, \; \exists \delta > 0, \; \forall x \in D, \; \forall y \in D, \quad |x - y| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(y)| < \eps.
  \]
\end{itemize}

The only change is position: in pointwise continuity, the phrase ``$\forall x \in D$'' sits \emph{before} ``$\exists \delta > 0$''. In uniform continuity, ``$\exists \delta > 0$'' sits \emph{before} ``$\forall x \in D$''. Read left to right, each definition is a list of moves in the game, and a player may use only what has already been chosen. Figure~\ref{35:fig-quantifier-order} lays out the two lists of moves side by side.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{The Order of Quantifiers:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item In \textbf{pointwise continuity}, the adversary provides a specific point $x$ and a tolerance $\eps$. The choice $\delta(x, \eps)$ may depend on the point $x$. If the slope grows steep near a boundary, $\delta$ can be chosen arbitrarily small near that location.
  \item In \textbf{uniform continuity}, the adversary provides only $\eps$. You must produce a single $\delta(\eps)$ that works across the entire domain $D$. If the slope grows arbitrarily steep anywhere in $D$, no single positive $\delta$ suffices globally, and uniform continuity fails.
\end{itemize}
\end{intuitionbox}

To see this difference in concrete arithmetic, consider $f(x) = 1/x$ on the open interval $(0, 1)$. The function is continuous at every single point $x_0 \in (0, 1)$. For any input $x \in (0, 1)$, the vertical difference is
\[
|f(x) - f(x_0)| = \left|\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{x_0}\right| = \frac{|x - x_0|}{x x_0}.
\]
If we keep $x$ close to $x_0$, say $|x - x_0| < x_0/2$, then $x > x_0 - x_0/2 = x_0/2$, which makes the denominator $x x_0 > x_0^2/2$. Taking reciprocals of positive numbers reverses the inequality, so $1/(x x_0) < 2/x_0^2$. Thus
\[
|f(x) - f(x_0)| < \frac{2|x - x_0|}{x_0^2}.
\]
To force this change to be strictly smaller than $\eps$, we set
\[
\delta = \min\left\{\frac{x_0}{2}, \, \frac{\eps x_0^2}{2}\right\}.
\]
The first entry keeps the denominator estimate valid. The second gives, whenever $|x - x_0| < \delta$,
\[
|f(x) - f(x_0)| < \frac{2|x - x_0|}{x_0^2} < \frac{2}{x_0^2} \cdot \frac{\eps x_0^2}{2} = \eps.
\]
That proves pointwise continuity: for any chosen $x_0$, a valid $\delta > 0$ exists.

Yet $f(x) = 1/x$ is \emph{not} uniformly continuous on $(0, 1)$. Our formula for $\delta$ shrinks in proportion to $x_0^2$. As $x_0 \to 0$, the curve gets steeper without bound, and the window that works shrinks toward zero. If an adversary challenges us with $\eps = 1$, no single universal $\delta > 0$ can work. For any candidate $\delta > 0$, choose an integer $n \ge 2$ such that $1/n < \delta$ (taking $n \ge 2$ keeps both test points inside $(0, 1)$). Now test the pair $x = 1/n$ and $y = 1/(2n)$ in $(0, 1)$. Their horizontal separation is
\[
|x - y| = \frac{1}{2n} < \frac{1}{n} < \delta,
\]
yet their vertical separation is
\[
|f(x) - f(y)| = |n - 2n| = n \ge 1 = \eps.
\]
The single $\delta$ fails. Hence $f(x) = 1/x$ is pointwise continuous on $(0, 1)$, but not uniformly continuous.

Figure~\ref{35:fig-pointwise-vs-uniform-hyperbola} plots this phenomenon directly on the graph of $f(x) = 1/x$. Where the curve is flatter, on the right, a fairly wide window $\delta_1$ meets a given tolerance $\eps$. As $x \to 0$, the curve steepens and the window $\delta_2$ that works shrinks toward zero. No single uniform $\delta$ can serve every point in $(0, 1)$ simultaneously.

\subsection{Cauchy's Sum Theorem and the Shock of ``Monster'' Curves}

The effort to make analysis precise produced famous false theorems and unexpected examples:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cauchy's False Sum Theorem (1821)}: Augustin-Louis Cauchy published a ``proof'' in his 1821 \emph{Cours d'Analyse} asserting that the limit of any convergent sequence of continuous functions is always continuous. In 1826, Niels Henrik Abel gave a counterexample: the Fourier series (an infinite sum of sine waves)
  \[
  \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\sin(nx)}{n} = \sin x + \frac{\sin 2x}{2} + \frac{\sin 3x}{3} + \cdots
  \]
  converges to a \emph{sawtooth wave}, a graph made of straight sloping segments separated by vertical jumps, over and over. Every partial sum (the sum of the first $N$ terms) is a finite sum of sine waves and so is continuous, yet the limit jumps at $x = 0, \pm 2\pi, \pm 4\pi, \dots$. Figure~\ref{35:fig-abel-sawtooth} shows the partial sum with $N = 8$ next to the limit.

  The modern textbook counterexample is even simpler: consider $f_n(x) = x^n$ on the closed interval $[0, 1]$. Each function $f_n$ is a smooth, continuous curve passing from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$. For any fixed $x \in [0, 1)$, $\lim_{n \to \infty} x^n = 0$. But at $x = 1$, $1^n = 1 \to 1$. The pointwise limit function is
  \[
  f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } 0 \le x < 1, \\ 1 & \text{if } x = 1, \end{cases}
  \]
  which has a jump discontinuity at $x = 1$ (its value leaps from $0$ to $1$ there). A sequence of continuous curves has converged pointwise (that is, at each $x$ separately) to a discontinuous limit function.
  
  For decades, mathematicians argued over what Cauchy had overlooked. In 1847, Philipp Ludwig von Seidel and George Gabriel Stokes isolated the missing concept, later made precise by Weierstrass: \emph{uniform convergence}. Cauchy had assumed only that the functions $f_n$ converge pointwise to $f$ on the domain $D$:
  \[
  \forall x \in D, \; \forall \eps > 0, \; \exists N \in \N, \; \forall n \ge N, \quad |f_n(x) - f(x)| < \eps,
  \]
  where $N$ can depend on both $\eps$ and the location $x$. But preserving continuity requires uniform convergence:
  \[
  \forall \eps > 0, \; \exists N \in \N, \; \forall x \in D, \; \forall n \ge N, \quad |f_n(x) - f(x)| < \eps,
  \]
  where a single index $N$ traps the entire curve inside an $\eps$-corridor around $f(x)$ simultaneously. Figure~\ref{35:fig-uniform-tube} draws this corridor: a band of height $\eps$ above and below the whole graph of $f$. Swapping two adjacent quantifiers had created a controversy lasting a quarter of a century.

\subsection{The Nested Interval Theorem and Completeness}

Why are the real numbers $\R$ the standard setting for calculus, whereas the rational numbers $\Q$ are insufficient? The answer is \textbf{completeness}: the real line has no gaps. One way to state it: every Cauchy sequence of real numbers (a sequence whose terms eventually stay as close to one another as you like, defined precisely in Exercise~5) converges to a limit that is itself a real number. An equivalent form, which we use below, is the \textbf{Least Upper Bound Property}: every non-empty set of real numbers that is bounded above (some number sits at or above all its elements) has a least upper bound (a \emph{supremum}, Chapter~16) in $\R$. The rationals fail this test: the set $\{q \in \Q : q^2 < 2\}$ is bounded above, but no rational number is its least upper bound.

A geometric form of completeness is the \textbf{Nested Interval Theorem}. In it, $\sup_n a_n$ means the supremum of the set $\{a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots\}$, and $\inf_n b_n$ the infimum (greatest lower bound) of $\{b_1, b_2, b_3, \dots\}$.

\begin{theorem}[Nested Interval Theorem]
Let $(I_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ be a sequence of closed, bounded intervals $I_n = [a_n, b_n]$ in $\R$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The intervals are nested: $I_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq I_3 \supseteq \cdots$.
  \item Their lengths shrink to zero: $\lim_{n \to \infty} (b_n - a_n) = 0$.
\end{enumerate}
Then their intersection contains exactly one point:
\[
\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty I_n = \{c\}, \qquad \text{where } c = \sup_{n} a_n = \inf_{n} b_n.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Because the intervals are nested ($I_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq I_3 \supseteq \cdots$), their left endpoints $a_n$ move rightward (non-decreasing) while their right endpoints $b_n$ move leftward (non-increasing):
\[
a_1 \le a_2 \le a_3 \le \cdots \le b_3 \le b_2 \le b_1.
\]
In particular, every left endpoint $a_n$ is less than or equal to every right endpoint $b_m$. (If $n \le m$, then $a_n \le a_m \le b_m$; if $n > m$, then $a_n \le b_n \le b_m$.)

Therefore, the set of left endpoints $A = \{a_n : n \in \N\}$ is non-empty and bounded above by every $b_m$. By the completeness of the real numbers (the Least Upper Bound Property), $A$ has a supremum in $\R$:
\[
c = \sup_{n} a_n.
\]
By definition of supremum, $a_n \le c$ for all $n \in \N$. Also, because every $b_m$ is an upper bound for $A$, the least upper bound cannot exceed any $b_m$, so $c \le b_m$ for all $m \in \N$. Combining these inequalities gives
\[
a_n \le c \le b_n \quad \text{for all } n \in \N,
\]
which proves that $c \in I_n$ for every $n$, so $c \in \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty I_n$.

To see that $c$ is the \emph{only} point in the intersection, suppose $c$ and $d$ both belong to $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty I_n$ with $c < d$. Then for every $n$, both points lie inside $[a_n, b_n]$, which forces the interval width to satisfy
\[
b_n - a_n \ge d - c > 0.
\]
Taking the limit as $n \to \infty$ contradicts hypothesis~(2) that $\lim_{n \to \infty} (b_n - a_n) = 0$. Hence the intersection contains exactly one point. Finally, the mirror-image argument (the right endpoints are non-empty and bounded below by every $a_n$, so $\inf_n b_n$ exists and satisfies $a_n \le \inf_n b_n \le b_n$ for all $n$) shows that $\inf_n b_n$ also lies in every $I_n$; by uniqueness, $\inf_n b_n = c$.
\end{proof}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Why does the theorem require intervals to be \emph{closed} $[a_n, b_n]$? Consider the open intervals $J_n = (0, 1/n)$ on $\R$, drawn in Figure~\ref{35:fig-open-intervals}. For any $x > 0$, by the Archimedean property (Chapter~20) there is some $n$ with $1/n \le x$, so $x \notin J_n$. And $0 \notin J_n$ by definition. The intersection is empty. The requirement that each interval be closed guarantees that the limit point belongs to every interval.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Scratchpad versus Presentation: Working Backward, Writing Forward}

Writing an analysis proof requires switching between two opposing modes of thought:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Scratchpad (Reverse Engineering)}: You begin by assuming the desired conclusion $|f(x) - L| < \eps$. You manipulate the expression backwards to see how small $|x - c|$ must be forced to become.
  \item \textbf{The Polished Proof (Forward Deduction)}: You discard the backwards scratchwork. You announce: ``Let $\eps > 0$ be given. Set $\delta = \dots$''. You then verify that whenever $0 < |x - c| < \delta$, the chain of inequalities leads forward to $|f(x) - L| < \eps$.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{35:fig-scratchpad-vs-proof} contrasts the two directions. The scratchpad works backwards from the target bound $|f(x) - L| < \eps$ to find what $\delta$ is needed. The polished proof runs forward: it states $\delta$ first, then proves that $|x - c| < \delta$ guarantees $|f(x) - L| < \eps$. In the figure, $f(x) - L$ has been factored as $(x - c)\,g(x)$, and $M$ is a bound on $|g(x)|$ for $x$ within distance $1$ of $c$; the example of $x^2$ below, with $g(x) = x + 2$ and $M = 5$, follows exactly this pattern.

As a first example, we prove from first principles that $\lim_{x \to 2} (3x - 1) = 5$.

\paragraph{Stage 1: The Scratchpad.}
We want $|(3x - 1) - 5| < \eps$. Simplifying gives
\begin{align*}
|(3x - 1) - 5| < \eps &\iff |3x - 6| < \eps \\
&\iff 3|x - 2| < \eps \\
&\iff |x - 2| < \frac{\eps}{3}.
\end{align*}
This tells us that if we enforce $|x - 2| < \eps/3$, the desired bound will hold. We have found our candidate: $\delta = \eps/3$.

\paragraph{Stage 2: The Polished Forward Proof.}
Now we write down the forward argument, giving the reader no hint of the backwards exploration:
\begin{quote}
Let $\eps > 0$ be given. Choose $\delta = \eps/3$. Assume $0 < |x - 2| < \delta$. Then
\begin{align*}
|(3x - 1) - 5| &= |3x - 6| \\
&= 3|x - 2| \\
&< 3\delta && \text{(because } |x - 2| < \delta\text{)} \\
&= 3 \cdot \frac{\eps}{3} = \eps.
\end{align*}
Because $\eps > 0$ was arbitrary, this proves that $\lim_{x \to 2} (3x - 1) = 5$.
\end{quote}

What happens when the function is nonlinear, like $f(x) = x^2$ near $c = 2$? On the scratchpad, we want:
\[
|x^2 - 4| = |x - 2| \cdot |x + 2| < \eps.
\]
Here we cannot divide by $|x + 2|$, because $\delta$ must be a fixed constant chosen before $x$ is known. We resolve this by putting a preliminary ceiling on $\delta$. We agree in advance that $\delta \le 1$. If $|x - 2| < 1$, then $1 < x < 3$, which guarantees that $|x + 2| < 5$. Under this constraint:
\[
|x^2 - 4| = |x - 2| \cdot |x + 2| < 5|x - 2|.
\]
To make $5|x - 2| < \eps$, we need $|x - 2| < \eps/5$. Combining our two requirements, we choose $\delta = \min\{1, \, \eps/5\}$. In the polished forward proof, you state this $\delta$ immediately and verify both bounds: if $0 < |x - 2| < \delta$, then $|x - 2| < 1$, so $|x + 2| < 5$, and also $|x - 2| < \eps/5$. Hence
\begin{align*}
|x^2 - 4| &= |x - 2| \cdot |x + 2| \\
&< 5\,|x - 2| && \text{(because } \delta \le 1\text{)} \\
&< 5 \cdot \frac{\eps}{5} = \eps && \text{(because } \delta \le \eps/5\text{)}.
\end{align*}
Figure~\ref{35:fig-delta-choices} draws both examples: the band of height $\eps$ around the limit, and the window of half-width $\delta$ that keeps the graph inside it.

\begin{trapbox}
Never include your backwards scratch calculations inside the final proof as if they were deductions. If you write:
\[
|3x - 6| < \eps \implies 3|x - 2| < \eps \implies |x - 2| < \frac{\eps}{3},
\]
you have shown only that \emph{if} the conclusion were true, \emph{then} $x$ would be close to $2$. A proof must show the exact reverse: that closeness of $x$ \emph{guarantees} the conclusion.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{What Quantifier Discipline Makes Possible---and Its Limits}

Weierstrass's quantifier-based definitions gave rigorous proofs of key results in analysis:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{When Limits Can Be Swapped}: The quantified definitions state exactly when two limiting operations (limits, derivatives, integrals) may be done in either order. For example, if $f_n$ converges uniformly to $f$ on $[a, b]$, then
  \[
  \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_a^b f_n(x) \, dx = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx:
  \]
  the limit of the areas equals the area of the limit. Without uniform convergence this can fail.
  \item \textbf{The Main Theorems of Calculus}: The Intermediate Value Theorem (a continuous function on $[a, b]$ takes every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$), the Extreme Value Theorem (it reaches a largest and a smallest value), the Mean Value Theorem, and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus are all proved from completeness and $\eps$-$\delta$ estimates.
  \item \textbf{Existence Theorems for Differential Equations}: A differential equation describes how a quantity changes, for example $y' = y$; a solution is a function obeying that rule. The Picard--Lindel\"of theorem says that, under mild conditions on the rule, each starting value gives exactly one solution, at least for a while. The proof uses a \emph{contraction}: a map that brings every two points strictly closer together by a fixed factor. In a complete space (one with no gaps, as $\R$ has none), repeatedly applying a contraction converges to a single point the map leaves unchanged (the Banach Fixed-Point Theorem), and that point is the solution.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
Classical $\eps$-$\delta$ analysis also has limitations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Clutter and Dependence on Distance}: $\eps$-$\delta$ proofs grow long in many dimensions, and on curved surfaces there is no simple subtraction $x - y$ to measure the distance $|x - y|$. Proofs written with one particular distance formula can hide which properties do not depend on that formula at all.
  \item \textbf{The Limits of the Riemann Integral}: The Riemann integral, the one taught in calculus, defines area by thin rectangles. It fails for functions that jump at points packed densely along the line. For example, Dirichlet's indicator function of the rationals, $\mathbf{1}_\Q$ (which equals $1$ on rationals and $0$ on irrationals), jumps in every interval, so the rectangle approximations never settle on one value (Figure~\ref{35:fig-dirichlet}). Also, a pointwise limit of Riemann-integrable functions can fail to be Riemann-integrable. As a result, collections of functions used for signals, such as $L^2$ (functions whose square has a finite area under the graph), have gaps under the Riemann integral: they are not complete. A more general integral due to Henri Lebesgue fixes this.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mental Check: The Quantifier Game}:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Negate the definition of uniform continuity: state precisely what an adversary must produce to prove that $f: D \to \R$ is \emph{not} uniformly continuous on $D$.
    \item Explain why uniform continuity on $D$ always implies pointwise continuity on $D$, but the reverse is false. Which quantifier moves where?
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Uniform Continuity on an Unbounded Domain}:
  Let $f: [1, \infty) \to \R$ be given by $f(x) = 1/x$. Prove that $f$ is uniformly continuous on $[1, \infty)$. (Show that for all $x, y \ge 1$, $|f(x) - f(y)| \le |x - y|$, and explain why choosing $\delta = \eps$ works for every pair of points simultaneously.)
  \item \textbf{Scratchpad and Proof for a Quadratic Limit}:
  Prove from the definition of limit that $\lim_{x \to 3} (x^2 - 2x) = 3$. Provide your backwards scratchpad exploration first (including how you choose a preliminary ceiling on $\delta$ to bound $|x + 1|$), followed by the complete, forward deduction proof.
  \item \textbf{Testing the Boundaries of Nested Intervals}:
  Give explicit counterexamples showing why both hypotheses in the Nested Interval Theorem are necessary:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Find a sequence of nested open intervals $J_1 \supset J_2 \supset \cdots$ whose lengths shrink to zero, but whose intersection $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty J_n$ is empty.
    \item Find a sequence of nested closed intervals $K_1 \supset K_2 \supset \cdots$ whose intersection is empty because the intervals are unbounded.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Cauchy Sequences and Completeness}:
  A sequence $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ in $\R$ is a \emph{Cauchy sequence} if for every $\eps > 0$, there exists an index $N \in \N$ such that $|a_n - a_m| < \eps$ whenever $n, m \ge N$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Prove that every convergent sequence in $\R$ is Cauchy.
    \item Give an example of a sequence of rational numbers that is Cauchy in $\Q$, but does not converge to any rational number in $\Q$. (This illustrates why completeness of $\R$ is equivalent to asserting that every Cauchy sequence converges.)
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Uniform Continuity Preserves Cauchy Sequences}:
  Let $D \subseteq \R$. Prove that if $f: D \to \R$ is uniformly continuous and $(x_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ is a Cauchy sequence of points in $D$, then the image sequence $(f(x_n))_{n=1}^\infty$ is also a Cauchy sequence. Then show that this property fails for ordinary pointwise continuous functions by considering $f(x) = 1/x$ on the domain $D = (0, 1)$ with the sequence $x_n = 1/(n+1)$.
\end{enumerate}
