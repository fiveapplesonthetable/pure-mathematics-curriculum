\section{Abstract Algebra Preview: Structure and Symmetry}
\chapterline

In Chapter~20, real analysis gave calculus rigorous foundations using $\eps$ and $\delta$. But those arguments still worked with numbers, inequalities, and distance on the real line.

Abstract algebra takes a step away from rulers and numerical measurements. Instead of asking how large an object is or how close two numbers lie, we study \textbf{structure} and \textbf{symmetry}: how transformations combine, which actions can be undone, and what patterns stay the same when a system is rearranged.

\subsection{The 300-year quest: how polynomial roots forced a revolution}

In 1545, Gerolamo Cardano's \emph{Ars Magna} published the general formula for cubic equations (found earlier by Scipione del Ferro and Niccol\`o Tartaglia), together with his student Lodovico Ferrari's solution of the quartic (degree-four) equation. For nearly three centuries afterward, a central problem in algebra was finding a general algebraic formula for the roots of a fifth-degree equation (the general quintic):
\[
a x^5 + b x^4 + c x^3 + d x^2 + e x + f = 0.
\]
Here, the \emph{roots} of a polynomial are the input values of $x$ where the formula evaluates to zero.
Mathematicians including Leonhard Euler, Joseph-Louis Lagrange, and Carl Friedrich Gauss attempted to solve the quintic using \emph{radicals}: explicit formulas built from addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and extracting $n$-th roots ($\sqrt[n]{\cdot}$), generalizing the quadratic formula taught in secondary school.

These attempts encountered a systematic obstacle. Substituting variables to eliminate terms generated auxiliary equations (called \emph{resolvents}) of degree six or higher, increasing the complexity rather than reducing it.

Why did algebraic substitutions fail to produce a formula?

In 1832, the French mathematician \'{E}variste Galois showed that whether an equation can be solved by radicals is decided by the \emph{symmetries of its roots}: the ways those roots can be \emph{permuted} (rearranged) among themselves.

Galois asked: if you permute the roots of a polynomial, which permutations preserve all algebraic relations among those roots? He called this collection of permutations a \textbf{group}---a set of actions that can be \emph{composed} (done one after another) and \emph{inverted} (undone). In 1854, the English mathematician Arthur Cayley realized that Galois's insight applied far beyond polynomials: a group can be any system of operations that obeys a short list of axioms (rules accepted as the starting point). Figure~\ref{21:fig-quest-timeline} places these events, together with the ones in the history box near the end of this chapter, on a single timeline.

\subsection{Geometric symmetry: the equilateral triangle}

To see what Galois and Cayley saw without getting lost in fifth-degree polynomials, consider an equilateral triangle sitting on a table.

Some moves leave the triangle looking exactly as it did before. You can rotate it by $120^\circ$, rotate it by $240^\circ$, or leave it alone (a rotation by $0^\circ$). You can also pick it up, flip it over across any of its three \emph{axes of symmetry} (mirror lines, each running from a corner to the midpoint of the opposite side), and set it back down. Figure~\ref{21:fig-triangle-symmetries} shows the three mirror lines and the $120^\circ$ rotation. A move like this, which does not stretch or bend the triangle and leaves it occupying the same spot, is called a \emph{symmetry} of the triangle.

In elementary geometry, the focus is on the triangle itself: its side lengths, its angles, its area. In abstract algebra, we focus on the collection of \emph{operations} that preserve the shape.

The objects we study are the transformations themselves:
\[
\{e, r, r^2, s_1, s_2, s_3\}.
\]
Here $e$ is the do-nothing identity, $r$ is counterclockwise rotation by $120^\circ$, and $r^2$ (meaning $r$ done twice) is rotation by $240^\circ$. The remaining elements $s_1, s_2, s_3$ are reflections across the three mirror lines in Figure~\ref{21:fig-triangle-symmetries}. Those axes stay fixed on the tabletop, so $s_1$ always flips across the vertical axis regardless of which vertex label currently sits at the top. Figure~\ref{21:fig-six-symmetries} shows where the corner labels end up after each of the six moves, starting from the position in Figure~\ref{21:fig-triangle-symmetries}. Each symmetry rearranges the labels $1, 2, 3$ in a different way, and these six rearrangements are all $3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 6$ possible orders of three labels. This is Galois's point of view: a symmetry can be recorded as a permutation.

What can you do with two transformations? You can perform one after the other. Doing this is called \textbf{composition}, and we write it like multiplication:
\[
a \cdot b = \text{``do } b \text{ first, then do } a\text{''}.
\]
When you compose two symmetries of the triangle, the result is always another symmetry of the triangle. If you rotate by $120^\circ$ and then rotate by $120^\circ$, you get a $240^\circ$ rotation ($r \cdot r = r^2$). If you reflect across $s_1$ and then rotate, the corners end up where a single reflection across a different axis would have put them.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} What happens if you reflect the triangle across axis $s_1$, and then immediately reflect it across $s_1$ again?
Flipping an object over and then flipping it back across the same mirror line returns every point to its original starting place: $s_1 \cdot s_1 = e$. Every reflection of the triangle undoes itself. How many times must you apply the rotation $r$ (by $120^\circ$) to return to the identity $e$?
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{The algebraic axioms of a group}

When mathematicians abstracted this behavior across geometry, number systems, and polynomial roots, they kept four properties.

\begin{definition}[Group]\label{21:def-group}
A \textbf{group} is a set $G$ equipped with a binary operation $\cdot : G \times G \to G$ (a rule combining any two elements into a third) satisfying four axioms:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Closure}: For all $a, b \in G$, the product $a \cdot b$ belongs to $G$.
\item \textbf{Associativity}: For all $a, b, c \in G$, $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$.
\item \textbf{Identity}: There exists an element $e \in G$ such that for all $a \in G$,
\[
e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a.
\]
\item \textbf{Inverses}: For each $a \in G$, there exists an element $b \in G$ such that
\[
a \cdot b = b \cdot a = e.
\]
We denote this inverse $b$ by $a^{-1}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

In plain words: closure says that combining two elements never takes you outside $G$; associativity says that in a chain such as $a \cdot b \cdot c$ it does not matter which pair you combine first (the left-to-right order of the letters stays the same); the identity $e$ is a do-nothing element; and the inverse $a^{-1}$ undoes $a$.

Commutativity ($a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ for all $a, b$, so swapping the order never changes the result) is \emph{not} on the list. A group where it does hold is called \textbf{abelian} (named after Niels Henrik Abel). A group where it fails for at least one pair is \textbf{non-abelian}.

In our triangle example, try holding the triangle, flipping it across $s_1$, and then rotating counterclockwise by $120^\circ$ ($r \cdot s_1$). Now reset the triangle to its original position and do the operations in the opposite order: rotate by $120^\circ$ first, and then flip across $s_1$ ($s_1 \cdot r$). The corners end up in different positions, as traced in Figure~\ref{21:fig-noncommutative-composition}. The group of symmetries of the triangle is called the \textbf{dihedral group} $D_3$. (``Dihedral'' means ``two-faced'': the flips show the triangle's back face. The \textbf{order} of a group is its number of elements, so $D_3$ has order 6.) Since $r \cdot s_1 \neq s_1 \cdot r$, the group $D_3$ is non-abelian.

\subsection{Examples and non-examples across mathematics}

Groups appear in many different settings:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Integers under addition} $(\Z, +)$: The identity is $0$, the inverse of $n$ is $-n$, and addition is associative. This is an abelian group because $a+b = b+a$.
\item \textbf{Nonzero rational numbers under multiplication} $(\Q \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$: The identity is $1$, and the inverse of $p/q$ is $q/p$. (The number $0$ must be excluded because it has no multiplicative inverse: no number times $0$ equals $1$.)
\item \textbf{Invertible $n \times n$ real matrices} $\mathrm{GL}_n(\R)$ under matrix multiplication (``GL'' stands for ``general linear''; a matrix $A$ is \emph{invertible} if some matrix $A^{-1}$ satisfies $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I$): The identity is the $n \times n$ identity matrix $I$, every invertible matrix has its inverse $A^{-1}$ in the set, and matrix multiplication is associative. For $n \ge 2$, matrix multiplication is non-commutative.
\item \textbf{Permutations of a finite set} $S_n$: The set of all bijections (one-to-one pairings) from $\{1, 2, \dots, n\}$ to itself under function composition. Each one is a rearrangement of the numbers $1, \dots, n$; the identity leaves every number in place, and the inverse of a rearrangement is its inverse function.
\end{itemize}

Systems that \textbf{fail} to be groups show why each axiom is needed:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Integers under multiplication} $(\Z, \cdot)$ \emph{fails inverses}: The number $1$ acts as an identity, but most integers have no multiplicative inverse inside $\Z$. For example, the inverse of $2$ would need to be $1/2$, which is not an integer.
\item \textbf{Natural numbers under addition} $(\N, +)$ \emph{fails identity and inverses}: In the set $\N = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, there is no element $e$ satisfying $n + e = n$, and no positive integer has an additive inverse in $\N$.
\item \textbf{Integers under subtraction} $(\Z, -)$ \emph{fails associativity}: The result of subtraction depends on grouping:
\[
(8 - 4) - 2 = 4 - 2 = 2, \qquad \text{but} \qquad 8 - (4 - 2) = 8 - 2 = 6.
\]
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Proving theorems purely from the axioms}

When you prove a theorem using only the group axioms, the conclusion holds in every group, without needing separate proofs for integers, matrices, or geometric transformations.

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of Identity]\label{21:thm-identity-unique}
In any group $G$, the identity element is unique.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $e$ and $e'$ are both identity elements in $G$.
Because $e$ is an identity element, multiplying it by any element leaves that element unchanged. In particular, for the element $e'$, we have:
\[
e \cdot e' = e'.
\]
On the other hand, because $e'$ is also an identity element, multiplying any element by $e'$ leaves that element unchanged. Applying this to the element $e$, we have:
\[
e \cdot e' = e.
\]
Putting the two facts together:
\begin{align*}
e &= e \cdot e' && \text{($e'$ is an identity)} \\
  &= e'         && \text{($e$ is an identity)}.
\end{align*}
Therefore, $e = e'$. There cannot be two distinct identity elements.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of Inverses]\label{21:thm-inverse-unique}
In any group $G$, each element has a unique inverse.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $a \in G$, and suppose that both $b$ and $c$ satisfy the definition of an inverse for $a$. That means:
\[
a \cdot b = b \cdot a = e \quad \text{and} \quad a \cdot c = c \cdot a = e.
\]
We examine the three-element product $c \cdot a \cdot b$. By associativity, we can group this product in two different ways without changing the final result:
\[
(c \cdot a) \cdot b = c \cdot (a \cdot b).
\]
Now replace each inner pair by $e$ and follow the chain from $b$ to $c$:
\begin{align*}
b &= e \cdot b             && \text{(identity)} \\
  &= (c \cdot a) \cdot b   && \text{($c$ is an inverse of $a$, so $c \cdot a = e$)} \\
  &= c \cdot (a \cdot b)   && \text{(associativity)} \\
  &= c \cdot e             && \text{($b$ is an inverse of $a$, so $a \cdot b = e$)} \\
  &= c                     && \text{(identity)}.
\end{align*}
So $b = c$: the inverse of $a$ is unique.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Inverse of a Product]\label{21:thm-inverse-product}
In any group $G$, for all $a, b \in G$, the inverse of the product $a \cdot b$ is given by reversing the order of their individual inverses:
\[
(a \cdot b)^{-1} = b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the definition of an inverse, we must verify that multiplying $(a \cdot b)$ by $(b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1})$ on either side produces the identity element $e$.

Multiplying on the right and applying associativity repeatedly:
\begin{align*}
(a \cdot b) \cdot (b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}) &= a \cdot (b \cdot b^{-1}) \cdot a^{-1} && \text{(associativity)} \\
&= a \cdot e \cdot a^{-1} && \text{(inverse: $b \cdot b^{-1} = e$)} \\
&= a \cdot a^{-1} && \text{(identity: $a \cdot e = a$)} \\
&= e && \text{(inverse)}.
\end{align*}
Multiplying on the left and applying associativity:
\begin{align*}
(b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}) \cdot (a \cdot b) &= b^{-1} \cdot (a^{-1} \cdot a) \cdot b && \text{(associativity)} \\
&= b^{-1} \cdot e \cdot b && \text{(inverse: $a^{-1} \cdot a = e$)} \\
&= b^{-1} \cdot b && \text{(identity: $b^{-1} \cdot e = b^{-1}$)} \\
&= e && \text{(inverse)}.
\end{align*}
Since $(b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1})$ acts as a valid two-sided inverse for $(a \cdot b)$, and inverses in any group are unique by Theorem~\ref{21:thm-inverse-unique}, we conclude that $(a \cdot b)^{-1} = b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
In these proofs, we did not appeal to numbers, division, or geometric reflections. We used only the group axioms: associativity, the identity, and inverses. The associative property allowed us to slide parentheses across products like $c \cdot a \cdot b$ and $(a \cdot b) \cdot (b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1})$, shifting our focus from outer elements to the adjacent inner inverse pair.

Theorem~\ref{21:thm-inverse-product} is often called the \textbf{socks-and-shoes principle} (illustrated in Figure~\ref{21:fig-socks-and-shoes}). Under our composition rule $a \cdot b = \text{``do } b \text{ first, then do } a\text{''}$, suppose $b$ puts on your socks and $a$ puts on your shoes; your morning routine is $a \cdot b$. At night, you cannot pull off your socks while your shoes are still on. You must undo the actions in reverse order: apply $a^{-1}$ (shoes off) first, then $b^{-1}$ (socks off) second, which is $b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{historybox}
Why did the general fifth-degree polynomial resist solution by radicals for three centuries?

In 1799, the Italian physician and mathematician Paolo Ruffini published a treatise arguing that no formula using radicals (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and $n$-th roots) could solve the general quintic. But his argument contained subtle gaps, and his contemporaries largely dismissed it. In 1824, the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel published the first rigorous proof of impossibility in a six-page pamphlet. Yet Abel's proof was an intricate technical calculation; it showed that a radical formula could not exist, but it did not explain the underlying structural reason why degree-four equations admit a radical formula while general quintic equations do not.

The structural answer came from \'{E}variste Galois. Between 1829 and 1831, Galois submitted memoirs on root permutations to the French Academy of Sciences. Augustin-Louis Cauchy lost one manuscript, Joseph Fourier took another home and died before reading it, and in 1831 Sim\'eon Denis Poisson rejected Galois's revision as ``incomprehensible'' in his report. On May 29, 1832, on the eve of a fatal duel at age twenty, Galois spent his final hours annotating his papers. In his parting letter to his friend Auguste Chevalier, he wrote, ``Ask Jacobi or Gauss publicly to give their opinion, not on the truth, but on the importance of these theorems,'' before signing off. He was shot the next morning and died the following day.
\end{historybox}

\begin{historybox}
Galois showed that whether an equation can be solved by radicals depends on the \emph{group of permutations of its roots}. Each time a formula takes an $n$-th root, it brings in new numbers whose symmetries just cycle around, and cyclic symmetries form an abelian group. So an equation is solvable by radicals exactly when its symmetry group can be taken apart, step by step, into abelian pieces; groups that can be taken apart this way are now called \emph{solvable}.
For the general quintic, the symmetry group is $S_5$, all $120$ rearrangements of the five roots. Inside it sits the \emph{alternating group} $A_5$: the $60$ rearrangements that can be made by an even number of swaps of two roots. $A_5$ is non-abelian and \emph{simple}, meaning it cannot be split into smaller pieces at all. (In technical terms, it has no \emph{normal subgroups} other than itself and $\{e\}$; Chapter~32 builds quotient groups and Chapter~36 defines normal subgroups.) So the quintic's symmetry cannot be taken apart into abelian pieces, and no radical formula exists. Figure~\ref{21:fig-solvable-chains} sets this beside the four-root case, where every piece is abelian.

Arthur Cayley (1854) then defined a group by axioms instead of as a set of permutations, and Camille Jordan (1870) published the first comprehensive book on the subject.

Yet one foundational question remains open. Galois proved that every polynomial with rational coefficients produces a finite symmetry group. In 1892, David Hilbert asked the reverse: \textbf{The Inverse Galois Problem}. Can \emph{every} finite group appear as the Galois root-symmetry group of some polynomial with rational coefficients? Over 130 years later, despite much progress by mathematicians like Igor Shafarevich (who proved the conjecture holds for all solvable groups in 1954), the general question for all finite groups remains an active unsolved problem in pure mathematics.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The reach of symmetry}

Stepping back from individual formulas to study symmetry let mathematicians connect problems that looked unrelated on the surface. In geometry, the closely related algebra of field extensions settled ancient Greek construction problems (Pierre Wantzel, 1837), in which only a compass and an unmarked straightedge may be used: they prove that no such construction can cut an arbitrary angle into three equal parts, or build a cube with twice the volume of a given cube. In modern physics, Emmy Noether proved that every continuous symmetry of a physical system (one that can be applied in any amount, such as shifting time by any number of seconds) comes with a conservation law, a quantity that never changes. Because the laws of physics are the same at every moment, energy is conserved; because they are the same in every direction, angular momentum is conserved. In the Standard Model of particle physics, fundamental particles are sorted into families by how they behave under \emph{Lie groups}: groups of smooth, continuous transformations, such as the group of all rotations of space.

Yet group theory has its own natural boundaries. A group possesses only a single operation, and every transformation must be invertible. Everyday mathematics frequently involves two operations that interact, such as addition and multiplication, where elements like zero have no multiplicative inverse. To handle these settings, mathematicians developed \emph{rings} (systems with addition and multiplication, like polynomials or integers) and \emph{fields} (where division by non-zero elements is also allowed).

Groups also do not measure distance, nearness, or gradual bending. In \emph{topology}, the study of shape that ignores stretching and bending (but not tearing), a ceramic coffee mug and a doughnut count as the same shape: each has a single hole through it (genus $1$, where the genus counts holes, as in Chapter~9). Rigid symmetries alone cannot explain how to deform one smoothly into the other without tearing.

To study shapes that bend, stretch, and flow without rigid rulers or discrete operations, Chapter~22 turns to \textbf{Topology Preview: Continuity Without Rulers}. Later, Chapters~32 and~36 use equivalence relations to build new groups from old ones (normal subgroups and quotient groups); Chapter~24 introduces rings, fields, and ideals, and Chapter~36, \textbf{Second Pass: Algebra as Lawful Manipulation}, develops them.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Identity is its Own Inverse (Mental Check):} Let $G$ be a group with identity element $e$. Prove that $e^{-1} = e$. (Use only the defining axiom of the inverse.)

\item \textbf{Diagnosing Group Axioms (Mental Check):} Consider the set of all integers equipped with subtraction, $(\Z, -)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Does closure hold?
\item Does associativity hold? Test $(a - b) - c$ versus $a - (b - c)$ with concrete integers.
\item Is there an identity element $e \in \Z$ satisfying $a - e = e - a = a$ for all $a$?
\item Conclude whether $(\Z, -)$ is a group.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Inverses in $D_3$ (Mental Check):} In the dihedral group $D_3$ of the equilateral triangle:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item What is the inverse of the rotation $r$ (by $120^\circ$)?
\item What is the inverse of the rotation $r^2$ (by $240^\circ$)?
\item For any reflection $s_i \in \{s_1, s_2, s_3\}$, what is $s_i^{-1}$?
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Left and Right Cancellation Laws (Routine Practice):} Let $G$ be a group and let $a, b, c \in G$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove the left cancellation law: if $a \cdot b = a \cdot c$, then $b = c$. (Explicitly indicate where you use the existence of $a^{-1}$, associativity, and the identity axiom.)
\item Prove the right cancellation law: if $b \cdot a = c \cdot a$, then $b = c$.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Extending the Socks-and-Shoes Principle (Routine Practice):} Let $G$ be a group and let $a, b, c \in G$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Use Theorem~\ref{21:thm-inverse-product} to prove that $(a \cdot b \cdot c)^{-1} = c^{-1} \cdot b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}$.
\item Give a real-world analogy (similar to socks and shoes) for reversing a sequence of three successive actions.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Building the Group of Order 3 (Routine Practice):} Suppose a group $G$ has exactly three elements: $G = \{e, a, b\}$, where $e$ is the identity.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item By the cancellation law, no element can appear more than once in any row or column of a Cayley table (every row and column must be a permutation of $\{e, a, b\}$). Using this row-column uniqueness property, show that $a \cdot a$ cannot equal $e$ or $a$, and must therefore equal $b$.
\item Fill in all nine entries of the Cayley table for $G$.
\item Is every group of order 3 necessarily abelian? Explain why from your table.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Groups of Exponent 2 are Abelian (Deeper):} Let $G$ be a group in which every element is its own inverse: that is, $x^2 = x \cdot x = e$ for all $x \in G$. Prove that $G$ must be abelian ($a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ for all $a, b \in G$). (Hint: Consider the product $a \cdot b$, apply the hypothesis that $(a \cdot b)^2 = e$, and use the socks-and-shoes rule.)

\item \textbf{Subgroup Identity Must Match (Deeper):} Let $G$ be a group with identity $e$, and let $H \subseteq G$ be a non-empty subset that forms a group in its own right under the same operation (called a \emph{subgroup}). Let $e_H$ be the identity element of $H$. Prove that $e_H = e$. (Hint: In $H$, $e_H \cdot e_H = e_H$. Since $e_H \in G$, view this equation inside $G$ and apply cancellation.)
\end{enumerate}
