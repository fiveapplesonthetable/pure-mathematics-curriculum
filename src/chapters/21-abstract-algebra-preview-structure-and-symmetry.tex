\section{Abstract Algebra Preview: Structure and Symmetry}
\chapterline

In Chapter~20, real analysis gave calculus rigorous foundations using $\eps$ and $\delta$. But those arguments still worked with numbers, inequalities, and distance on the real line.

Abstract algebra takes a step away from rulers and numerical measurements. Instead of asking how large an object is or how close two numbers lie, we study \textbf{structure} and \textbf{symmetry}: how transformations combine, which actions can be undone, and what patterns stay the same when a system is rearranged.

\subsection{The 300-year quest: how polynomial roots forced a revolution}

For nearly three centuries after Scipione del Ferro, Niccol\`o Tartaglia, and Gerolamo Cardano discovered the general formula for cubic equations in 1545, and Lodovico Ferrari solved the quartic (degree-four) equation, a central problem in algebra was finding an algebraic formula for the roots of a fifth-degree equation (the general quintic):
\[
a x^5 + b x^4 + c x^3 + d x^2 + e x + f = 0.
\]
Here, the \emph{roots} of a polynomial are the input values of $x$ where the formula evaluates to zero. 
Mathematicians including Leonhard Euler, Joseph-Louis Lagrange, and Carl Friedrich Gauss attempted to solve the quintic using \emph{radicals}: explicit formulas built from addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and extracting $n$-th roots ($\sqrt[n]{\cdot}$), generalizing the quadratic formula taught in secondary school.

These attempts encountered a systematic obstacle. Substituting variables to eliminate terms generated auxiliary equations (called \emph{resolvents}) of degree six or higher, increasing the complexity rather than reducing it.

Why did algebraic substitutions fail to produce a formula? The difficulty was structural: solvability by radicals depends on the symmetry of the roots.

In 1832, the French mathematician \'{E}variste Galois showed that the solvability of an equation is determined not by numerical coefficients, but by the \emph{symmetries of its roots}---the ways those roots can be permuted among themselves.

Galois asked: if you permute the roots of a polynomial, which permutations preserve all algebraic relations among those roots? He called this collection of permutations a \textbf{group}---a set of actions that can be composed and inverted consistently. In 1854, the English mathematician Arthur Cayley realized that Galois's insight applied far beyond polynomials: a group is an abstract system of operations satisfying algebraic axioms.

\subsection{Geometric symmetry: the equilateral triangle}

To see what Galois and Cayley saw without getting lost in fifth-degree polynomials, consider an equilateral triangle sitting on a table.

You can perform operations on this triangle that leave it looking completely undisturbed. You can rotate it by $120^\circ$, rotate it by $240^\circ$, or leave it entirely alone (a rotation by $0^\circ$). You can also pick it up, flip it over across any of its three axes of symmetry, and set it back down.

In elementary geometry, the focus is on the triangle itself: its side lengths, its angles, its area. In abstract algebra, we focus on the collection of \emph{operations} that preserve the shape.

The objects of study are not numbers. They are the transformations themselves:
\[
\{e, r, r^2, s_1, s_2, s_3\}
\]
where $e$ is the do-nothing identity, $r$ is counterclockwise rotation by $120^\circ$, $r^2$ is rotation by $240^\circ$, and $s_1, s_2, s_3$ are reflections across the three altitude lines shown in Figure~\ref{21:fig-triangle-symmetries}.

What can you do with two transformations? You can perform one after the other. That composition is an algebraic operation:
\[
a \cdot b = \text{``do } b \text{ first, then do } a\text{''}.
\]
When you compose two symmetries of the triangle, the result is always another symmetry of the triangle. If you rotate by $120^\circ$ and then rotate by $120^\circ$, you get a $240^\circ$ rotation ($r \cdot r = r^2$). If you reflect across $s_1$ and then rotate, the vertices land in a configuration identical to having performed a single reflection across a different axis.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} What happens if you reflect the triangle across axis $s_1$, and then immediately reflect it across $s_1$ again? 
Flipping an object over and then flipping it back across the same mirror line returns every point to its original starting place: $s_1 \cdot s_1 = e$. Every reflection in $D_3$ undoes itself! How many times must you apply rotation $r = +120^\circ$ to return to the identity $e$?
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{The algebraic axioms of a group}

When mathematicians abstracted this behavior across geometry, number systems, and polynomial roots, they extracted four fundamental properties that make the algebra work.

\begin{definition}[Group]\label{21:def-group}
A \textbf{group} is a set $G$ equipped with a binary operation $\cdot : G \times G \to G$ (a rule combining any two elements into a third) satisfying four axioms:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Closure}: For all $a, b \in G$, the product $a \cdot b$ belongs to $G$.
\item \textbf{Associativity}: For all $a, b, c \in G$, $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$.
\item \textbf{Identity}: There exists an element $e \in G$ such that for all $a \in G$,
\[
e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a.
\]
\item \textbf{Inverses}: For each $a \in G$, there exists an element $b \in G$ such that
\[
a \cdot b = b \cdot a = e.
\]
We denote this inverse $b$ by $a^{-1}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Notice that commutativity ($a \cdot b = b \cdot a$) is \emph{not} on the list. A group where the order of operations does not matter is called \textbf{abelian} (named after Niels Henrik Abel). But many important groups are \textbf{non-abelian}.

In our triangle example, try holding the triangle, flipping it across $s_1$, and then rotating counterclockwise by $120^\circ$ ($r \cdot s_1$). Now reset the triangle to its original position and do the operations in the opposite order: rotate by $120^\circ$ first, and then flip across $s_1$ ($s_1 \cdot r$). The corners end up in completely different configurations, as traced in Figure~\ref{21:fig-noncommutative-composition}! The group of symmetries of the triangle, denoted $D_3$ (the dihedral group of order 6), is non-abelian: the order of operations matters.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} In school arithmetic, addition and multiplication are commutative: $3 + 5 = 5 + 3$ and $3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3$. Because of this early experience, we often take commutativity for granted. 

In geometry and the physical world, actions rarely commute. Pick up a book: rotate it $90^\circ$ toward you, then rotate it $90^\circ$ clockwise. Reset the book and do the two rotations in the reverse order. The cover faces in a different direction! Order of operations matters.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Examples and non-examples across mathematics}

Once you have this definition, you begin recognizing groups in widely different settings:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Integers under addition} $(\Z, +)$: The identity is $0$, the inverse of $n$ is $-n$, and addition is associative. This is an abelian group because $a+b = b+a$.
\item \textbf{Nonzero rational numbers under multiplication} $(\Q \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$: The identity is $1$, and the inverse of $p/q$ is $q/p$. (The number $0$ must be excluded because it has no multiplicative inverse: no number times $0$ equals $1$.)
\item \textbf{Invertible $n \times n$ real matrices} $\mathrm{GL}_n(\R)$ under matrix multiplication: The identity is the $n \times n$ identity matrix $I$, every invertible matrix has an inverse matrix $A^{-1}$, and matrix multiplication is associative. For $n \ge 2$, matrix multiplication is non-commutative.
\item \textbf{Permutations of a finite set} $S_n$: The set of all bijections (one-to-one pairings) from $\{1, 2, \dots, n\}$ to itself under function composition.
\end{itemize}

Equally illuminating are systems that \textbf{fail} to form groups, which show why each axiom is necessary:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Integers under multiplication} $(\Z, \cdot)$ \emph{fails inverses}: The number $1$ acts as an identity, but most integers have no multiplicative inverse inside $\Z$. For example, the inverse of $2$ would need to be $1/2$, which is not an integer.
\item \textbf{Natural numbers under addition} $(\N, +)$ \emph{fails identity and inverses}: In the set $\N = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, there is no element $e$ satisfying $n + e = n$, and no positive integer has an additive inverse in $\N$.
\item \textbf{Integers under subtraction} $(\Z, -)$ \emph{fails associativity}: Subtraction depends fundamentally on grouping: $(8 - 4) - 2 = 4 - 2 = 2$, but $8 - (4 - 2) = 8 - 2 = 6$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Proving theorems purely from the axioms}

A central strength of abstract algebra is that when you prove a theorem using only the group axioms, the conclusion holds in every group, without needing separate proofs for integers, matrices, or geometric transformations.

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of Identity]\label{21:thm-identity-unique}
In any group $G$, the identity element is unique.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $e$ and $e'$ are both identity elements in $G$.
Because $e$ is an identity element, multiplying it by any element leaves that element unchanged. In particular, for the element $e'$, we have:
\[
e \cdot e' = e'.
\]
On the other hand, because $e'$ is also an identity element, multiplying any element by $e'$ leaves that element unchanged. Applying this to the element $e$, we have:
\[
e \cdot e' = e.
\]
Comparing these two equations gives:
\[
e = e \cdot e' = e'.
\]
Therefore, $e = e'$. There cannot be two distinct identity elements.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of Inverses]\label{21:thm-inverse-unique}
In any group $G$, each element has a unique inverse.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $a \in G$, and suppose that both $b$ and $c$ satisfy the definition of an inverse for $a$. That means:
\[
a \cdot b = b \cdot a = e \quad \text{and} \quad a \cdot c = c \cdot a = e.
\]
We examine the three-element product $c \cdot a \cdot b$. By associativity, we can group this product in two different ways without changing the final result:
\[
(c \cdot a) \cdot b = c \cdot (a \cdot b).
\]
Now substitute our knowledge of inverses into each side:
\begin{align*}
(c \cdot a) \cdot b &= e \cdot b = b, \\
c \cdot (a \cdot b) &= c \cdot e = c.
\end{align*}
Equating the two results gives $b = c$. The inverse of $a$ is unique.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Inverse of a Product]\label{21:thm-inverse-product}
In any group $G$, for all $a, b \in G$, the inverse of the product $a \cdot b$ is given by reversing the order of their individual inverses:
\[
(a \cdot b)^{-1} = b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the definition of an inverse, we must verify that multiplying $(a \cdot b)$ by $(b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1})$ on either side produces the identity element $e$.

Multiplying on the right and applying associativity repeatedly:
\begin{align*}
(a \cdot b) \cdot (b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}) &= a \cdot (b \cdot b^{-1}) \cdot a^{-1} \\
&= a \cdot e \cdot a^{-1} \\
&= a \cdot a^{-1} \\
&= e.
\end{align*}
Multiplying on the left and applying associativity:
\begin{align*}
(b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}) \cdot (a \cdot b) &= b^{-1} \cdot (a^{-1} \cdot a) \cdot b \\
&= b^{-1} \cdot e \cdot b \\
&= b^{-1} \cdot b \\
&= e.
\end{align*}
Since $(b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1})$ acts as a valid two-sided inverse for $(a \cdot b)$, and inverses in any group are unique by Theorem~\ref{21:thm-inverse-unique}, we conclude that $(a \cdot b)^{-1} = b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
In these proofs, we did not appeal to numbers, division, or geometric reflections. We used only associativity and the definition of the identity element. The associative property allowed us to slide parentheses across products like $c \cdot a \cdot b$ and $(a \cdot b) \cdot (b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1})$, shifting our focus from outer elements to the adjacent inner inverse pair.

Theorem~\ref{21:thm-inverse-product} is the familiar \textbf{socks-and-shoes principle} (illustrated in Figure~\ref{21:fig-socks-and-shoes}): in the morning, you put on your socks first, then put on your shoes ($a \cdot b$). At night, to reverse the entire process, you cannot pull off your socks while your shoes are still on! You must undo the actions in reverse order: take off your shoes first, and then take off your socks ($b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}$).
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{historybox}
Why did the general fifth-degree polynomial resist solution by radicals for three centuries? 

In 1799, the Italian physician and mathematician Paolo Ruffini published a treatise arguing that no formula using radicals (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and $n$-th roots) could solve the general quintic. But his argument contained subtle gaps, and his contemporaries largely dismissed it. In 1824, the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel published the first rigorous proof of impossibility in a six-page pamphlet. Yet Abel's proof was an intricate technical calculation; it showed that a radical formula could not exist, but it did not explain the underlying structural reason why degree-four equations admit a radical formula while general quintic equations do not.

The structural answer came from \'{E}variste Galois. Between 1829 and 1831, Galois submitted memoirs on root permutations to the French Academy of Sciences: Augustin-Louis Cauchy lost one manuscript, Joseph Fourier took another home and died before reading it, and in 1831 Sim\'eon Denis Poisson rejected Galois's revision as ``incomprehensible.'' On May 29, 1832, on the eve of a fatal duel at age twenty, Galois spent his final hours annotating his papers, writing to his friend Auguste Chevalier: ``Ask Jacobi or Gauss publicly to give their opinion, not on the truth, but on the importance of these theorems.'' He was shot the next morning and died the following day.

Galois showed that an equation's solvability is dictated by the \emph{symmetry group of permutations of its roots}. Taking an $n$-th root corresponds algebraically to adjoining elements whose symmetry is cyclic (and hence abelian). An equation is solvable by radicals if and only if its symmetry group can be decomposed step-by-step into nested abelian quotient groups (a property now called \emph{solvable}). 
The general quintic fails because its core root symmetry is the alternating group $A_5$ (the sixty even rearrangements of five roots). This group is \emph{simple}: it contains no non-trivial normal subgroups, so it cannot be factored into simpler pieces. The quintic cannot be solved by radicals because its symmetry cannot be broken down.

Arthur Cayley (1854) subsequently freed group theory from permutations, defining abstract groups by relational axioms, and Camille Jordan (1870) published the first comprehensive treatise on the subject. 

Yet one foundational question remains open. Galois proved that every polynomial with rational coefficients produces a finite symmetry group. In 1892, David Hilbert asked the reverse: \textbf{The Inverse Galois Problem}. Can \emph{every} finite group appear as the Galois root-symmetry group of some polynomial with rational coefficients? Over 130 years later, despite vast progress by mathematicians like Igor Shafarevich (who proved the conjecture holds for all solvable groups in 1954), the general question for all finite groups remains an active unsolved problem in pure mathematics.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The reach of symmetry}

Stepping back from individual formulas to study symmetry let mathematicians connect problems that looked unrelated on the surface. In geometry, Galois theory settled ancient Greek compass-and-straightedge problems, proving that trisecting an arbitrary angle or doubling the volume of a cube is algebraically impossible. In modern physics, Emmy Noether proved that every continuous physical symmetry corresponds directly to a conservation law---invariance under time translation yields conservation of energy, while rotational invariance yields conservation of angular momentum. In the Standard Model of particle physics, fundamental particles are classified using the representations of Lie groups (continuous groups of smooth geometric transformations).

Yet group theory has its own natural boundaries. A group possesses only a single operation, and every transformation must be invertible. Everyday mathematics frequently involves two operations that interact, such as addition and multiplication, where elements like zero have no multiplicative inverse. To handle these richer environments, mathematicians developed \emph{rings} (systems with addition and multiplication, like polynomials or integers) and \emph{fields} (where division by non-zero elements is also allowed).

Even more fundamentally, groups capture discrete or continuous symmetries, but they do not measure distance, nearness, or continuous deformation. A ceramic coffee mug and a doughnut share the same basic topological shape---each is a surface with a single hole through it (genus $1$)---yet rigid algebraic symmetries alone cannot explain how to deform one smoothly into the other without tearing.

To study shapes that bend, stretch, and flow without rigid rulers or discrete operations, Chapter~22 turns to \textbf{Topology Preview: Continuity Without Rulers}. Later, Chapter~33 develops equivalence relations into normal subgroups and quotient structures, while Chapter~36 develops \textbf{Second Pass: Algebra as Lawful Manipulation}, introducing rings, fields, and ideals.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Identity is its Own Inverse (Mental Check):} Let $G$ be a group with identity element $e$. Prove that $e^{-1} = e$. (Use only the defining axiom of the inverse.)

\item \textbf{Diagnosing Group Axioms (Mental Check):} Consider the set of all integers equipped with subtraction, $(\Z, -)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Does closure hold?
\item Does associativity hold? Test $(a - b) - c$ versus $a - (b - c)$ with concrete integers.
\item Is there an identity element $e \in \Z$ satisfying $a - e = e - a = a$ for all $a$?
\item Conclude whether $(\Z, -)$ is a group.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Inverses in $D_3$ (Mental Check):} In the dihedral group $D_3$ of the equilateral triangle:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item What is the inverse of the rotation $r = +120^\circ$?
\item What is the inverse of the rotation $r^2 = +240^\circ$?
\item For any reflection $s_i \in \{s_1, s_2, s_3\}$, what is $s_i^{-1}$?
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Left and Right Cancellation Laws (Routine Practice):} Let $G$ be a group and let $a, b, c \in G$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove the left cancellation law: if $a \cdot b = a \cdot c$, then $b = c$. (Explicitly indicate where you use the existence of $a^{-1}$, associativity, and the identity axiom.)
\item Prove the right cancellation law: if $b \cdot a = c \cdot a$, then $b = c$.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Extending the Socks-and-Shoes Principle (Routine Practice):} Let $G$ be a group and let $a, b, c \in G$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Use Theorem~\ref{21:thm-inverse-product} to prove that $(a \cdot b \cdot c)^{-1} = c^{-1} \cdot b^{-1} \cdot a^{-1}$.
\item Give a real-world analogy (similar to socks and shoes) for reversing a sequence of three successive actions.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Building the Group of Order 3 (Routine Practice):} Suppose a group $G$ has exactly three elements: $G = \{e, a, b\}$, where $e$ is the identity.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item By the cancellation law, no element can appear more than once in any row or column of a Cayley table (every row and column must be a permutation of $\{e, a, b\}$). Using this row-column uniqueness property, show that $a \cdot a$ cannot equal $e$ or $a$, and must therefore equal $b$.
\item Fill in all nine entries of the Cayley table for $G$.
\item Is every group of order 3 necessarily abelian? Explain why from your table.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Groups of Exponent 2 are Abelian (Deeper):} Let $G$ be a group in which every element is its own inverse: that is, $x^2 = x \cdot x = e$ for all $x \in G$. Prove that $G$ must be abelian ($a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ for all $a, b \in G$). (Hint: Consider the product $a \cdot b$, apply the hypothesis that $(a \cdot b)^2 = e$, and use the socks-and-shoes rule.)

\item \textbf{Subgroup Identity Must Match (Deeper):} Let $G$ be a group with identity $e$, and let $H \subseteq G$ be a non-empty subset that forms a group in its own right under the same operation (called a \emph{subgroup}). Let $e_H$ be the identity element of $H$. Prove that $e_H = e$. (Hint: In $H$, $e_H \cdot e_H = e_H$. Since $e_H \in G$, view this equation inside $G$ and apply cancellation.)
\end{enumerate}
