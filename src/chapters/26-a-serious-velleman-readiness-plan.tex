\section{A Serious Velleman Readiness Plan}
\chapterline

\subsection{The great transition crisis: why students hit the analysis wall}

In Chapter~25, we treated stalled proofs like programs with diagnosable bugs. But debugging is reactive: it repairs an argument that has already gone off the rails. When facing a blank page, you need a proactive plan for constructing proofs from the start.

At university, the step from the early courses, which are mostly calculation, to the later courses, which are built on proof, is a well-known hurdle. Students who do well in calculus and computational linear algebra often find real analysis and abstract algebra surprisingly hard.

The goal changes. Earlier courses ask you to follow step-by-step procedures that produce a number or a formula. Proof-based courses ask you to work with exact definitions, to read statements with several nested quantifiers, and to build arguments in which every step follows from earlier ones.

In 1994, the American mathematician Daniel J. Velleman wrote a book for this step, \emph{How to Prove It: A Structured Approach}. His idea was that proof writing can be taught as a set of explicit methods. Structured programming in the 1970s made programs readable by replacing tangled jumps with functions, loops, and variables that exist only inside a clearly marked block. In the same way, Velleman taught proofs as a craft: break the goal into smaller goals, keep track of where each variable is introduced and what it may stand for (its \emph{scope}, Chapter~3), and pick a proof strategy that fits the logical form of the claim.

\subsection{Building the four-tier conceptual pyramid}
\label{26:sec-pyramid}

Readers of introductory proof texts often find the early chapters on truth tables easy, then get stuck once nested quantifiers, families of sets (sets whose elements are themselves sets), and relations appear. From that point on, a single proof has to combine several definitions and keep track of the scope of several variables.

This plan prepares you for proof-based textbooks. Readiness comes from four layers of habits, each resting on the one below, rather than from a collection of separate tricks (Figure~\ref{26:fig-pyramid}). The bottom layer is \emph{propositional logic}: the logic of whole statements joined by ``and'', ``or'', ``not'', and ``if \dots\ then'', before any quantifiers appear.

What each layer contains:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Tier 1 (Propositional Logic)}: \emph{Propositional logic} is the logic of whole statements joined by connectives, without looking inside the statements at variables or quantifiers. You master logical connectives ($\land$ for ``and'', $\lor$ for ``or'', $\neg$ for ``not'', $\implies$ for ``implies''), truth conditions, and the contrapositive (proving ``if $P$ then $Q$'' by establishing the equivalent statement ``if not $Q$ then not $P$'').
\item \textbf{Tier 2 (Quantifiers \& Scope)}: You keep track of where each variable is introduced and what it may stand for. You distinguish between universal statements ($\forall$, which demand introducing an arbitrary element) and existential statements ($\exists$, which demand producing a concrete witness).
\item \textbf{Tier 3 (Sets \& Functions)}: Sets are the objects most proofs are about. You prove set equality via double containment (showing both $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq A$), track function images and preimages, and test for injectivity and surjectivity.
\item \textbf{Tier 4 (Structural Thinking)}: You handle equivalence relations, build quotient sets, and carry out mathematical induction.
\end{itemize}

\begin{walkbox}
Suppose you want to prove that the linear function $f: \R \to \R$ given by $f(x) = 3x + 1$ is injective. You pick up your pencil and write: ``Let $x_1, x_2 \in \R$ be arbitrary, and assume $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$.'' Which layer of the pyramid decided that opening sentence? All three lower layers did. Tier~3 says what injective means, Tier~2 says that a ``for all'' claim starts with arbitrary elements, and Tier~1 says that an ``if \dots\ then'' claim starts by assuming the ``if'' part.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{The four core readiness milestones}
\label{26:sec-milestones}

Before tackling Velleman's exercises, you should be able to execute four foundational tasks without hesitation:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Translate fluently between English and symbolic logic}: You can take an informal mathematical sentence (such as ``every nonzero integer divides zero'') and write down its exact quantified statement,
\[
\forall n \in \Z \setminus \{0\}, \; \exists k \in \Z, \; 0 = k \cdot n,
\]
attaching a quantifier to each free variable of the predicate $0 = k \cdot n$ so that every variable's role is clear. Figure~\ref{26:fig-translation} shows which piece of the sentence becomes which piece of the formula.
\item \textbf{Negate nested quantifiers cleanly}: When faced with a statement with several nested quantifiers, such as $\forall \eps > 0 \; \exists \delta > 0 \; \forall x \; (A \implies B)$, where $A$ and $B$ are statements about $x$, you move the $\neg$ inward one quantifier at a time, flipping each one ($\forall \leftrightarrow \exists$), and negate the inner implication correctly using $\neg(A \implies B) \equiv A \land \neg B$, rather than mistakenly negating the premise $A$:
\begin{align*}
\neg\bigl(\forall \eps > 0 \; \exists \delta > 0 \; \forall x \; (A \implies B)\bigr)
  &\equiv \exists \eps > 0 \; \neg\bigl(\exists \delta > 0 \; \forall x \; (A \implies B)\bigr) \\
  &\equiv \exists \eps > 0 \; \forall \delta > 0 \; \neg\bigl(\forall x \; (A \implies B)\bigr) \\
  &\equiv \exists \eps > 0 \; \forall \delta > 0 \; \exists x \; \neg(A \implies B) \\
  &\equiv \exists \eps > 0 \; \forall \delta > 0 \; \exists x \; (A \land \neg B).
\end{align*}
\item \textbf{Chase elements across sets}: To prove a set inclusion like $A \cap (B \cup C) \subseteq (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$, you pick an arbitrary element $x$ in the left set, expand the definitions of intersection ($\land$) and union ($\lor$), and trace where $x$ lands using logic rules (Figure~\ref{26:fig-element-chase}).
\item \textbf{Distinguish hypothesis from conclusion}: You never assume what you are trying to prove, and you recognize whether an argument calls for direct deduction, contrapositive, or contradiction.
\end{enumerate}

To turn these milestones into a routine, Velleman introduced the \emph{Given--Goal} method. Facing a blank page, you do not start writing from left to right and hope. You first look at the outermost logical form of the claim (its main quantifier or connective) and use it to set up two shelves: what you know (the Given shelf) and what you must establish (the Goal shelf), as visualized in Figure~\ref{26:fig-architecture}.

\subsection{Two common study traps: passive reading and calculation drift}
\label{26:sec-traps}

Before beginning daily practice, watch for two common study traps that slow progress.

The first hazard is the \textbf{passive reading trap}. When reading a published proof in a textbook, each step follows logically from the preceding one. Checking an existing argument is largely a process of verifying deductions. But writing a proof on a blank page requires a different skill: you must work backward from the goal, set up arbitrary variables, decide whether to proceed directly or by contrapositive or contradiction, and discard approaches that lead nowhere. Reading alone does not develop the habit of structuring arguments independently. Figure~\ref{26:fig-reading-vs-generation} contrasts the two activities.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The illusion of the solved example}: When you read a completed proof line by line, every step makes sense, so it feels as if you understand the material. But recognizing a correct step is a different skill from producing one. When you close the book and face a blank sheet of paper, the printed steps are no longer there to lean on. Test your understanding by closing the text and rebuilding the proof from scratch on unlined paper.
\end{trapbox}

The second hazard is \textbf{calculation drift}: falling back on calculation drills because they feel productive. When students struggle with their first proof course, their natural instinct is to do what worked in calculus. But calculating fifty more integrals, solving larger differential equations, or diagonalizing another $4 \times 4$ matrix trains a different skill. Calculation follows a fixed recipe toward a number or formula; writing a proof means tracking quantifiers, chasing elements between sets, and keeping straight what you have assumed versus what you still owe. No amount of arithmetic drill will teach you how to choose the right hypothesis to unpack.

\subsection{The ten-proof readiness portfolio}
\label{26:sec-portfolio}

Before moving to advanced textbooks, write out complete, self-contained proofs of these ten benchmark theorems on unlined paper. If you can write all ten with clear justifications and no logical gaps, you are well prepared for proof-based mathematics courses.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Parity of sums}: The sum of any two even integers is even.
\item \textbf{Parity of products}: The product of any two odd integers is odd.
\item \textbf{Transitivity of divisibility}: For all integers $a, b, c$, if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$.
\item \textbf{Square parity}: For any integer $n$, if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.
\item \textbf{Irrationality}: The number $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
\item \textbf{Distributivity of sets}: For any sets $A, B, C$,
\[
A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C).
\]
\item \textbf{Inverse images and intersections}: For any function $f: X \to Y$ and subsets $S$ and $T$ of $Y$,
\[
f^{-1}(S \cap T) = f^{-1}(S) \cap f^{-1}(T).
\]
\item \textbf{Injective composition}: If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are injective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective.
\item \textbf{Congruence is an equivalence relation}: For any fixed positive integer $n$, the relation $a \equiv b \pmod n$ on $\Z$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
\item \textbf{Sum of the first $n$ integers}: For every integer $n \ge 1$,
\[
1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
\]
\end{enumerate}

Here is the Given--Goal method applied to one benchmark, Theorem~8.

\begin{example}[Portfolio Proof: Injective Composition]
Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be injective functions. Prove that $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective.
\end{example}

\textbf{Setup}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Outer logical shape}: An implication. We assume the premise (both $f$ and $g$ are injective) and target the conclusion ($g \circ f$ is injective).
\item \emph{Unpacking the goal}: By definition, $g \circ f$ is injective means:
\[
\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \; \bigl((g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2\bigr).
\]
\item \emph{Populating the shelves}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Given shelf}: $f$ is injective; $g$ is injective; arbitrary elements $x_1, x_2 \in A$; assumption $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$.
\item \textbf{Goal shelf}: Show $x_1 = x_2$.
\end{itemize}
\item \emph{The bridge}: The assumption states $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$. Because $g$ is injective, matching outputs compel matching inputs, so $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Because $f$ is injective, matching outputs again compel matching inputs, so $x_1 = x_2$. The goal is reached. Figure~\ref{26:fig-injective-composition} shows this chain running backward from $C$ to $A$.
\end{itemize}

Now write out the polished proof in flowing prose:

\begin{proof}
Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be injective functions. To prove that $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective, let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ be arbitrary and assume that $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$. Since $g$ is injective, any two inputs producing identical outputs under $g$ must themselves be equal; the same holds for $f$. So
\begin{align*}
(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)
  &\implies g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) && \text{(definition of composition)} \\
  &\implies f(x_1) = f(x_2) && \text{($g$ is injective)} \\
  &\implies x_1 = x_2 && \text{($f$ is injective)}.
\end{align*}
Because $x_1$ and $x_2$ were arbitrary elements of $A$ satisfying $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$, we conclude that $g \circ f$ is injective.
\end{proof}

\begin{historybox}
Transition texts, books written to carry students from calculation courses to proof courses, appeared in the late 20th century in response to a documented problem. After mid-20th-century curriculum reforms influenced by Bourbaki (the French group of mathematicians from Chapter~12), upper-division university courses, the third- and fourth-year courses of a degree, placed heavy emphasis on set theory built from axioms and on abstract structures. Mathematics education researchers, including Annie and John Selden, documented that many undergraduates struggled to translate informal mathematical statements into symbolic logic or to write the first steps of a proof.

Traditionally, students were expected to absorb proof techniques implicitly through lectures and reading textbooks that presented only final deductions, omitting exploratory scratch work and search strategies.

In the 1980s and 1990s, authors such as Daniel Solow (\emph{How to Read and Do Proofs}, 1982) and Daniel J. Velleman (\emph{How to Prove It}, 1994) introduced explicit instruction in proof techniques, emphasizing goal decomposition, variable scoping, and logical structure.

This approach sparked debate. Some educators cautioned that explicit templates could lead to mechanical formulaic writing. Proponents argued that making mathematical grammar explicit clarified the underlying expectations for all students.

Making proof structures explicit led to the widespread adoption of ``transition to higher mathematics'' courses at universities. Today, this focus extends to computer proof assistants such as Lean, Coq, and Isabelle, programs that check every step of a proof mechanically. Mathematicians and educators actively discuss whether formal proof assistants help students understand proof architecture or risk shifting focus toward software syntax.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{From structured templates to mathematical fluency}

Working up the four layers builds fluency in the right order: propositional logic, quantifier order, and basic set operations become second nature before you have to handle abstract groups or metric spaces (sets with a distance function, the setting of later analysis) at the same time. The ten-proof portfolio gives you a clear test: once you can write out all ten arguments cleanly from memory on blank paper, you have the working habits that introductory analysis and algebra courses expect on day one.

Explicit proof templates are training wheels, and they are meant to come off eventually. Textbook exercises hand you tidy hypotheses and familiar definitions, whereas harder problems often make you reformulate the question before any standard template fits. With enough practice, though, tracking scope and unpacking definitions stops feeling like a checklist and starts feeling like ordinary reading.

Next comes the \textbf{Second Pass} through the curriculum, which revisits the core areas in more depth. Chapter~27, \textbf{Second Pass: Logic as a Language You Can Speak}, starts with formal logic, this time used as a working language for writing mathematics.

\subsection{Exercises}
\label{26:sec-exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Quantifier order warm-up}: Consider the true statement: ``Every positive real number has a smaller positive real number.''
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Write this statement in symbolic logic using $\forall$ and $\exists$.
    \item Reverse the order of the two quantifiers. Translate the resulting symbolic sentence back into plain English.
    \item Is the reversed statement true or false? Explain the conceptual difference between the two statements.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Proof architecture selection}: For each of the following claims, identify the outermost logical connective and state whether a direct proof, a contrapositive proof, or a proof by contradiction is the most natural strategy:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item For every integer $n$, if $5n + 3$ is even, then $n$ is odd.
    \item There is no rational number $x$ such that $x^2 = 3$.
    \item For any sets $A, B, C$, if $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq C$, then $A \subseteq C$.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Element chasing on set differences}: Let $A$ and $B$ be arbitrary sets. Prove by element chasing that
  \[
  (A \setminus B) \cup (A \cap B) \subseteq A.
  \]
  Explicitly state where you introduce an arbitrary element, where you unpack the union into a logical disjunction ($\lor$), and how you branch into cases.

  \item \textbf{Surjective composition}: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be surjective functions. Use the Given--Goal architecture to prove that $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective. (Trace carefully: which function's surjectivity must you invoke first to construct an input element?)

  \item \textbf{Equivalence relation verification}: Define a relation $\sim$ on the set of integers $\Z$ by $a \sim b$ if and only if $a^2 \equiv b^2 \pmod 5$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Prove that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation (verify reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity).
    \item List all distinct equivalence classes of $\sim$ in $\Z$.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Diagnosing flawed induction}: Recall the ``All horses have the same color'' false proof analyzed in Chapter~25 (Exercise~4). State the exact induction step where the deduction bridge collapses, and explain why the two subsets $\{h_1, \dots, h_k\}$ and $\{h_2, \dots, h_{k+1}\}$ fail to overlap when $k=1$.

  \item \textbf{Portfolio capstone: Preimages of unions}: Let $f: X \to Y$ be a function and let $C, D \subseteq Y$. Prove by double containment that
  \[
  f^{-1}(C \cup D) = f^{-1}(C) \cup f^{-1}(D).
  \]
  Write out the proof in complete, publication-quality prose, clearly justifying every step where you transition between set membership and logical connectives.
\end{enumerate}
