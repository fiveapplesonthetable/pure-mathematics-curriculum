\subsection{The great transition crisis: why students hit the analysis wall}

In Chapter~25, we treated stalled proofs like programs with diagnosable bugs. But debugging is reactive: it repairs an argument that has already gone off the rails. When facing a blank page, you need a proactive plan for constructing proofs from the start.

In university mathematics curricula, the transition from lower-division calculation to upper-division proof is a well-known hurdle. Students who succeed in calculus and computational linear algebra often find courses such as real analysis and abstract algebra surprisingly demanding.

The difficulty stems from a fundamental shift in objectives: earlier courses emphasize executing procedural algorithms to compute numbers or formulas, whereas proof-based courses require formulating definitions, analyzing logical structures with nested quantifiers, and constructing rigorous deductive arguments.

In 1994, the American mathematician Daniel J. Velleman addressed this transition with the publication of \emph{How to Prove It: A Structured Approach}. Velleman recognized that proof construction is an architectural discipline. Just as structured programming in computer science replaced unstructured code with modular functions, clear variable lifetimes, and explicit loops, mathematical proof writing could be taught as a systematic craft of goal decomposition, quantifier scoping, and logical strategy selection.

\subsection{Building the four-tier conceptual pyramid}
\label{26:sec-pyramid}

Readers working through introductory proof texts often find the material straightforward during the initial chapters on truth tables, but encounter difficulty once nested quantifiers, families of sets, and relations are introduced. At that stage, proofs require coordinating multiple definitions and managing variable scope.

This readiness plan ensures that you approach proof-based textbooks with momentum, confidence, and clear comprehension. Readiness comes from building four layers of habits, each supporting the next, rather than memorizing isolated tricks (Figure~\ref{26:fig-pyramid}).

Notice how the pyramid functions in practice:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Tier 1 (Foundations of Logic)}: Basic propositional logic. You master logical connectives ($\land$ for ``and'', $\lor$ for ``or'', $\neg$ for ``not'', $\implies$ for ``implies''), truth conditions, and the contrapositive (proving ``if $P$ then $Q$'' by establishing the equivalent statement ``if not $Q$ then not $P$'').
\item \textbf{Tier 2 (Quantifiers \& Scoping)}: You control variable lifespans. You distinguish between universal statements ($\forall$, which demand introducing an arbitrary element) and existential statements ($\exists$, which demand producing a concrete witness).
\item \textbf{Tier 3 (Set Machinery \& Functions)}: You use sets as your working domain. You prove set equality via double containment (showing both $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq A$), track function images and preimages, and test for injectivity and surjectivity.
\item \textbf{Tier 4 (Structural Thinking)}: Abstract mathematical structures. You handle equivalence relations, build quotient sets, and carry out mathematical induction.
\end{itemize}

\begin{walkbox}
Suppose you want to prove that the linear function $f: \R \to \R$ given by $f(x) = 3x + 1$ is injective. You pick up your pencil and write: ``Let $x_1, x_2 \in \R$ be arbitrary, and assume $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$.'' Pause and consider: which tier of the conceptual pyramid dictated that exact opening sentence? Notice that Tier~3 defined what injectivity means, Tier~2 supplied the universal quantifier rule instructing you to pick arbitrary elements, and Tier~1 dictated the conditional rule instructing you to assume the premise!
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{The four core readiness milestones}
\label{26:sec-milestones}

Before tackling Velleman's exercises, you should be able to execute four foundational tasks without hesitation:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Translate fluently between English and symbolic logic}: You can take an informal mathematical sentence (such as ``every nonzero integer divides zero'') and write down its exact predicate formula ($\forall n \in \Z \setminus \{0\}, \; \exists k \in \Z, \; 0 = k \cdot n$)---a logical sentence with variables that becomes true or false once values are specified.
\item \textbf{Negate nested quantifiers cleanly}: When faced with a multi-round quantifier statement such as $\forall \eps > 0 \; \exists \delta > 0 \; \dots$, you flip every quantifier ($\forall \leftrightarrow \exists$) systematically and negate the inner predicate without accidentally turning an implication into a false premise.
\item \textbf{Chase elements across sets}: To prove a set inclusion like $A \cap (B \cup C) \subseteq (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$, you pick an arbitrary element $x$ in the left set, expand the definitions of intersection ($\land$) and union ($\lor$), and trace where $x$ lands using logic rules.
\item \textbf{Distinguish hypothesis from conclusion}: You never assume what you are trying to prove, and you recognize whether an argument calls for direct deduction, contrapositive, or contradiction.
\end{enumerate}

To turn these milestones into an operational routine, Velleman introduced the \emph{Given--Goal} decomposition. When you sit before a blank page, you do not write randomly from left to right. Instead, you analyze the outermost logical structure of the claim to set up two shelves: what you know (the Given shelf) and what you must establish (the Goal shelf), as visualized in Figure~\ref{26:fig-architecture}.

\subsection{Two common study traps: passive reading and calculation drift}
\label{26:sec-traps}

Before beginning daily practice, be mindful of two common study traps that slow progress.

The first hazard is the \textbf{passive reading trap}. When reading a published proof in a textbook, each step follows logically from the preceding one. Checking an existing argument is largely a process of verifying deductions. But writing a proof on a blank page requires a different skill: you must work backward from the goal, set up arbitrary variables, decide whether to proceed directly or by contrapositive or contradiction, and discard unfruitful approaches. Reading alone does not develop the habit of structuring arguments independently.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The illusion of the solved example}: Reading through a completed proof line by line triggers the recognition heuristic: every step makes logical sense, so your brain reports that you understand the material. But recognition is not generation. When you close the book and face a blank sheet of paper, that support vanishes. Always test your comprehension by closing the text and reconstructing the proof from scratch on unlined paper.
\end{trapbox}

The second hazard is the \textbf{calculation fallacy}. When students struggle with their first proof course, their natural instinct is to do what worked in calculus: work through more calculation drills. But calculating fifty more integrals, solving larger differential equations, or diagonalizing another $4 \times 4$ matrix trains a completely different skill. Calculation follows a fixed recipe toward a number or formula; writing a proof means tracking quantifiers, chasing elements between sets, and keeping straight what you have assumed versus what you still owe. No amount of arithmetic drill will teach you how to choose the right hypothesis to unpack.

\subsection{The ten-proof readiness portfolio}
\label{26:sec-portfolio}

Before moving to advanced textbooks, write out complete, self-contained proofs of these ten benchmark theorems on unlined paper. If you can write all ten with clear justifications and no logical gaps, you are well prepared for proof-based mathematics courses.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Parity of sums}: The sum of any two even integers is even.
\item \textbf{Parity of products}: The product of any two odd integers is odd.
\item \textbf{Transitivity of divisibility}: For all integers $a, b, c$, if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$.
\item \textbf{Square parity}: For any integer $n$, if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.
\item \textbf{Irrationality}: The number $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
\item \textbf{Distributivity of sets}: For any sets $A, B, C$, $A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$.
\item \textbf{Inverse images and intersections}: For any function $f: X \to Y$ and subsets $S, T \subseteq Y$, $f^{-1}(S \cap T) = f^{-1}(S) \cap f^{-1}(T)$.
\item \textbf{Injective composition}: If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are injective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective.
\item \textbf{Congruence is an equivalence relation}: For any fixed positive integer $n$, the relation $a \equiv b \pmod n$ on $\Z$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
\item \textbf{Sum of the first $n$ integers}: For every integer $n \ge 1$,
\[
1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
\]
\end{enumerate}

To see how the Given--Goal architecture works on a benchmark theorem, let us walk through Theorem~8.

\begin{example}[Exemplar Portfolio Proof: Injective Composition]
Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be injective functions. Prove that $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective.
\end{example}

\textbf{Architectural Setup}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Outer logical shape}: An implication. We assume the premise (both $f$ and $g$ are injective) and target the conclusion ($g \circ f$ is injective).
\item \emph{Unpacking the goal}: By definition, $g \circ f$ is injective means:
\[
\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \; (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2.
\]
\item \emph{Populating the shelves}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Given shelf}: $f$ is injective; $g$ is injective; arbitrary elements $x_1, x_2 \in A$; assumption $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$.
\item \textbf{Goal shelf}: Show $x_1 = x_2$.
\end{itemize}
\item \emph{The bridge}: The assumption states $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$. Because $g$ is injective, matching outputs compel matching inputs, so $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Because $f$ is injective, matching outputs again compel matching inputs, so $x_1 = x_2$. The goal is reached!
\end{itemize}

Now write out the polished proof in flowing prose:

\begin{proof}
Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be injective functions. To prove that $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective, let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ be arbitrary and assume that
\[
(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2).
\]
By the definition of function composition, this equality means
\[
g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)).
\]
Since $g$ is injective, any two inputs producing identical outputs under $g$ must themselves be equal. Therefore,
\[
f(x_1) = f(x_2).
\]
Similarly, since $f$ is injective, $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$ implies that
\[
x_1 = x_2.
\]
Because $x_1$ and $x_2$ were arbitrary elements of $A$ satisfying $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$, we conclude that $g \circ f$ is injective.
\end{proof}

\begin{walkbox}
Consider Theorem~4 from the portfolio: ``For any integer $n$, if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.'' If you attempt a direct proof, you assume $n^2 = 2k$ and write $n = \sqrt{2k}$, stalling because the square root does not preserve integer factorization. Why does switching to the contrapositive (``if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd'') turn an intractable square root into a simple forward algebraic expansion?
\end{walkbox}

\begin{historybox}
The emergence of structured transition texts in the late 20th century addressed a documented challenge in undergraduate mathematics education. Following curriculum reforms in the mid-20th century influenced by the Bourbaki group, upper-division university courses placed heavy emphasis on axiomatic set theory and abstract structures. Studies by mathematics education researchers, including Annie and John Selden, documented that many undergraduate mathematics students struggled to translate informal mathematical statements into symbolic logic or to formulate the initial steps of a proof.

Traditionally, students were expected to absorb proof techniques implicitly through lectures and reading textbooks that presented only final deductions, omitting exploratory scratch work and search strategies.

In the 1980s and 1990s, authors such as Daniel Solow (\emph{How to Read and Do Proofs}, 1982) and Daniel J. Velleman (\emph{How to Prove It}, 1994) introduced explicit instruction in proof techniques, emphasizing goal decomposition, variable scoping, and logical structure.

This approach sparked debate. Some educators cautioned that explicit templates could lead to mechanical formulaic writing. Proponents argued that making mathematical grammar explicit clarified the underlying expectations for all students.

Making proof structures explicit led to the widespread adoption of ``transition to higher mathematics'' courses at universities. Today, this focus extends to interactive computer proof assistants such as Lean, Coq, and Isabelle---software systems that verify mathematical proofs with complete syntactic rigor. Mathematicians and educators actively discuss whether formal proof assistants help students understand proof architecture or risk shifting focus toward software syntax.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{From structured templates to mathematical fluency}

Working up the four-tier pyramid builds fluency in the right order, making propositional logic, quantifier order, and basic set operations second nature before you have to juggle abstract groups or metric spaces at the same time. The ten-proof portfolio gives you a clear, honest test: once you can write out all ten arguments cleanly from memory on blank paper, you have the working habits that introductory analysis and algebra courses expect on day one.

Explicit proof templates are training wheels, and they are meant to come off eventually. Textbook exercises hand you tidy hypotheses and familiar definitions, whereas harder problems often make you reformulate the question before any standard template fits. With enough practice, though, tracking scope and unpacking definitions stops feeling like a checklist and starts feeling like ordinary reading.

Having established this foundation, we begin our \textbf{Second Pass} through the curriculum, revisiting core areas with greater mathematical depth. Chapter~27 begins this sequence with \textbf{Second Pass: Logic as a Language You Can Speak}, examining formal logic as an expressive working language.

\subsection{Exercises}
\label{26:sec-exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Quantifier order warm-up}: Consider the true statement: ``Every positive real number has a smaller positive real number.''
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Write this statement in symbolic logic using $\forall$ and $\exists$.
    \item Reverse the order of the two quantifiers. Translate the resulting symbolic sentence back into plain English.
    \item Is the reversed statement true or false? Explain the conceptual difference between the two statements.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Proof architecture selection}: For each of the following claims, identify the outermost logical connective and state whether a direct proof, a contrapositive proof, or a proof by contradiction is the most natural strategy:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item For every integer $n$, if $5n + 3$ is even, then $n$ is odd.
    \item There is no rational number $x$ such that $x^2 = 3$.
    \item For any sets $A, B, C$, if $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq C$, then $A \subseteq C$.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Element chasing on set differences}: Let $A$ and $B$ be arbitrary sets. Prove by element chasing that
  \[
  (A \setminus B) \cup (A \cap B) \subseteq A.
  \]
  Explicitly state where you introduce an arbitrary element, where you unpack the union into a logical disjunction ($\lor$), and how you branch into cases.

  \item \textbf{Surjective composition}: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be surjective functions. Use the Given--Goal architecture to prove that $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective. (Trace carefully: which function's surjectivity must you invoke first to construct an input element?)

  \item \textbf{Equivalence relation verification}: Define a relation $\sim$ on the set of integers $\Z$ by $a \sim b$ if and only if $a^2 \equiv b^2 \pmod 5$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Prove that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation (verify reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity).
    \item List all distinct equivalence classes of $\sim$ in $\Z$.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Diagnosing flawed induction}: Recall the ``All horses have the same color'' false proof analyzed in Chapter~25 (Exercise~4). State the exact induction step where the deduction bridge collapses, and explain why the two subsets $\{h_1, \dots, h_k\}$ and $\{h_2, \dots, h_{k+1}\}$ fail to overlap when $k=1$.

  \item \textbf{Portfolio capstone: Preimages of unions}: Let $f: X \to Y$ be a function and let $C, D \subseteq Y$. Prove by double containment that
  \[
  f^{-1}(C \cup D) = f^{-1}(C) \cup f^{-1}(D).
  \]
  Write out the proof in complete, publication-quality prose, clearly justifying every step where you transition between set membership and logical connectives.
\end{enumerate}
