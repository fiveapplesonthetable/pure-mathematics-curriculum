\subsection{The bridge: from Descartes and Bézout to algebraic geometry}

In Chapter~23, differential geometry extended calculus to curved spaces by approximating each small patch with a flat tangent plane. This works well as long as the surface remains smooth.

When a curve crosses itself in a loop or pinches into a sharp point, this approach fails. At a crossing point there is no single tangent line, and at a sharp tip the derivative is undefined.

Yet the equation of a pinched curve can be simple. The curve $y^2 = x^3$ has a sharp point at the origin, but the equation itself is an ordinary polynomial with nothing special happening at $(0,0)$. Instead of using calculus to approximate curves by lines, algebraic geometry studies shapes directly through the polynomial equations that define them.

The bridge between equations and geometric shapes was built in 1637, when Ren\'e Descartes published \emph{La G\'eom\'etrie} as an appendix to his philosophical \emph{Discourse on Method}. By introducing coordinate axes to label points by pairs of numbers $(x, y)$, Descartes showed that every polynomial equation in $x$ and $y$ cuts out a curve, and that many classical curves (lines, circles, parabolas) can be described by such equations. An \textbf{algebraic curve} is the set of all points in the plane satisfying a polynomial equation $f(x, y) = 0$. The \textbf{degree} of the curve is the largest total power appearing in any single term of $f$, where the total power of a term such as $x^2y$ is $2 + 1 = 3$. A straight line $ax + by + c = 0$ has degree 1, while a circle $x^2 + y^2 - r^2 = 0$ has degree 2.

A century later, in 1779, the French mathematician \'{E}tienne B\'ezout asked how many points two algebraic curves of degrees $d_1$ and $d_2$ in the plane have in common. (We assume the two curves share no whole piece of curve; a line and itself, for example, would meet in infinitely many points.) B\'ezout's answer was that the number of intersection points should equal the product of the degrees:
\[
\text{Number of intersections} = d_1 \cdot d_2.
\]
A line ($d_1 = 1$) and a circle ($d_2 = 2$) should intersect in $1 \cdot 2 = 2$ points. Two ellipses ($d_1 = 2, d_2 = 2$) should intersect in $2 \cdot 2 = 4$ points.

Yet when tested in the \textbf{real affine plane} $\R^2$ (the ordinary plane of pairs of real numbers; the word ``affine'' means that no extra points at infinity have been added), exceptions appear immediately:
\begin{itemize}
\item Two parallel lines have $d_1 = 1$ and $d_2 = 1$, yet they do not meet: $0 \neq 1 \cdot 1$.
\item A circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ and the line $y = 2$ have no real intersection points: $0 \neq 2$.
\item A line tangent to a circle touches at only a single point: $1 \neq 2$.
\end{itemize}
Was B\'ezout's rule wrong? No: the rule holds once we count in the right place and in the right way. Three adjustments account for the missing points:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Complex solutions}: For the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ and the horizontal line $y = 2$, substituting $y = 2$ into the circle equation gives $x^2 + 4 = 1$, so $x^2 = -3$. In the real numbers, there are no solutions. But in the complex numbers $\C$, we obtain $x = \pm i\sqrt{3}$. The two intersection points $(i\sqrt{3}, 2)$ and $(-i\sqrt{3}, 2)$ lie in the complex affine plane $\C^2$, the set of all pairs of complex numbers.
\item \textbf{Multiplicity}: For the unit circle and the line $y = 1$, substituting gives $x^2 + 1 = 1$, so $x^2 = 0$. The equation $x \cdot x = 0$ has the root $x = 0$ twice over; we say this root has \textbf{multiplicity} 2. The circle and line are tangent at $(0, 1)$, and that single contact point counts as two intersection points that have merged.
\item \textbf{Points at infinity}: In flat Euclidean geometry, parallel lines never meet. Add one new point for each direction in the plane, and declare that all lines with that direction pass through it. The new points together form the \textbf{line at infinity}, and the enlarged plane is the \textbf{projective plane} $\mathbb{P}^2$ (developed by Jean-Victor Poncelet in 1822). In the projective plane, two parallel lines meet at their shared point at infinity, giving $1 \cdot 1 = 1$. Figure~\ref{24:fig-parallel-infinity} shows the idea through a perspective drawing, where parallel railway tracks seem to meet on the horizon.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Equations as geometric shapes: smooth curves, nodes, and cusps}

In differential geometry, every point on a smooth manifold must have a neighborhood that looks like flat Euclidean space, with a single well-defined tangent space. Polynomial equations, however, easily produce shapes with points where smoothness breaks down. Figure~\ref{24:fig-smooth-node-cusp} shows the three basic cases (a \emph{cubic} is a curve of degree 3):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Smooth curves}: every point has a unique tangent line, as on the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$.
\item \textbf{Nodes (crossing points)}: the curve crosses itself, with two or more distinct tangent lines at a single point, like the nodal cubic $y^2 = x^3 + x^2$.
\item \textbf{Cusps (sharp pinches)}: two branches meet at a point and share a single tangent line, like the cuspidal cubic $y^2 = x^3$.
\end{itemize}

Why does calculus fail at the node and the cusp?
Consider a curve given by an equation $f(x, y) = 0$ rather than by a formula $y = g(x)$ (such a curve is called \textbf{implicit}). Its partial derivatives $\partial f / \partial x$ (differentiate in $x$, holding $y$ constant) and $\partial f / \partial y$ form the \textbf{gradient} vector (both terms from Chapter~23)
\[
\nabla f(a, b) = \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b), \ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b) \right),
\]
and in multivariable calculus the tangent line to the curve at a point $(a, b)$ is
\[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b)(x - a) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b)(y - b) = 0.
\]
The \textbf{Implicit Function Theorem} of calculus guarantees that if the gradient at $(a, b)$ is not the zero vector, then near $(a, b)$ the curve is the graph of a smooth function, and this formula gives its unique tangent line. We say a function \textbf{vanishes} at a point when its value there is $0$. A point $(a, b)$ \emph{on the curve} $f(x, y) = 0$ is a \textbf{singularity} (or \textbf{singular point}) when both partial derivatives vanish there:
\[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b) = 0.
\]
When both derivatives vanish, the gradient is $(0, 0)$. The tangent-line formula collapses into $0 = 0$, which describes no line at all.

To see what the curve does near a singularity at the origin, we keep only the terms of lowest total degree in the polynomial. Near $(0,0)$ the numbers $x$ and $y$ are small, so higher powers such as $x^3$ are much smaller than $x^2$ or $y^2$ and can be ignored to first approximation:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The nodal cubic}: For the curve $f(x, y) = 0$ with $f(x, y) = y^2 - x^3 - x^2$, the partial derivatives are
\[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = -3x^2 - 2x, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y.
\]
At $(0, 0)$, both are zero. The lowest-degree terms are the quadratic terms, and setting them to zero gives
\[
y^2 - x^2 = (y - x)(y + x) = 0.
\]
This gives two distinct tangent lines ($y = x$ and $y = -x$) crossing at the origin.
\item \textbf{The cuspidal cubic}: For the curve $f(x, y) = 0$ with $f(x, y) = y^2 - x^3$, the derivatives are
\[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = -3x^2, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y.
\]
At $(0, 0)$, both vanish. The lowest-degree term is $y^2 = 0$, giving the single line $y = 0$ counted twice. Both branches $y = x^{3/2}$ and $y = -x^{3/2}$ leave the origin along this line, so they share one tangent and form a sharp point.
\end{enumerate}
So even where the gradient formula gives nothing, the lowest-degree terms of the polynomial describe the shape of the curve near the singularity.

\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Affine varieties and polynomial rings}

To build a dictionary between shapes and equations, we fix a \textbf{field} $k$ of numbers---a number system where addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by non-zero elements are valid, such as the rational numbers $\Q$, real numbers $\R$, or complex numbers $\C$.

We then consider the \textbf{polynomial ring} $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, the collection of all polynomials in $n$ variables $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with coefficients in $k$. A \textbf{ring} is a number system in which you can add, subtract, and multiply, but not necessarily divide: dividing one polynomial by another usually does not give a polynomial. This ring is \textbf{commutative}, meaning multiplication does not depend on order ($f \cdot g = g \cdot f$). The space $k^n$ of all $n$-tuples of elements of $k$ is called \textbf{affine space}, in the same sense of ``affine'' as before: ordinary coordinate space, without points at infinity.

\begin{definition}
Let $S \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ be a collection of polynomials. The \textbf{affine variety} (or \textbf{zero locus}) defined by $S$ is the set of all points in $k^n$ where every polynomial in $S$ vanishes:
\[
V(S) = \{a \in k^n : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in S\}.
\]
When $S = \{f_1, \dots, f_m\}$ is a finite list, we write $V(f_1, \dots, f_m)$.
\end{definition}

For example, in three-dimensional space $\R^3$:
\begin{itemize}
\item The zero locus $V(z)$ is the flat $xy$-plane ($z = 0$).
\item The zero locus $V(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 1)$ is the 2-dimensional unit sphere.
\item The zero locus $V(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 1, \, z)$ is the set of points satisfying both equations: the unit circle lying flat in the $xy$-plane.
\item The zero locus $V(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 1, \, z, \, y)$ adds the condition $y = 0$, leaving only the two points $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(-1, 0, 0)$.
\end{itemize}
Intersecting shapes corresponds to taking the union of their defining equations:
\[
V(S_1 \cup S_2) = V(S_1) \cap V(S_2).
\]
Each additional equation imposes a new constraint and cuts the shape down further. Figure~\ref{24:fig-adding-equations} follows the examples above, with $f_1 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 1$, $f_2 = z$, and $f_3 = y$.

\subsection{Ideals: the algebraic structure of vanishing sets}

Why do algebraic geometers work with ideals rather than arbitrary lists of polynomial equations?

First, different equations can define the same shape. For instance, in one dimension, the equations $x = 0$, $x^2 = 0$, and $x^3 = 0$ all define the same single point: the origin $x = 0$.

Second, the polynomials that vanish on a shape are \emph{closed} under two operations: combining them in these ways never produces a polynomial outside the collection. If a polynomial $f$ vanishes at every point of a set $X$, and another polynomial $g$ also vanishes on $X$, then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Their sum $f + g$ vanishes on $X$: for every $a \in X$,
\[
(f + g)(a) = f(a) + g(a) = 0 + 0 = 0.
\]
\item For any polynomial $h \in k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, the product $h \cdot f$ vanishes on $X$: for every $a \in X$,
\[
(h \cdot f)(a) = h(a) \cdot f(a) = h(a) \cdot 0 = 0.
\]
\end{enumerate}
So the set of all polynomials that vanish on $X$ is closed under addition, and it \emph{absorbs} multiplication: multiplying one of its members by any polynomial at all gives another member. A set with these two properties is called an \textbf{ideal}.

\begin{definition}
An \textbf{ideal} $I$ in a commutative ring $R$ is a subset $I \subseteq R$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $0 \in I$, and if $a, b \in I$, then $a+b \in I$ and $-a \in I$ (it is an additive subgroup).
\item If $r \in R$ and $a \in I$, then $r \cdot a \in I$ (it absorbs multiplication from $R$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Just as zero absorbs multiplication in arithmetic ($0 \cdot r = 0$), an ideal absorbs multiplication from the whole ring. Given finitely many polynomials $f_1, \dots, f_m \in R$, the \textbf{ideal generated by} $f_1, \dots, f_m$ is the set of all their combinations with polynomial coefficients:
\[
(f_1, \dots, f_m) = \{h_1 f_1 + \dots + h_m f_m : h_1, \dots, h_m \in R\}.
\]
In particular, $(0) = \{0\}$ is the \textbf{zero ideal}, and $(f) = \{h \cdot f : h \in R\}$, the set of all multiples of a single polynomial $f$, is called a \textbf{principal ideal}.

This establishes a two-way dictionary:
\begin{itemize}
\item To any ideal of polynomials $I \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, we associate its geometric shape:
\[
V(I) = \{a \in k^n : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in I\}.
\]
\item To any geometric shape $X \subseteq k^n$, we associate the ideal of all polynomials that vanish on it:
\[
I(X) = \{f \in k[x_1, \dots, x_n] : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } a \in X\}.
\]
\end{itemize}

Under this dictionary, inclusion reverses direction:
\[
X_1 \subseteq X_2 \implies I(X_2) \subseteq I(X_1).
\]
Why does inclusion reverse? To vanish on a larger set $X_2$, a polynomial must satisfy more constraints. A larger set of points therefore has a smaller ideal of vanishing polynomials, as Figure~\ref{24:fig-inclusion-reversal} illustrates.

What happens if you travel from algebra to geometry and back? If you start with the ideal $J = (x^2) \subset \C[x]$, its zero locus is $V(J) = \{0\}$. But taking the vanishing ideal of that point gives $I(\{0\}) = (x)$, because $x$ vanishes at $0$. The power is lost: geometric points detect whether a polynomial evaluates to zero, but cannot distinguish $x = 0$ from $x^2 = 0$. Figure~\ref{24:fig-round-trip} traces this round trip.

To capture this loss of powers algebraically, we define the \textbf{radical} of an ideal $J$, the set of all polynomials some power of which lies in $J$:
\[
\sqrt{J} = \{f \in R : f^m \in J \text{ for some integer } m \ge 1\}.
\]
For example, $\sqrt{(x^2)} = (x)$, because $x \notin (x^2)$ but $x^2 \in (x^2)$. An ideal is called \textbf{radical} if $\sqrt{J} = J$.

This relationship is made precise by David Hilbert's \textbf{Nullstellensatz}:
\begin{theorem}[Hilbert's Nullstellensatz, 1893]
If $k$ is an algebraically closed field (a field in which every non-constant polynomial has a root, such as the complex numbers $\C$), and $J \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is an ideal, then:
\[
I(V(J)) = \sqrt{J}.
\]
\end{theorem}
The Nullstellensatz (German for ``zero-places theorem'') says that the shape $V(J)$ determines $\sqrt{J}$, though not $J$ itself: $(x)$ and $(x^2)$ give the same shape. When restricted to radical ideals, the correspondence between ideals and algebraic varieties is a bijection: each radical ideal matches exactly one variety, and each variety exactly one radical ideal.

\begin{historybox}
Modern algebraic geometry grew out of two turning points: Hilbert's non-constructive revolution in algebra, and a crisis over visual intuition in geometry.

In the late nineteenth century, algebraists sought to classify \textbf{polynomial invariants}---polynomial expressions that remain unchanged when coordinate axes are rotated or transformed (such as the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ of the quadratic $ax^2 + bx + c$). Mathematicians spent decades performing hand calculations trying to prove that every system of invariants could be generated by a finite list of building blocks. In 1890, David Hilbert proved that \emph{every} ideal in a polynomial ring has a finite generating set (the \textbf{Hilbert Basis Theorem}). Because Hilbert's proof was non-constructive---using proof by contradiction rather than computing the generators explicitly---the leading invariant theorist of the day, Paul Gordan, exclaimed: ``This is not mathematics, it is theology!'' Yet Hilbert's theorem showed that every algebraic variety is cut out by a finite number of polynomial equations.

Shortly thereafter, a deeper difficulty arose in the Italian school of algebraic geometry. In the early twentieth century, mathematicians including Guido Castelnuovo, Federigo Enriques, and Francesco Severi made progress classifying algebraic surfaces using \textbf{synthetic geometry}: reasoning directly from geometric drawings and intuition without coordinate equations. By the 1930s, this approach ran into errors when applied to singular points, overlapping components, and points of tangency. Several published theorems about intersection counts on surfaces turned out to be false.

Algebraic geometry was then rebuilt on the theory of commutative rings. In the 1920s, Emmy Noether developed the abstract theory of rings and ideals, presented in B.L. van der Waerden's 1930 textbook \emph{Moderne Algebra}. In the 1940s, Oscar Zariski and Andr\'e Weil developed rigorous foundations using commutative algebra and the theory of fields.

In the late 1950s and 1960s, Alexander Grothendieck generalized varieties by introducing \textbf{schemes}. Grothendieck associated to every commutative ring $R$ a geometric space: its \textbf{prime spectrum} $\operatorname{Spec}(R)$, whose points are the \textbf{prime ideals} of the ring (ideals $P$ such that whenever $fg \in P$, then $f \in P$ or $g \in P$; an example is the ideal $(x - a)$ in $\C[x]$). Schemes incorporate \textbf{nilpotent elements}---algebraic quantities like $\eps \neq 0$ whose square is zero ($\eps^2 = 0$)---giving them a geometric interpretation as infinitesimal tangent vectors or thickened points. By allowing number rings like the integers $\Z$ to be treated on the same footing as polynomial rings, Grothendieck created \textbf{arithmetic geometry}, the framework in which Andrew Wiles proved Fermat's Last Theorem in 1994.

Today, algebraic geometry connects with topology, number theory, and mathematical physics. The \textbf{Hodge Conjecture}, one of the Clay Millennium Prize Problems, asks whether certain features that topology detects inside a shape cut out by polynomials with complex coordinates always come from smaller shapes that are also cut out by polynomials. Another open problem is \textbf{resolution of singularities in positive characteristic}. A field has \emph{positive characteristic} if adding $1$ to itself some number of times gives $0$, as in clock arithmetic modulo a prime $p$, and \emph{characteristic zero} otherwise (as in $\Q$, $\R$, $\C$). In 1964 Heisuke Hironaka proved that in characteristic zero every singular variety can be replaced by a smooth one that differs from it only near the singular points; in positive characteristic this is still unknown.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The dictionary between equations and shapes}

Translating back and forth between ideals and varieties pays off across several parts of mathematics:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Exact intersection theory}: B\'ezout's theorem becomes an exact algebraic identity. Curves of degrees $d_1$ and $d_2$ in the complex projective plane (the projective plane with complex coordinates) that share no common component intersect in exactly $d_1 \cdot d_2$ points, counted with multiplicity.
\item \textbf{Elliptic curves and cryptography}: A cubic curve $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$ with $4a^3 + 27b^2 \neq 0$ is called an \textbf{elliptic curve}; the condition on $a$ and $b$ guarantees that the curve has no singular points. It has degree 3, so by B\'ezout's theorem any straight line (degree 1) meets it in $1 \cdot 3 = 3$ points of the projective plane, counted with multiplicity. If you draw a line through two points $P$ and $Q$ on the curve, it hits a third point $R$ (possibly $P$ or $Q$ again, if the line is tangent there, or the single point at infinity in the vertical direction, if the line is vertical). Reflecting $R$ across the $x$-axis gives a point defined to be the sum $P + Q$, as Figure~\ref{24:fig-chord-tangent} shows. This chord-and-tangent rule makes the points of an elliptic curve into an abelian group, which is the basis of Elliptic Curve Cryptography, used in digital signatures and secure communication protocols.
\item \textbf{Number theory as geometry}: Asking whether an equation has whole-number or fraction solutions is the same as asking whether a shape contains points with integer or rational coordinates. For example, dividing $x^n + y^n = z^n$ by $z^n$ gives
\[
\left( \frac{x}{z} \right)^{\!n} + \left( \frac{y}{z} \right)^{\!n} = 1,
\]
so with $X = x/z$ and $Y = y/z$, positive integer solutions become points with positive rational coordinates on the curve $X^n + Y^n = 1$. For $n = 2$ this curve is the unit circle, and the solution $3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$ becomes the point $(3/5, 4/5)$, as Figure~\ref{24:fig-rational-points} shows.
\end{itemize}

Algebraic geometry also has limits. It handles only shapes cut out by polynomials. It cannot easily handle \emph{transcendental} functions (functions such as $\sin x$ and $e^x$ that are not given by any polynomial equation), nor arbitrary smooth surfaces such as the boundary of a flowing fluid, which belong to differential geometry. In addition, the standard method for computing with systems of polynomial equations, \textbf{Gr\"obner bases}, is the polynomial version of Gaussian elimination (the row-reduction method for solving systems of linear equations). Its running time in the worst case can grow \emph{doubly exponentially}, roughly like $2^{2^n}$ where $n$ is the number of variables. Many systems are solved quickly in practice, but the worst cases grow so fast that some systems in only a handful of variables are out of reach of any computer.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{B\'ezout on a cubic (Mental check)}: A line $L$ has degree 1 and an elliptic curve $E: y^2 = x^3 - 4x$ has degree 3. According to B\'ezout's theorem, in how many points (counted with multiplicity) must $L$ and $E$ intersect in the complex projective plane? If a non-vertical line with real slope passes through two distinct real points on $E$, why must its third intersection point with $E$ also have real coordinates (hint: substitute $y = mx + c$ into $E$ and recall that the sum of the three roots of $x^3 - m^2 x^2 + \dots = 0$ equals $m^2$)? What serves as the third intersection point if the line is vertical?
\item \textbf{B\'ezout and multiplicity}: Consider the parabola $y = x^2$ (degree 2) and the line $y = mx - 1$ (degree 1).
\begin{enumerate}
\item Substitute $y = mx - 1$ into $y = x^2$ to obtain a quadratic equation in $x$.
\item For which values of $m$ is the line tangent to the parabola? Show that for these values, the quadratic has a single real root of multiplicity 2.
\item For $m = 1$, find the two intersection points in $\C^2$. Verify that they form a complex conjugate pair, giving $1 \cdot 2 = 2$ complex intersection points.
\end{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Singularity detection}: Let $f(x, y) = y^2 - x^2(x + 2) = 0$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute the partial derivatives $\partial f / \partial x$ and $\partial f / \partial y$.
\item Find all points in $\R^2$ where both partial derivatives vanish simultaneously, and verify which of them actually lies on the curve $f(x, y) = 0$ to identify the singular point $(a, b)$.
\item By examining the lowest-degree terms of $f(x, y)$ near this point, determine whether the singularity is a node (crossing point with two distinct tangents) or a cusp (single shared tangent).
\end{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Ideals and radicals}: Let $J = (x^2, xy) \subseteq \R[x, y]$ be the ideal generated by $x^2$ and $xy$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Find the zero locus $V(J) \subseteq \R^2$. (What line does this describe?)
\item Find the vanishing ideal $I(V(J))$.
\item Explain why $I(V(J)) \neq J$. Which polynomial vanishes on $V(J)$ but is missing from $J$? Verify that its square belongs to $J$, confirming that $I(V(J)) = \sqrt{J}$.
\end{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Inclusion reversal}: Let $X_1 \subseteq X_2 \subseteq k^n$ be two geometric shapes, and let $S_1 \subseteq S_2 \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ be two sets of polynomials.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Prove that $V(S_2) \subseteq V(S_1)$. Explain in one plain sentence why adding more equations shrinks the set of solutions.
\item Prove that $I(X_2) \subseteq I(X_1)$. Explain in one plain sentence why enlarging the shape shrinks the set of vanishing polynomials.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
