\subsection{The bridge: from Descartes and Bézout to geometric algebra}

In Chapter~23, differential geometry extended calculus to curved spaces by approximating each small patch with a flat tangent plane. This works well as long as the surface remains smooth.

When a curve crosses itself in a loop or pinches into a sharp point, derivatives break down. At a crossing point, there is no single tangent line; at a sharp tip, derivatives become undefined.

Yet the algebraic equation of a pinched curve is simple. The polynomial equation $y^2 = x^3$ has no trouble at the origin. Instead of using calculus to approximate curves with lines, algebraic geometry uses polynomials to study shapes directly.

The bridge between equations and geometric shapes was constructed in 1637, when Ren\'e Descartes published \emph{La G\'eom\'etrie} as an appendix to his philosophical \emph{Discourse on Method}. By introducing coordinate axes to label points by pairs of numbers $(x, y)$, Descartes demonstrated that every algebraic polynomial equation cuts out a geometric curve, and every geometric locus has an algebraic equation. An \textbf{algebraic curve} is simply the set of all points in the plane satisfying a polynomial equation $f(x, y) = 0$. The \textbf{degree} of the curve is the highest sum of powers in any single term: a straight line $ax + by + c = 0$ has degree 1, while a circle $x^2 + y^2 - r^2 = 0$ has degree 2.

A century later, in 1779, the French mathematician \'{E}tienne B\'ezout asked how many points of intersection two algebraic curves in the plane must have if their degrees are $d_1$ and $d_2$. B\'ezout formulated a general rule: the number of intersection points should equal the product of their degrees:
\[
\text{Number of intersections} = d_1 \cdot d_2.
\]
A line ($d_1 = 1$) and a circle ($d_2 = 2$) should intersect in $1 \cdot 2 = 2$ points. Two ellipses ($d_1 = 2, d_2 = 2$) should intersect in $2 \cdot 2 = 4$ points.

Yet when tested in the real affine plane $\R^2$, exceptions immediately appear:
\begin{itemize}
\item Two parallel lines have $d_1 = 1$ and $d_2 = 1$, yet they do not meet: $0 \neq 1 \cdot 1$.
\item A circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ and the line $y = 2$ have no real intersection points: $0 \neq 2$.
\item A line tangent to a circle touches at only a single point: $1 \neq 2$.
\end{itemize}
Was B\'ezout's rule incorrect? In the real affine plane, three natural geometric phenomena explain these apparent discrepancies:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Complex solutions}: For the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ and the horizontal line $y = 2$, substituting $y = 2$ into the circle equation gives $x^2 + 4 = 1$, so $x^2 = -3$. In the real numbers, there are no solutions. But in the complex numbers $\C$, we obtain $x = \pm i\sqrt{3}$. The two intersection points $(i\sqrt{3}, 2)$ and $(-i\sqrt{3}, 2)$ lie in the complex affine plane $\C^2$.
\item \textbf{Multiplicity}: For the unit circle and the line $y = 1$, substituting gives $x^2 + 1 = 1$, so $x^2 = 0$. The single contact point $(0, 1)$ is a repeated root with algebraic \textbf{multiplicity} 2. The circle and line are tangent, so the two intersection points coincide.
\item \textbf{Points at infinity}: In flat Euclidean geometry, parallel lines never meet. By adjoining points at infinity corresponding to directions of lines, we obtain the \textbf{projective plane} $\mathbb{P}^2$ (developed by Jean-Victor Poncelet in 1822). In projective geometry, parallel lines meet at a point on the line at infinity, giving $1 \cdot 1 = 1$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Equations as geometric shapes: smooth curves, nodes, and cusps}

In differential geometry, every point on a smooth manifold must have a neighborhood that looks like flat Euclidean space, with a single well-defined tangent space. Polynomial equations, however, naturally produce shapes with points where smoothness breaks down:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Smooth curves}: every point has a unique tangent line, as on the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$.
\item \textbf{Nodes (crossing points)}: the curve crosses itself, possessing two or more distinct tangent lines at a single point, like the nodal cubic $y^2 = x^3 + x^2$.
\item \textbf{Cusps (sharp pinches)}: two branches meet at a point and share a single tangent line, like the cuspidal cubic $y^2 = x^3$.
\end{itemize}

Why do singularities require an algebraic approach?
Consider an implicit curve $f(x, y) = 0$. In multivariable calculus, the tangent line to the curve at a point $(a, b)$ is determined by the gradient vector $\nabla f(a, b) = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a,b), \, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a,b)\right)$:
\[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b)(x - a) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b)(y - b) = 0.
\]
By the Implicit Function Theorem, this formula produces a unique tangent line as long as the gradient is non-zero. A point $(a, b)$ is a \textbf{singularity} (or \textbf{singular point}) when both partial derivatives vanish simultaneously:
\[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b) = 0.
\]
When both derivatives vanish, the gradient is $(0, 0)$. The linear tangent formula collapses into $0 = 0$, providing no single tangent line.

To determine what the curve does near a singularity, we examine the lowest-degree non-zero terms in the polynomial:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The nodal cubic}: For $f(x, y) = y^2 - x^3 - x^2 = 0$, the partial derivatives are $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = -3x^2 - 2x$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y$. At $(0, 0)$, both are zero. The lowest-degree terms are the quadratic terms: $y^2 - x^2 = 0$, which factors as $(y - x)(y + x) = 0$. This gives two distinct tangent lines ($y = x$ and $y = -x$) crossing at the origin.
\item \textbf{The cuspidal cubic}: For $f(x, y) = y^2 - x^3 = 0$, the derivatives are $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = -3x^2$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y$. At $(0, 0)$, both vanish. The lowest-degree term is $y^2 = 0$, giving a single repeated line ($y = 0$). The two branches meet horizontally with higher-order tangency, forming a sharp cusp.
\end{enumerate}
Even where gradient-based formulas do not apply, the lowest-degree terms in the polynomial describe the local geometry of the singularity.

\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Affine varieties and polynomial rings}

To build a universal dictionary between shapes and equations, we fix a \textbf{field} $k$ of numbers---a number system where addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by non-zero elements are valid, such as the rational numbers $\Q$, real numbers $\R$, or complex numbers $\C$.

We then consider the \textbf{polynomial ring} $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, the collection of all polynomials in $n$ variables with coefficients in $k$. In a ring, you can add, subtract, and multiply polynomials, and multiplication is commutative ($f \cdot g = g \cdot f$). The ambient space $k^n$ is called \textbf{affine space}---the word ``affine'' simply reminds us that we are working in ordinary coordinate space, without extra points at infinity.

\begin{definition}
Let $S \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ be a collection of polynomials. The \textbf{affine variety} (or \textbf{zero locus}) defined by $S$ is the set of all points in $k^n$ where every polynomial in $S$ vanishes simultaneously:
\[
V(S) = \{a \in k^n : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in S\}.
\]
When $S = \{f_1, \dots, f_m\}$ is a finite list, we write $V(f_1, \dots, f_m)$.
\end{definition}

For example, in three-dimensional space $\R^3$:
\begin{itemize}
\item The zero locus $V(z)$ is the flat $xy$-plane ($z = 0$).
\item The zero locus $V(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 1)$ is the 2-dimensional unit sphere.
\item The zero locus $V(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 1, \, z)$ is the simultaneous solution of both equations: the unit circle lying flat in the $xy$-plane.
\end{itemize}
Intersecting geometric shapes corresponds directly to taking the union of their defining equations:
\[
V(S_1 \cup S_2) = V(S_1) \cap V(S_2).
\]
Each additional equation imposes a new constraint, carving the geometric space down to a smaller shape.

\subsection{Ideals: the algebraic structure of vanishing sets}

Why do algebraic geometers work with ideals rather than arbitrary lists of polynomial equations?

First, different equations can define the same shape. For instance, in one dimension, the equations $x = 0$, $x^2 = 0$, and $x^3 = 0$ all define the same single point: the origin $x = 0$.

Second, polynomial equations generate closed algebraic systems. If a polynomial $f$ vanishes at every point of a set $X$, and another polynomial $g$ also vanishes on $X$, then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Their sum $(f+g)(a) = f(a) + g(a) = 0 + 0 = 0$ vanishes on all $a \in X$.
\item For any polynomial $h \in k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, the product $(h \cdot f)(a) = h(a) \cdot f(a) = h(a) \cdot 0 = 0$ vanishes on all $a \in X$.
\end{enumerate}
The set of all polynomials that vanish on $X$ is closed under addition and absorbs multiplication by any element of the ring. This structure defines an \textbf{ideal}.

\begin{definition}
An \textbf{ideal} $I$ in a commutative ring $R$ is a subset $I \subseteq R$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $0 \in I$, and if $a, b \in I$, then $a+b \in I$ and $-a \in I$ (it is an additive subgroup).
\item If $r \in R$ and $a \in I$, then $r \cdot a \in I$ (it absorbs multiplication from $R$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Just as zero absorbs multiplication in arithmetic ($0 \cdot r = 0$), an ideal absorbs multiplication from the whole ring.

This establishes a two-way dictionary:
\begin{itemize}
\item To any ideal of polynomials $I \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, we associate its geometric shape:
\[
V(I) = \{a \in k^n : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in I\}.
\]
\item To any geometric shape $X \subseteq k^n$, we associate the ideal of all polynomials that vanish on it:
\[
I(X) = \{f \in k[x_1, \dots, x_n] : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } a \in X\}.
\]
\end{itemize}

Notice how inclusion behaves under this dictionary: it reverses direction!
\[
X_1 \subseteq X_2 \implies I(X_2) \subseteq I(X_1).
\]
Why does inclusion reverse? To vanish on a larger set $X_2$, a polynomial must satisfy more constraints. A larger geometric subset therefore yields a smaller ideal of vanishing polynomials.

\end{center}

What happens if you travel from algebra to geometry and back? If you start with the ideal $J = (x^2) \subset \C[x]$, its zero locus is $V(J) = \{0\}$. But taking the vanishing ideal of that point gives $I(\{0\}) = (x)$, because $x$ vanishes at $0$. The power is lost: geometric points detect whether a polynomial evaluates to zero, but cannot distinguish $x = 0$ from $x^2 = 0$.

To capture this loss of powers algebraically, we define the \textbf{radical} of an ideal $J$:
\[
\sqrt{J} = \{f \in R : f^m \in J \text{ for some integer } m \ge 1\}.
\]
An ideal is called \textbf{radical} if $\sqrt{J} = J$.

This relationship is formalized by David Hilbert's \textbf{Nullstellensatz}:
\begin{theorem}[Hilbert's Nullstellensatz, 1893]
If $k$ is an algebraically closed field (such as the complex numbers $\C$), and $J \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is an ideal, then:
\[
I(V(J)) = \sqrt{J}.
\]
\end{theorem}
The Nullstellensatz (literally the ``zero-places theorem'') establishes that the geometric shape $V(J)$ determines the ideal $J$ up to taking radicals. When restricted to radical ideals, the correspondence between ideals and algebraic varieties is a bijection.

\begin{historybox}
Modern algebraic geometry grew out of two turning points: Hilbert's non-constructive revolution in algebra, and a crisis over visual intuition in geometry.

In the late nineteenth century, algebraists sought to classify \textbf{polynomial invariants}---polynomial expressions that remain unchanged when coordinate axes are rotated or transformed (such as the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ of a quadratic). Mathematicians spent decades performing hand calculations trying to prove that every system of invariants could be generated by a finite list of building blocks. In 1890, David Hilbert proved that \emph{every} ideal in a polynomial ring has a finite generating set (the \textbf{Hilbert Basis Theorem}). Because Hilbert's proof was non-constructive---using proof by contradiction rather than computing the generators explicitly---the leading invariant theorist of the day, Paul Gordan, exclaimed: ``This is not mathematics, it is theology!'' Yet Hilbert's theorem showed that every algebraic variety is cut out by a finite number of polynomial equations.

Shortly thereafter, a deeper difficulty arose in the Italian school of algebraic geometry. In the early twentieth century, mathematicians including Guido Castelnuovo, Federigo Enriques, and Francesco Severi made progress classifying algebraic surfaces using \textbf{synthetic geometry}: reasoning directly from geometric drawings and intuition without coordinate equations. By the 1930s, this approach ran into errors when applied to singular points, multiple overlapping components, and infinitesimal tangencies. Several published theorems about intersection counts on surfaces turned out to be false.

Algebraic geometry was subsequently rebuilt on the foundation of commutative ring theory. In the 1920s, Emmy Noether formulated the abstract theory of ideals and ascending chain conditions, presented in B.L. van der Waerden's 1930 textbook \emph{Moderne Algebra}. In the 1940s, Oscar Zariski and Andr\'e Weil developed rigorous foundations using commutative algebra and field theory.

In the late 1950s and 1960s, Alexander Grothendieck generalized varieties by introducing \textbf{schemes}. Grothendieck associated to every commutative ring $R$ a geometric space: its \textbf{prime spectrum} $\operatorname{Spec}(R)$, whose points are the prime ideals of the ring. Schemes incorporate \textbf{nilpotent elements}---algebraic quantities like $\eps \neq 0$ whose square is zero ($\eps^2 = 0$)---giving them a geometric interpretation as infinitesimal tangent vectors or thickened points. By allowing number rings like the integers $\Z$ to be treated on the same footing as polynomial rings, Grothendieck created \textbf{arithmetic geometry}, the framework in which Andrew Wiles proved Fermat's Last Theorem in 1994.

Today, algebraic geometry connects with topology, number theory, and mathematical physics. The \textbf{Hodge Conjecture}, one of the Clay Millennium Prize Problems, asks whether certain topological cohomology classes on complex projective varieties are algebraic. Meanwhile, the \textbf{resolution of singularities in positive characteristic} remains open: while Heisuke Hironaka proved in 1964 that singular varieties over fields of characteristic zero can be resolved to smooth varieties, whether an analogous resolution always exists in positive characteristic is still unresolved.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The dictionary between equations and shapes}

Translating back and forth between ideals and varieties pays off across several parts of mathematics:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Exact intersection theory}: B\'ezout's theorem becomes an exact algebraic identity. Curves of degrees $d_1$ and $d_2$ in the complex projective plane intersect in precisely $d_1 \cdot d_2$ points, counted with their exact algebraic multiplicities.
\item \textbf{Elliptic curves and cryptography}: A cubic curve $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$ has degree 3. By B\'ezout's theorem, any straight line (degree 1) intersects the cubic in $1 \cdot 3 = 3$ points. If you draw a line through two points $P$ and $Q$ on the curve, it must hit a third point $R$. Reflecting $R$ across the $x$-axis defines a group addition $P + Q$. This geometric chord-and-tangent rule turns elliptic curves into abelian groups, providing the basis for Elliptic Curve Cryptography used in digital signatures and secure communication protocols.
\item \textbf{Number theory as geometry}: Asking whether an equation has integer solutions (like $x^n + y^n = z^n$) is equivalent to asking whether an algebraic curve contains points with rational or integer coordinates.
\end{itemize}

Yet algebraic geometry has deliberate boundaries. It is confined strictly to shapes carved out by polynomials. It cannot easily handle transcendental functions like sines and exponentials, nor can it model the arbitrary smooth boundaries of physical fluid dynamics (where differential geometry reigns). In addition, computing with polynomial ideals algorithmically via \textbf{Gr\"obner bases} (the non-linear counterpart of Gaussian elimination) has doubly exponential worst-case computational complexity ($2^{2^n}$). A modest system of ten polynomial equations can quickly exhaust the memory of supercomputers.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{B\'ezout on a cubic (Mental check)}: A line $L$ has degree 1 and an elliptic curve $E: y^2 = x^3 - 4x$ has degree 3. According to B\'ezout's theorem, in how many points must $L$ and $E$ intersect in the complex projective plane? If a straight line passes through two known real points on $E$, why is it mathematically guaranteed that the line intersects $E$ at a third point?
\item \textbf{B\'ezout and multiplicity}: Consider the parabola $y = x^2$ (degree 2) and the line $y = mx - 1$ (degree 1).
\begin{enumerate}
\item Substitute $y = mx - 1$ into $y = x^2$ to obtain a quadratic equation in $x$.
\item For which values of $m$ is the line tangent to the parabola? Show that for these values, the quadratic has a single real root of multiplicity 2.
\item For $m = 1$, find the two intersection points in $\C^2$. Verify that they form a complex conjugate pair, giving $1 \cdot 2 = 2$ complex intersection points.
\end{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Singularity detection}: Let $f(x, y) = y^2 - x^2(x + 2) = 0$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute the partial derivatives $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$.
\item Find the point $(a, b)$ where both partial derivatives vanish simultaneously.
\item By examining the lowest-degree terms of $f(x, y)$ near this point, determine whether the singularity is a node (crossing point with two distinct tangents) or a cusp (single shared tangent).
\end{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Ideals and radicals}: Let $J = (x^2, xy) \subseteq \R[x, y]$ be the ideal generated by $x^2$ and $xy$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Find the zero locus $V(J) \subseteq \R^2$. (What line does this describe?)
\item Find the vanishing ideal $I(V(J))$.
\item Explain why $I(V(J)) \neq J$. Which polynomial vanishes on $V(J)$ but is missing from $J$? Verify that its square belongs to $J$, confirming that $I(V(J)) = \sqrt{J}$.
\end{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Inclusion reversal}: Let $X_1 \subseteq X_2 \subseteq k^n$ be two geometric shapes, and let $S_1 \subseteq S_2 \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ be two sets of polynomials.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Prove that $V(S_2) \subseteq V(S_1)$. Explain in one plain sentence why adding more equations shrinks the set of solutions.
\item Prove that $I(X_2) \subseteq I(X_1)$. Explain in one plain sentence why enlarging the shape shrinks the set of vanishing polynomials.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
