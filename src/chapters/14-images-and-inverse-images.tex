\section{Images and Inverse Images}
\chapterline

Up to this point, we have considered functions acting on individual points: given an input $x$, we evaluate the output $f(x)$. 

Functions also act on subsets of their domain and codomain. A function $f: X \to Y$ provides natural operations between subsets of $X$ and subsets of $Y$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Image (Pushforward):} Given a subset $S \subseteq X$, collect all outputs $f(s)$ in $Y$.
\item \textbf{Inverse Image (Preimage):} Given a subset $T \subseteq Y$, collect all points in $X$ whose outputs belong to $T$.
\end{itemize}

At first glance, one might expect pushing forward and pulling backward to be symmetric. But an important difference appears: \emph{pulling backward preserves more structure than pushing forward}. Preimages preserve unions, intersections, and complements. Forward images, by contrast, do not generally preserve intersections or complements whenever a function maps distinct inputs to the same output.

\begin{historybox}
This algebraic asymmetry was studied in the early twentieth century by Felix Hausdorff (\emph{Grundz\"uge der Mengenlehre}, 1914) alongside Maurice Fr\'echet, Kazimierz Kuratowski, and Henri Lebesgue. 

Mathematicians were working to generalize calculus beyond Euclidean $\R^n$. In abstract spaces without coordinates or distances, how can one define whether a function is continuous or whether a set is measurable?

An initial instinct was to push sets forward: if $U$ is open, should $f(U)$ be open? That fails even for simple functions. Hausdorff realized that the structure flows backward: the inverse image operation $f^{-1}$ preserves unions, intersections, and complements without exception. It forms a Boolean algebra homomorphism, making the preimage a standard tool in topology and measure theory.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Formal Definitions}

\begin{definition}[Forward Image]
Let $f: X \to Y$ be a function and let $S \subseteq X$. The \textbf{image} of $S$ under $f$ is
\[
f(S) = \{f(s) \in Y : s \in S\} = \{y \in Y : (\exists s \in S)(f(s) = y)\}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Inverse Image / Preimage]
Let $f: X \to Y$ be a function and let $T \subseteq Y$. The \textbf{inverse image} (or \textbf{preimage}) of $T$ under $f$ is
\[
f^{-1}(T) = \{x \in X : f(x) \in T\}.
\]
\end{definition}

\subsection{Why Preimages Preserve Set Operations}

To understand why preimages preserve set operations without exception, notice that determining whether $x \in f^{-1}(T)$ comes down to evaluating a single output: does $f(x)$ land inside $T$?

Because $f$ is a function, every element $x$ has exactly one output $f(x)$. There is no ambiguity. The point $f(x)$ either belongs to an intersection, union, or complement, or it does not.

\begin{theorem}[Preimages Distribute Over Intersections]
Let $f: X \to Y$ be a function and let $T_1, T_2 \subseteq Y$. Then
\[
f^{-1}(T_1 \cap T_2) = f^{-1}(T_1) \cap f^{-1}(T_2).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $x \in X$ be arbitrary. We trace the logical biconditionals:
\begin{align*}
x \in f^{-1}(T_1 \cap T_2) 
&\iff f(x) \in T_1 \cap T_2 && \text{(definition of preimage)} \\
&\iff f(x) \in T_1 \text{ and } f(x) \in T_2 && \text{(definition of intersection)} \\
&\iff x \in f^{-1}(T_1) \text{ and } x \in f^{-1}(T_2) && \text{(definition of preimage)} \\
&\iff x \in f^{-1}(T_1) \cap f^{-1}(T_2) && \text{(definition of intersection)}.
\end{align*}
Since each step is an equivalence, the two sets contain identical elements:
\[
f^{-1}(T_1 \cap T_2) = f^{-1}(T_1) \cap f^{-1}(T_2). \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

The identical reasoning applies to complements and unions:

\begin{theorem}[Preimages Preserve Complements]
Let $f: X \to Y$ be a function and let $T \subseteq Y$. Then
\[
f^{-1}(Y \setminus T) = X \setminus f^{-1}(T).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $x \in X$ be arbitrary. We trace the equivalences:
\begin{align*}
x \in f^{-1}(Y \setminus T) 
&\iff f(x) \in Y \setminus T && \text{(definition of preimage)} \\
&\iff f(x) \notin T && \text{(definition of set difference)} \\
&\iff \neg (f(x) \in T) && \text{(meaning of } \notin \text{)} \\
&\iff \neg (x \in f^{-1}(T)) && \text{(definition of preimage)} \\
&\iff x \notin f^{-1}(T) && \text{(meaning of } \notin \text{)} \\
&\iff x \in X \setminus f^{-1}(T) && \text{(since } x \in X \text{)}.
\end{align*}
Because every step is an equivalence, the two sets are identical.
\end{proof}

This property also holds across infinite families of sets. If $\{T_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in I}$ is any family of subsets in $Y$, indexed by any set $I$:
\[
f^{-1}\left(\bigcup_{\alpha \in I} T_\alpha\right) = \bigcup_{\alpha \in I} f^{-1}(T_\alpha), \qquad f^{-1}\left(\bigcap_{\alpha \in I} T_\alpha\right) = \bigcap_{\alpha \in I} f^{-1}(T_\alpha).
\]
In algebraic terms, the preimage operation is a \textbf{Boolean algebra homomorphism}: it preserves intersections, unions, and complements.

\subsection{Where Forward Images Break Down}

Now contrast the behavior of preimages with what happens when we push sets forward through $f$.

\begin{theorem}
Let $f: X \to Y$ and let $S_1, S_2 \subseteq X$. Then
\[
f(S_1 \cap S_2) \subseteq f(S_1) \cap f(S_2).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $y \in f(S_1 \cap S_2)$ be arbitrary. By definition of the forward image, there exists some $x \in S_1 \cap S_2$ such that $f(x) = y$.

Since $x \in S_1 \cap S_2$, we know that $x \in S_1$ and $x \in S_2$.
\begin{itemize}
\item Because $x \in S_1$ and $f(x) = y$, we have $y \in f(S_1)$.
\item Because $x \in S_2$ and $f(x) = y$, we have $y \in f(S_2)$.
\end{itemize}
Thus $y \in f(S_1) \cap f(S_2)$. Hence $f(S_1 \cap S_2) \subseteq f(S_1) \cap f(S_2)$.
\end{proof}

\emph{Why does the reverse inclusion fail?} 
Suppose $y \in f(S_1) \cap f(S_2)$. This means $y \in f(S_1)$ and $y \in f(S_2)$.
Therefore, there exists some $x_1 \in S_1$ with $f(x_1) = y$, and there exists some $x_2 \in S_2$ with $f(x_2) = y$.
\emph{Does this guarantee $x_1 = x_2$?}

No! If $f$ is not injective, $x_1$ and $x_2$ can be two distinct inputs that have the same output $y$. If $x_1 \neq x_2$, neither input needs to belong to the intersection $S_1 \cap S_2$. As a result, $f(S_1 \cap S_2)$ can be empty even though $f(S_1) \cap f(S_2)$ is non-empty!

\begin{example}[A Clean Counterexample]
Let $f: \R \to \R$ be $f(x) = x^2$.
Take the disjoint subsets $S_1 = \{-2\}$ and $S_2 = \{2\}$.
Their intersection is empty: $S_1 \cap S_2 = \emptyset$, so
\[
f(S_1 \cap S_2) = f(\emptyset) = \emptyset.
\]
However, computing the images separately:
\[
f(S_1) = \{4\}, \qquad f(S_2) = \{4\}, \implies f(S_1) \cap f(S_2) = \{4\}.
\]
Here $\emptyset \subsetneq \{4\}$. The equality fails because $-2$ and $2$ both map to $4$ under the squaring map.
\end{example}

What property ensures that forward images preserve intersections? We need distinct inputs to produce distinct outputs. That is precisely injectivity from Chapter~13.

\begin{theorem}[Injectivity Characterizes Intersection Preservation]
Let $f: X \to Y$ be a function. Then
\[
f(S_1 \cap S_2) = f(S_1) \cap f(S_2) \quad \text{for all subsets } S_1, S_2 \subseteq X
\]
if and only if $f$ is injective.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
This is a bidirectional claim, so we prove both directions separately.

($\implies$) Assume that $f$ preserves intersections for all pairs of subsets. We must show $f$ is injective. We prove the contrapositive: suppose $a, b \in X$ with $a \neq b$. 

Consider the disjoint singletons $S_1 = \{a\}$ and $S_2 = \{b\}$. Since $a \neq b$, we have $S_1 \cap S_2 = \emptyset$, so
\[
f(S_1 \cap S_2) = f(\emptyset) = \emptyset.
\]
By our assumption, $f(S_1) \cap f(S_2) = f(S_1 \cap S_2) = \emptyset$. But direct evaluation gives
\[
f(S_1) = \{f(a)\} \quad \text{and} \quad f(S_2) = \{f(b)\}.
\]
Since their intersection is empty, $\{f(a)\} \cap \{f(b)\} = \emptyset$, which forces $f(a) \neq f(b)$. Hence $f$ is injective.

($\impliedby$) Assume that $f$ is injective. We already know that $f(S_1 \cap S_2) \subseteq f(S_1) \cap f(S_2)$ holds for any function. It remains to prove the reverse inclusion: $f(S_1) \cap f(S_2) \subseteq f(S_1 \cap S_2)$.

Let $y \in f(S_1) \cap f(S_2)$. Then $y \in f(S_1)$ and $y \in f(S_2)$.
By definition of forward image, there exists some $x_1 \in S_1$ with $f(x_1) = y$, and some $x_2 \in S_2$ with $f(x_2) = y$.
Since $f(x_1) = y = f(x_2)$ and $f$ is injective, we must have $x_1 = x_2$.
Call this shared input $x$. Because $x \in S_1$ and $x \in S_2$, we have $x \in S_1 \cap S_2$.
Therefore, $y = f(x) \in f(S_1 \cap S_2)$.
This establishes that $f(S_1) \cap f(S_2) \subseteq f(S_1 \cap S_2)$, completing the equality.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Forward Images Distribute Over Unions]
Let $f: X \to Y$ and let $S_1, S_2 \subseteq X$. Then
\[
f(S_1 \cup S_2) = f(S_1) \cup f(S_2).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We trace the element membership directly:
\begin{align*}
y \in f(S_1 \cup S_2) 
&\iff (\exists x \in S_1 \cup S_2)(f(x) = y) \\
&\iff \exists x \in X \, ((x \in S_1 \lor x \in S_2) \land f(x) = y) \\
&\iff \exists x \in X \, ((x \in S_1 \land f(x) = y) \lor (x \in S_2 \land f(x) = y)) \\
&\iff (\exists x \in S_1)(f(x) = y) \lor (\exists x \in S_2)(f(x) = y) \\
&\iff y \in f(S_1) \lor y \in f(S_2) \\
&\iff y \in f(S_1) \cup f(S_2). \qedhere
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

Why do forward images preserve unions but not intersections? This follows from the quantifier logic of Chapter~4: the existential quantifier $\exists$ distributes across ``or'' ($\lor$), but not across ``and'' ($\land$). To belong to the image of a union, an element needs to come from at least one source set. To belong to the image of an intersection, the same input must belong to both source sets, which distinct inputs with identical outputs prevent.

\subsection{Round Trips: Pushing Forward and Pulling Backward}

What happens when you combine both operations in sequence? If you take a set $S \subseteq X$, push it forward to $f(S)$, and then pull it back to $f^{-1}(f(S))$, do you return to exactly the set $S$ you started with? 

Not always. Let us trace both round trips.

\begin{theorem}[The Domain Round Trip]
Let $f: X \to Y$ and let $S \subseteq X$. Then
\[
S \subseteq f^{-1}(f(S)).
\]
Equality holds for all subsets $S \subseteq X$ if and only if $f$ is injective.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $x \in S$. Then its output $f(x)$ belongs to $f(S)$ by definition of the forward image. By definition of the preimage, any point whose output lands in $f(S)$ belongs to $f^{-1}(f(S))$. Thus $x \in f^{-1}(f(S))$, proving $S \subseteq f^{-1}(f(S))$.

Now consider when equality holds.
If $f$ is injective and $x \in f^{-1}(f(S))$, then $f(x) \in f(S)$, so $f(x) = f(s)$ for some $s \in S$. Since $f$ is injective, $x = s$, which means $x \in S$. Thus $f^{-1}(f(S)) \subseteq S$, giving equality.

Conversely, suppose $f^{-1}(f(S)) = S$ holds for all $S \subseteq X$. If $f(a) = f(b)$, set $S = \{a\}$. Then $f(S) = \{f(a)\}$. Since $f(b) = f(a)$, the element $b$ satisfies $f(b) \in f(S)$, so $b \in f^{-1}(f(S)) = \{a\}$. Therefore $b = a$, proving $f$ is injective.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The Codomain Round Trip]
Let $f: X \to Y$ and let $T \subseteq Y$. Then
\[
f(f^{-1}(T)) \subseteq T.
\]
Equality holds for all subsets $T \subseteq Y$ if and only if $f$ is surjective.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $y \in f(f^{-1}(T))$. Then $y = f(x)$ for some $x \in f^{-1}(T)$. By definition of the preimage, $x \in f^{-1}(T)$ means $f(x) \in T$. Since $y = f(x)$, we have $y \in T$. Thus $f(f^{-1}(T)) \subseteq T$.

Now consider when equality holds.
If $f$ is surjective, let $y \in T$. Because $f$ is surjective, there exists some $x \in X$ with $f(x) = y \in T$. Thus $x \in f^{-1}(T)$, so $y = f(x) \in f(f^{-1}(T))$, giving $T \subseteq f(f^{-1}(T))$ and hence equality.

Conversely, suppose $f(f^{-1}(T)) = T$ holds for all $T \subseteq Y$. In particular, choose $T = \{y\}$ for any $y \in Y$. Then $f(f^{-1}(\{y\})) = \{y\}$. The set $f(f^{-1}(\{y\}))$ cannot be empty, so $f^{-1}(\{y\})$ is non-empty. This means there is at least one $x \in X$ with $f(x) = y$, proving $f$ is surjective.
\end{proof}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The Round-Trip Duality:}
\begin{align*}
S &\subseteq f^{-1}(f(S)) && \text{(equality for all } S \iff f \text{ is injective)} \\
f(f^{-1}(T)) &\subseteq T && \text{(equality for all } T \iff f \text{ is surjective)}
\end{align*}
Pushing forward and pulling backward do not cancel each other out. On the domain side, injectivity ensures that no points outside $S$ map into $f(S)$, so the preimage returns exactly $S$. On the codomain side, surjectivity ensures that every element of $T$ is hit by some point in $X$, so pushing forward the preimage covers all of $T$.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{Connection to Topology: Continuity via Preimages}

This good behavior of preimages is why modern topology defines \emph{continuous functions} via inverse images:
\begin{center}
\emph{A function $f: X \to Y$ is continuous if for every open set $V \subseteq Y$, the preimage $f^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$.}
\end{center}
An \textbf{open set} is a region where every point has room around it in all directions---like an open interval $(a, b)$ on the real line. 

If topologists had tried to define continuity using forward images (demanding that $f(U)$ be open whenever $U$ is open), familiar smooth functions would fail:
\begin{itemize}
\item The parabola $f(x) = x^2$ maps the open interval $(-1, 1)$ to $[0, 1)$, which includes its boundary point $0$.
\item The constant function $f(x) = 5$ maps $\R$ to the single point $\{5\}$, which is not open in $\R$.
\end{itemize}
Preimages do not suffer from these boundary issues: if an output sits inside an open set $V$, continuity ensures that nearby inputs stay inside $V$, so $f^{-1}(V)$ remains open.

\subsection{The Limits of Set Transport and Where We Go Next}

Transporting sets forward and backward connects different mathematical areas:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Measure theory and probability:} In rigorous probability, a random variable $X$ is a measurable function from a sample space $\Omega$ to $\R$. The probability of an event $E \subseteq \R$ is computed by pulling $E$ back: $\mathbb{P}(X \in E) = \mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(E))$.
\item \textbf{Algebraic pullbacks:} Differential forms, cohomology classes, and vector bundles all pull back along functions via preimages.
\end{itemize}

Yet image and preimage operations have a common limit: they transport subsets between two pre-existing spaces $X$ and $Y$.

They cannot build a \emph{new} mathematical space from within an existing one---collapsing redundancies, factoring out symmetries, or gluing opposite edges of a square together to create a cylinder or a torus.

To construct new spaces by grouping points together, we need a new tool. In Chapter~15, we introduce \textbf{Relations and Equivalence}, showing how three simple properties allow us to partition a space and build new mathematical objects.

\subsection{Exercises}

\subsubsection*{Warm-Up and Mental Checks}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Let $f: \R \to \R$ be the constant function $f(x) = 7$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Compute $f([0, 10])$ and $f(\emptyset)$.
\item Compute $f^{-1}(\{7\})$, $f^{-1}(\{3\})$, and $f^{-1}([0, 10])$.
\end{enumerate}

\item Let $f: \R \to \R$ be the absolute value function $f(x) = |x|$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Compute $f([-3, 2])$.
\item Compute $f^{-1}([1, 4])$ and $f^{-1}([-5, -1])$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Practice and Computations}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item Let $f: \R \to \R$ be defined by $f(x) = x^2 - 2x$. Find:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $f([0, 3])$.
\item $f^{-1}([-1, 3])$.
\item $f^{-1}(\{-5\})$.
\end{enumerate}

\item Let $f: X \to Y$ and let $S \subseteq X$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Does forward image preserve complements? That is, does $f(X \setminus S) = Y \setminus f(S)$ always hold?
\item Either prove this equality or provide a concrete counterexample with simple finite sets.
\end{enumerate}

\item Let $f: X \to Y$ and let $T_1, T_2 \subseteq Y$. Prove directly using element chasing that
\[
f^{-1}(T_1 \setminus T_2) = f^{-1}(T_1) \setminus f^{-1}(T_2).
\]
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Deeper Structural Problems}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Arbitrary Families:} Let $f: X \to Y$ and let $\{T_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in I}$ be an arbitrary collection of subsets of $Y$, where the index set $I$ may be finite, countably infinite, or uncountable.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove that $f^{-1}\left(\bigcup_{\alpha \in I} T_\alpha\right) = \bigcup_{\alpha \in I} f^{-1}(T_\alpha)$.
\item Prove that $f^{-1}\left(\bigcap_{\alpha \in I} T_\alpha\right) = \bigcap_{\alpha \in I} f^{-1}(T_\alpha)$.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Forward Images of Intersections:} Let $f: X \to Y$. Prove that $f$ is injective if and only if for every pair of disjoint subsets $A, B \subseteq X$ (meaning $A \cap B = \emptyset$), their images are disjoint: $f(A) \cap f(B) = \emptyset$.

\item \textbf{Round-Trip Invertibility:} Let $f: X \to Y$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove that $f$ is bijective if and only if for every subset $S \subseteq X$, $f^{-1}(f(S)) = S$, and for every subset $T \subseteq Y$, $f(f^{-1}(T)) = T$.
\item In this case, how does the set operation $f^{-1}(T)$ relate to the inverse function $f^{-1}: Y \to X$?
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
