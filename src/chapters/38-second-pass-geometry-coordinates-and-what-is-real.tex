\section{Second Pass: Geometry, Coordinates, and What Is Real}
\chapterline

In the previous chapter, we explored topology as the study of stable nearness. By setting aside rulers, angles, and coordinates, topology isolates the qualitative properties that survive continuous stretching: connectedness, compactness, and the existence of holes.

Physics and engineering, however, still need distances, angles, velocities, and curvature---and for those, we need calculus.

Before Ren\'e Descartes introduced analytic geometry in 1637, geometry was synthetic: Euclid proved theorems by drawing circles and straight lines in the sand. Descartes realized that if you lay down perpendicular coordinate axes, every geometric point becomes a pair of numbers $(x, y)$, and every curve becomes an algebraic equation. Calculus could then proceed by taking derivatives of formulas.

Yet coordinates are an observer's choice rather than an intrinsic feature of the space itself. Different coordinate systems describe the same underlying geometry with different numbers.

When studying a curved space---such as the surface of a sphere or four-dimensional spacetime in general relativity---no single coordinate chart can cover the entire space without singularities or distortion. Differential geometry provides the framework for doing calculus on curved spaces intrinsically, without requiring a single global coordinate system or an ambient higher-dimensional space.

\begin{historybox}
In 1827, Carl Friedrich Gauss proved his \emph{Theorema Egregium} (Latin for ``Remarkable Theorem''), demonstrating that the Gaussian curvature of a surface can be determined entirely from distances measured along the surface itself. Gaussian curvature measures how much a two-dimensional surface bends intrinsically: a flat sheet of paper has zero Gaussian curvature and can be rolled into a cylinder without stretching, whereas a sphere has positive curvature and wrinkles if you press it flat onto a table. Gauss showed that two-dimensional creatures living on a surface could measure its curvature without ever looking down on it from an outside third dimension. In 1854, Gauss's student Bernhard Riemann generalized this in his Habilitation lecture (the formal lecture required to qualify as a professor in Germany), showing how to define geometry in any number of dimensions through an infinitesimal distance formula now called a Riemannian metric.

For decades, mathematicians resisted higher-dimensional curved spaces as abstract fantasies, while Kantian philosophers argued that Euclidean geometry was an innate prerequisite of human thought. The tools available at the time were multivariable calculus, linear algebra, and the early tensor calculus (then called ``absolute differential calculus'') developed by Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro and Tullio Levi-Civita to write equations that retain their form in every coordinate system.

In 1912, Albert Einstein realized that Newtonian gravity was an illusion: mass curves four-dimensional spacetime, and freely falling objects follow the straightest possible paths (geodesics) through that curved geometry. Desperate for mathematical tools to express this without preferred coordinate frames, Einstein turned to his friend, the mathematician Marcel Grossmann, who introduced him to the work of Ricci-Curbastro and Levi-Civita. In 1918, Hermann Weyl published \emph{Raum, Zeit, Materie} (\emph{Space, Time, Matter}), establishing coordinate-free differential geometry and pioneering gauge theory, which models fundamental forces through local symmetries.

This shift reshaped general relativity, black hole physics, the expanding universe, and the geometric gauge theories that form the Standard Model of particle physics. Yet the deepest question remains open: smooth differential geometry assumes spacetime is a continuous manifold at every scale, but quantum mechanics dictates that at the subatomic Planck scale ($\approx 10^{-35}$ meters), quantum uncertainty causes spacetime to fluctuate violently. Unifying smooth curved geometry with quantum mechanics in a theory of quantum gravity remains one of the greatest unsolved challenges in mathematical physics.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The Coordinate Trap: Map versus Territory}

Consider the surface of the Earth. A navigator can describe any location using latitude $\theta \in [-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ and longitude $\varphi \in [0, 2\pi)$. This coordinate system is practically indispensable, but it creates mathematical illusions that exist only on paper:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ambiguity at the poles}: At the North Pole, every value of longitude $\varphi$ describes the exact same geographic point. The coordinate grid maps an entire segment on the map to a single geographic point.
  \item \textbf{Artificial velocity spikes}: If you walk in a small circle near the North Pole, your longitude $\varphi$ can change by $360^\circ$ in a few seconds. The angular velocity $\frac{d\varphi}{dt}$ is large even though your physical speed is modest.
  \item \textbf{Map distortion}: On a standard Mercator projection, Greenland appears comparable in size to Africa, even though Africa is actually fourteen times larger. Near the poles, the map distorts physical areas significantly.
\end{itemize}

None of these pathologies belong to the Earth itself. The Earth is a smooth, symmetric sphere. The coordinate singularity at the North Pole is a property of the coordinate representation, not of the sphere itself.

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If an airplane flies the shortest route from Tokyo to London, the flight path drawn on a flat Mercator map appears as a wide arc curving far north over Siberia and the Arctic. Why does the pilot not fly along the straight line drawn with a ruler between the two cities on the map?

Because the straight line on the flat map is an artifact of the projection. The path curving across the Arctic is a \emph{great circle}---the shortest, straightest possible route across the curved sphere. The map distorted straightness.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{The Guiding Principle of Differential Geometry:}
An intrinsic geometric property (such as the length of a path, the curvature of space, or the rate of fluid expansion) must not depend on the arbitrary coordinate grid chosen by an observer. Coordinates are a temporary computational convenience; geometric reality is coordinate-free.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Coordinate Illusions and Einstein's Hole Argument}

Separating geometric reality from coordinate systems required decades of unlearning. Even the founders of the field repeatedly mistook coordinate artifacts for features of nature.

Between 1913 and late 1915, Albert Einstein wrestled with what is now known as the \textbf{Hole Argument} (\emph{Lochbetrachtung}). Einstein wanted the laws of gravity to be \emph{generally covariant}---meaning that the equations look identical no matter what coordinate system an observer uses. He imagined an empty region of spacetime (a ``hole'') surrounded by matter. If the gravitational equations are generally covariant, one can smoothly distort the coordinate grid inside the hole while keeping it unchanged outside. This coordinate distortion generates two mathematically distinct metric formulas, $g_{\mu\nu}$ and $g'_{\mu\nu}$, from the exact same matter distribution. (Here, the metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$ is the mathematical ruler that computes physical distances and time intervals from coordinate increments.) Einstein initially concluded that if the same physical matter could produce two mathematically distinct metric fields, general covariance would violate determinism.

For two years, Einstein abandoned general covariance to work on a restricted theory. His breakthrough arrived in November 1915: \emph{spacetime points have no physical identity of their own}. The coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$ are mere bookkeeping tags. What is physically real is not the value of a metric at a coordinate label, but the intersection of physical paths---what Einstein called \emph{spacetime coincidences}, such as two particles colliding or a light beam striking a clock face. General covariance was saved, and the Einstein field equations were born.

A second coordinate illusion lasted even longer. In 1916, Karl Schwarzschild solved Einstein's equations for the spacetime around a spherical star. At the radius $r = 2GM/c^2$ (now known as the event horizon of a black hole), the time component of his metric formula vanished and the radial component became infinite. For forty years, leading physicists (including Einstein in 1939) believed this was a physical barrier that crushed infalling matter. Only in 1958 did David Finkelstein and Martin Kruskal show that the singularity at $r = 2GM/c^2$ is an artifact of Schwarzschild's coordinate choice---completely analogous to the North Pole singularity on a Mercator map. An astronaut falling across the event horizon experiences nothing unusual at the boundary; the infinite blowup belonged to the map, not the territory.

A third trap arises in interpreting tangent vectors on manifolds. When first encountering tangent vectors on a surface, one often visualizes an arrow protruding into an ambient three-dimensional space. An abstract manifold, however, does not require an ambient embedding. Differential geometry defines tangent vectors intrinsically: as \emph{directional derivative operators} (derivations) or equivalence classes of curves through a point $p$. A tangent vector at $p$ acts on smooth functions defined on the manifold, measuring their rate of change along curves passing through $p$.

\subsection{Manifolds and the Atlas of Charts}

How can we do calculus on a curved space without ambient coordinates? The standard approach uses the cartographic concept of an \textbf{atlas}.

You cannot flatten the entire surface of the Earth onto a single sheet of paper without tearing or distortion. But you can easily make a flat map of a single city or province. An atlas is a book of flat maps whose edges overlap. To navigate across the globe, you read one flat map until you near its border, then transition smoothly to the next.

A \textbf{topological manifold} $M$ of dimension $n$ is a topological space that locally looks like ordinary flat Euclidean space $\R^n$. Around every point $p \in M$, there is an open neighborhood $U \subseteq M$ and a homeomorphism (a continuous bijection with a continuous inverse)
\[
\phi: U \longrightarrow \widetilde{U} \subseteq \R^n.
\]
The pair $(U, \phi)$ is called a \textbf{chart}, or local coordinate map. Just as an atlas of the Earth consists of overlapping local maps of individual regions, an \textbf{atlas} on $M$ is a collection of charts $\{(U_\alpha, \phi_\alpha)\}$ whose domains cover all of $M$, so that $\bigcup_\alpha U_\alpha = M$.

\paragraph{A Concrete Example: Stereographic Projection of the Circle.}
Consider the unit circle in the plane,
\[
S^1 = \{(x, y) \in \R^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}.
\]
Can we cover the circle with a single coordinate chart mapping into $\R$? From Chapter~37, we know that $S^1$ is compact (closed and bounded in $\R^2$), while any open subset of $\R$ is non-compact. Because compactness is a topological property preserved under homeomorphisms, no single chart can ever cover all of $S^1$. We need at least two.

Stereographic projection provides two elegant charts:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Chart 1 (North Pole Projection)}: Let $N = (0, 1)$ be the North pole. For any point $P = (x, y) \in S^1 \setminus \{N\}$, draw the straight line from $N$ through $P$ until it hits the horizontal axis $y = 0$. The line has parameterization $L(t) = (tx, 1 - t(1 - y))$. It intersects $y = 0$ when $t = \frac{1}{1-y}$, giving the real coordinate
  \[
  u = \phi_1(x, y) = \frac{x}{1-y}.
  \]
  The domain is $U_1 = S^1 \setminus \{N\}$, covering every point of the circle except the North pole.
  \item \textbf{Chart 2 (South Pole Projection)}: Let $S = (0, -1)$ be the South pole. For any point $P = (x, y) \in S^1 \setminus \{S\}$, draw the straight line from $S$ through $P$ until it hits the horizontal axis $y = 0$. A parallel calculation gives $t = \frac{1}{1+y}$, yielding the coordinate
  \[
  v = \phi_2(x, y) = \frac{x}{1+y}.
  \]
  The domain is $U_2 = S^1 \setminus \{S\}$, covering every point except the South pole.
\end{enumerate}
Together, $U_1 \cup U_2 = S^1$. Every point on the circle has a home in at least one chart (Figure~\ref{geom:fig-stereographic}).

\subsection{Transition Maps: Where Calculus Lives}

When two charts $(U_\alpha, \phi_\alpha)$ and $(U_\beta, \phi_\beta)$ overlap, a point in $U_\alpha \cap U_\beta$ receives two competing coordinate descriptions: $x = \phi_\alpha(p)$ and $y = \phi_\beta(p)$.

The conversion between these descriptions is the \textbf{transition map}:
\[
\tau_{\alpha \beta} \;=\; \phi_\beta \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1} : \phi_\alpha(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta) \longrightarrow \phi_\beta(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta).
\]

Let us compute the transition map for our circle charts. On the overlap $U_1 \cap U_2 = S^1 \setminus \{N, S\}$, every point has both a $u$-coordinate and a $v$-coordinate. Multiplying them together gives:
\[
u \cdot v = \left(\frac{x}{1-y}\right) \left(\frac{x}{1+y}\right) = \frac{x^2}{1-y^2}.
\]
Because $(x, y)$ lies on the circle, $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, which means $1 - y^2 = x^2$. Substituting this into the denominator yields:
\[
u \cdot v = \frac{x^2}{x^2} = 1 \quad \implies \quad v(u) = \frac{1}{u}.
\]
The transition map converting $u$-coordinates to $v$-coordinates is $\tau_{12}(u) = 1/u$. Since the origin $u = 0$ corresponds to the South pole (which is excluded from the overlap $U_1 \cap U_2$), this map is defined on $\R \setminus \{0\}$. The derivative of $1/u$ is $-1/u^2$, its second derivative is $2/u^3$, and derivatives of all orders exist. The transition map is infinitely differentiable ($C^\infty$).

The transition map $\tau_{\alpha\beta}$ is an ordinary multivariable function between open subsets of $\R^n$, where differentiability is already defined. Figure~\ref{geom:fig-transition-map} illustrates how charts transfer calculus from $\R^n$ to the manifold.

\begin{formalbox}
A topological manifold $M$ is a \textbf{smooth manifold} if all its transition maps $\phi_\beta \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1}$ are $C^\infty$ smooth functions between open subsets of $\R^n$.
\end{formalbox}

Calculus on a manifold does not require an ambient embedding space. A function $f: M \to \R$ is defined to be smooth if its local representation $f \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1}: \phi_\alpha(U_\alpha) \to \R$ is smooth in the standard sense of multivariable calculus. Because transition maps between charts are smooth, the multivariable chain rule ensures that smoothness of $f$ is independent of the choice of chart.

\subsection{What Manifold Geometry Unlocks---and Where Smoothness Breaks Down}

The coordinate-free manifold perspective reshaped modern physics and mathematics:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{General Relativity}: The Einstein Field Equations, $G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu}$, describe gravity without any ambient background space, relating the intrinsic curvature tensor $G_{\mu\nu}$ of four-dimensional spacetime to the stress-energy tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$ of matter and radiation. This geometry predicted gravitational waves, black holes, and cosmological expansion.
  \item \textbf{Global Topology via Calculus}: The de Rham cohomology theorem proves that differential forms (integrands that measure lengths, areas, and flux) compute the exact topological hole invariants of a manifold ($H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \cong H^k(M; \R)$). Similarly, the Gauss--Bonnet theorem proves that integrating Gaussian curvature $K$ over an entire closed surface yields its topological Euler characteristic: $\int_M K \, dA = 2\pi \chi(M)$. Local calculus detects global topology.
  \item \textbf{Gauge Theories in Particle Physics}: The Standard Model of particle physics is written in the language of connections and curvature on fiber bundles---spaces where an internal symmetry group (such as $\mathrm{U}(1)$ for electromagnetism or $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ for the strong nuclear force) is attached smoothly to every point of spacetime.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
Yet the smooth manifold paradigm faces acute barriers:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Inability to Handle Physical Singularities}: Smooth manifolds require infinitely differentiable charts. They break down completely at physical singularities---such as the center of a black hole or the cosmic initial singularity of the Big Bang---where curvature diverges to infinity and spacetime ceases to be a smooth manifold.
  \item \textbf{Failure at Geometric Cusps and Self-Intersections}: Shapes defined by simple polynomial equations often have sharp corners or self-crossings. For example, the cuspidal cubic curve $y^2 = x^3$ has a sharp pinch at the origin, and the nodal curve $y^2 = x^2(x+1)$ crosses itself at the origin like a figure-eight. At the crossing, any small open neighborhood contains four branches meeting at a point, which is topologically impossible for an open interval in $\R^1$. Differential geometry cannot study these points directly without discarding them.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mental check: Why one chart is never enough.} Explain why the 2-sphere $S^2 = \{(x, y, z) \in \R^3 : x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\}$ cannot be covered by a single chart $(U, \phi)$ with $U = S^2$ and $\phi(U)$ an open subset of $\R^2$. (Hint: Recall from Chapter~37 that $S^2$ is compact, but non-empty open subsets of $\R^2$ are never compact.)
  \item \textbf{Stereographic inversion.} In the stereographic projection of the circle $S^1$ from the North pole $N = (0, 1)$, we obtained the coordinate $u = \frac{x}{1-y}$ for any point $(x, y) \in S^1 \setminus \{N\}$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Invert this map algebraically to show that the point $(x, y)$ can be reconstructed from $u$ by the formulas
    \[
    x = \frac{2u}{u^2 + 1}, \qquad y = \frac{u^2 - 1}{u^2 + 1}.
    \]
    \item Verify directly that $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ for every real number $u \in \R$.
    \item What point on the circle corresponds to $u = 0$? What happens to $(x, y)$ as $u \to +\infty$ and as $u \to -\infty$?
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{When transition maps fail to be smooth.} Consider the real line $\R$ equipped with two charts: $(U_1, \phi_1)$ with $U_1 = \R$ and $\phi_1(x) = x$, and $(U_2, \phi_2)$ with $U_2 = \R$ and $\phi_2(x) = x^{1/3}$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Compute the transition map $\tau_{12} = \phi_2 \circ \phi_1^{-1}$ and its inverse $\tau_{21} = \phi_1 \circ \phi_2^{-1}$.
    \item Is $\tau_{12}$ differentiable at the origin $x = 0$?
    \item Does the collection of these two charts form a valid smooth atlas for $\R$? Explain why or why not.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Tangent vectors as derivations.} Let $M$ be a smooth manifold and $p \in M$. A derivation at $p$ is a linear map $v: C^\infty(M) \to \R$ that satisfies the Leibniz product rule:
  \[
  v(fg) = f(p)v(g) + g(p)v(f) \quad \text{for all } f, g \in C^\infty(M).
  \]
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Prove that if $c \in \R$ is a constant function on $M$, then $v(c) = 0$. (Hint: Apply the product rule to the function $1 = 1 \cdot 1$, and then use linearity for $c = c \cdot 1$.)
    \item Explain in one intuitive paragraph why this algebraic condition captures the ordinary product rule of calculus without needing coordinate axes.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Intrinsic curvature: Cylinder versus sphere.} An ant crawls on a cylinder of radius $R$; another crawls on a sphere of radius $R$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item If you slit a hollow cylinder along a straight line parallel to its axis, you can unroll it completely flat onto a tabletop without stretching, tearing, or wrinkling. According to Gauss's \emph{Theorema Egregium}, what is the Gaussian curvature $K$ of the cylinder?
    \item Can you press a spherical orange peel flat onto a tabletop without tearing or squishing it? What does this imply about the Gaussian curvature of the sphere?
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
