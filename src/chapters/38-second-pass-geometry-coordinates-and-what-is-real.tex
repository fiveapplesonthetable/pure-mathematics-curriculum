\section{Second Pass: Geometry, Coordinates, and What Is Real}
\chapterline

In the previous chapter, we explored topology as the study of stable nearness. By setting aside rulers, angles, and coordinates, topology isolates the qualitative properties that survive continuous stretching: connectedness, compactness, and the existence of holes.

Physics and engineering, however, still need distances, angles, velocities, and curvature---and for those, we need calculus.

Before Ren\'e Descartes introduced analytic geometry (geometry done with coordinates and equations) in 1637, geometry was \emph{synthetic}: Euclid reasoned directly about figures built from straight lines and circles, with no numbers attached to points. Descartes realized that if you lay down perpendicular coordinate axes, every geometric point becomes a pair of numbers $(x, y)$, and every curve becomes an algebraic equation. Geometric questions could then be answered by algebra, and later by taking derivatives of formulas.

Yet coordinates are an observer's choice rather than a feature of the space itself. Rotate the axes or move the origin and every number changes, while the shape stays the same. Different coordinate systems describe the same underlying geometry with different numbers. Figure~\ref{geom:fig-two-grids} shows two points described in two coordinate systems: their coordinates change completely, while the distance between them stays $5$.

On a curved space---such as the surface of a sphere, or the four-dimensional spacetime of general relativity---no single coordinate system can cover the whole space without breaking down somewhere: there are points where one point gets many coordinate values, or where formulas blow up to infinity. Such points are called \emph{coordinate singularities}. Differential geometry is the framework for doing calculus on curved spaces \emph{intrinsically}, meaning using only measurements made within the space. It needs neither a single global coordinate system nor a larger surrounding (\emph{ambient}) space to sit in. This chapter asks which features of a geometric description are real and which belong only to the coordinates.

\begin{historybox}
In 1827, Carl Friedrich Gauss proved his \emph{Theorema Egregium} (Latin for ``Remarkable Theorem''), demonstrating that the Gaussian curvature of a surface can be determined entirely from distances measured along the surface itself. Gaussian curvature measures how much a two-dimensional surface bends intrinsically: a flat sheet of paper has zero Gaussian curvature and can be rolled into a cylinder without stretching, whereas a sphere has positive curvature and wrinkles if you press it flat onto a table. Gauss showed that two-dimensional creatures living on a surface could measure its curvature without ever looking down on it from an outside third dimension. For example, on a sphere a triangle whose sides are shortest paths has angles adding up to more than $180^\circ$, something its inhabitants can check with a protractor (Figure~\ref{geom:fig-sphere-triangle}). In 1854, Gauss's student Bernhard Riemann generalized this in his Habilitation lecture (the formal lecture required to qualify as a professor in Germany), showing how to define geometry in any number of dimensions through a formula for the length of a tiny step in any direction at each point, now called a Riemannian metric (Chapter~23).

For decades, mathematicians resisted higher-dimensional curved spaces as abstract fantasies, while Kantian philosophers argued that Euclidean geometry was an innate prerequisite of human thought. The tools available at the time were multivariable calculus, linear algebra, and the early tensor calculus (then called ``absolute differential calculus'') developed by Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro and Tullio Levi-Civita. A \emph{tensor} is a quantity, such as a metric, whose list of components changes by a fixed rule when coordinates change; Ricci-Curbastro and Levi-Civita used tensors to write equations that keep the same form in every coordinate system.

In 1912, Albert Einstein realized that gravity need not be treated as a force: mass curves four-dimensional spacetime, and freely falling objects follow the straightest possible paths (geodesics) through that curved geometry. Needing mathematical tools to express this without a preferred coordinate system, Einstein turned to his friend, the mathematician Marcel Grossmann, who introduced him to the work of Ricci-Curbastro and Levi-Civita. In 1918, Hermann Weyl published \emph{Raum, Zeit, Materie} (\emph{Space, Time, Matter}), establishing coordinate-free differential geometry and pioneering gauge theory, which models the fundamental forces through symmetries that can be applied independently at each point of spacetime.

This shift reshaped general relativity, black hole physics, the expanding universe, and the geometric gauge theories that make up the Standard Model of particle physics (the current theory of the known elementary particles and of every fundamental force except gravity). One question remains open: smooth differential geometry assumes spacetime is a continuous manifold at every scale, but quantum mechanics suggests that at the Planck scale ($\approx 10^{-35}$ meters) the geometry of spacetime itself should fluctuate. Unifying smooth curved geometry with quantum mechanics in a theory of quantum gravity is an unsolved problem in mathematical physics.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The Coordinate Trap: Map versus Territory}

Consider the surface of the Earth. A navigator can describe any location using latitude $\theta \in [-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ and longitude $\varphi \in [0, 2\pi)$. This coordinate system is indispensable in practice, but it produces features that exist only on paper:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ambiguity at the poles}: At the North Pole, every value of longitude $\varphi$ describes the exact same geographic point. A whole segment of the coordinate rectangle (latitude $\pi/2$, every longitude) corresponds to a single point of the Earth.
  \item \textbf{Artificial velocity spikes}: If you walk in a small circle around the North Pole, your longitude $\varphi$ changes by $360^\circ$ in a few seconds. The angular velocity $d\varphi/dt$ (the rate at which your longitude changes) is huge even though your physical speed is modest (Figure~\ref{geom:fig-pole-circle}).
  \item \textbf{Map distortion}: The Mercator projection is the familiar rectangular world map on which lines of latitude and longitude form a grid of straight lines. On it, Greenland appears comparable in size to Africa, even though Africa is about fourteen times larger. The closer a region is to a pole, the more the map enlarges its area: a region at latitude $\theta$ is drawn with its area multiplied by $1/\cos^2\theta$ (Figure~\ref{geom:fig-mercator}).
\end{itemize}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If an airplane flies the shortest route from Tokyo to London, the flight path drawn on a flat Mercator map appears as a wide arc curving far north over Siberia and the Arctic. Why does the pilot not fly along the straight line drawn with a ruler between the two cities on the map?

Because the straight line on the flat map is an artifact of the projection. The path curving across the Arctic is a \emph{great circle} (a circle on the sphere whose center is the center of the Earth, like the equator)---the shortest, straightest possible route across the curved sphere.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{The Guiding Principle of Differential Geometry:}
An intrinsic geometric property (such as the length of a path, the curvature of space, or the rate of fluid expansion) must not depend on the arbitrary coordinate grid chosen by an observer. Coordinates are a temporary computational convenience; geometric reality is coordinate-free.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Coordinate Illusions and Einstein's Hole Argument}

Separating geometric reality from coordinate systems took decades. Even the founders of the field mistook coordinate artifacts for features of nature, more than once.

Between 1913 and late 1915, Albert Einstein wrestled with what is now known as the \textbf{Hole Argument} (\emph{Lochbetrachtung}). Einstein wanted the laws of gravity to be \emph{generally covariant}, meaning that the equations take the same form in every coordinate system an observer might use. In his theory, gravity is described by the \textbf{metric tensor} $g_{\mu\nu}$, the mathematical ruler that converts small coordinate steps into physical distances and time intervals. The indices $\mu$ and $\nu$ each run over the four coordinates $t, x, y, z$, so $g_{\mu\nu}$ is a $4 \times 4$ table of functions on spacetime. Einstein imagined an empty region of spacetime (a ``hole'') surrounded by matter. If the gravitational equations are generally covariant, one can smoothly distort the coordinate grid inside the hole while keeping it unchanged outside (Figure~\ref{geom:fig-hole}). Rewriting a solution in the distorted coordinates and then reading the new formulas as if they were in the old coordinates gives a second solution: two mathematically distinct metric formulas, $g_{\mu\nu}$ and $g'_{\mu\nu}$, that agree outside the hole, differ inside it, and come from exactly the same matter distribution. Einstein initially concluded that general covariance would violate determinism: the matter, and everything outside the hole, would fail to fix the geometry inside it.

For two years, Einstein abandoned general covariance to work on a restricted theory. His breakthrough arrived in November 1915: \emph{spacetime points have no physical identity of their own}. The coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$ are only bookkeeping tags. What is physically real is not the value of a metric at a coordinate label, but the meeting of physical paths---what Einstein called \emph{spacetime coincidences}, such as two particles colliding or a light beam striking a clock face. The metrics $g_{\mu\nu}$ and $g'_{\mu\nu}$ describe the same coincidences, so they describe the same physical world in two different coordinate descriptions, and determinism is not threatened. Einstein restored general covariance and published his field equations that same month.

A second coordinate illusion lasted even longer. In 1916, Karl Schwarzschild solved Einstein's equations for the spacetime around a spherical star of mass $M$. At the radius $r = 2GM/c^2$, where $G$ is Newton's gravitational constant and $c$ is the speed of light, the time component of his metric formula became zero and the radial component $g_{rr}$ became infinite. This radius is now known as the \emph{event horizon} of a black hole: the boundary beyond which nothing, not even light, can escape. For decades, leading physicists (including Einstein in 1939) believed the blowup at this radius was a real physical barrier. In 1958 David Finkelstein, and in 1960 Martin Kruskal and George Szekeres, found coordinates in which nothing blows up at $r = 2GM/c^2$, showing that the singularity there is an artifact of Schwarzschild's coordinate choice---analogous to the North Pole on a latitude--longitude map. An astronaut falling across the event horizon experiences nothing unusual at the boundary; the infinite blowup belonged to the map, not the territory.

There is also a way to see this without changing coordinates: compute a measure of curvature whose value does not depend on the coordinates at all. One such measure for Schwarzschild's spacetime is the Kretschmann scalar, a single number at each point built from the full curvature:
\[
\mathcal{K} = \frac{48\,G^2M^2}{c^4 r^6}.
\]
It is finite at the horizon and becomes infinite only at the center $r = 0$, which is a genuine singularity. Figure~\ref{geom:fig-schwarzschild} compares the two.

A third trap arises in interpreting tangent vectors on manifolds. When first encountering tangent vectors on a surface, one often visualizes an arrow sticking out into the surrounding three-dimensional space. An abstract manifold, however, need not sit inside any larger space (it need not be \emph{embedded} in one). Differential geometry defines a tangent vector at a point $p$ intrinsically, in either of two equivalent ways:
\begin{itemize}
  \item As a \emph{class of curves}: two curves through $p$ count as the same tangent vector if, read in a chart (a local coordinate map, defined in the next section), they pass through $p$ with the same velocity. This groups curves into equivalence classes in the sense of Chapter~15 (Figure~\ref{geom:fig-curve-classes}).
  \item As a \emph{directional derivative operator}, called a \textbf{derivation}: a rule $v$ that takes a smooth function $f \colon M \to \R$ (one that has derivatives of all orders when written in coordinates) and returns a number $v(f)$, the rate at which $f$ changes as you move through $p$ along a curve $\gamma$ with $\gamma(0) = p$:
  \[
  v(f) = \left.\frac{d}{dt} f(\gamma(t))\right|_{t=0}.
  \]
\end{itemize}
Neither description mentions a surrounding space.

\subsection{Manifolds and the Atlas of Charts}

How can we do calculus on a curved space without ambient coordinates? The standard approach borrows the mapmakers' idea of an \textbf{atlas}.

You cannot flatten the entire surface of the Earth onto a single sheet of paper without tearing or distortion. But you can make a flat map of a single city or province. An atlas is a book of flat maps whose edges overlap. To navigate across the globe, you read one flat map until you near its border, then switch to the next map, which overlaps the first.

A \textbf{topological manifold} $M$ of dimension $n$ is a topological space that locally looks like ordinary flat Euclidean space $\R^n$. Around every point $p \in M$, there is an open neighborhood $U \subseteq M$ and a homeomorphism (a continuous bijection with a continuous inverse) onto an open subset $\widetilde{U}$ of $\R^n$:
\[
\phi \colon U \longrightarrow \widetilde{U} \subseteq \R^n.
\]
The pair $(U, \phi)$ is called a \textbf{chart}, or local coordinate map. Just as an atlas of the Earth consists of overlapping local maps of individual regions, an \textbf{atlas} on $M$ is a collection of charts $\{(U_\alpha, \phi_\alpha)\}$ whose domains cover all of $M$, so that $\bigcup_\alpha U_\alpha = M$. (The index $\alpha$ is a name tag telling the charts apart.)

\paragraph{A Concrete Example: Stereographic Projection of the Circle.}
Consider the unit circle in the plane,
\[
S^1 = \{(x, y) \in \R^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}.
\]
Can we cover the circle with a single coordinate chart mapping into $\R$? From Chapter~37, we know that $S^1$ is compact (closed and bounded in $\R^2$), while any non-empty open subset of $\R$ is non-compact. Because compactness is a topological property preserved under homeomorphisms, no single chart can ever cover all of $S^1$. We need at least two.

\emph{Stereographic projection} supplies them. It sends points of the circle to points of a line by drawing straight lines from a fixed point of the circle:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Chart 1 (North Pole Projection)}: Let $N = (0, 1)$ be the North pole. For any point $P = (x, y) \in S^1 \setminus \{N\}$, draw the straight line from $N$ through $P$ until it hits the horizontal axis $y = 0$. For a real number $t$, the points of this line are
  \[
  L(t) = N + t\,(P - N) = (0, 1) + t\,(x,\; y - 1) = \bigl(tx,\; 1 - t(1 - y)\bigr);
  \]
  the line passes through $N$ at $t = 0$ and through $P$ at $t = 1$. Its height $1 - t(1 - y)$ is zero when $t = 1/(1-y)$, and at that $t$ its first coordinate $tx$ gives the real coordinate
  \[
  u = \phi_1(x, y) = \frac{x}{1-y}.
  \]
  The domain is $U_1 = S^1 \setminus \{N\}$, covering every point of the circle except the North pole.
  \item \textbf{Chart 2 (South Pole Projection)}: Let $S = (0, -1)$ be the South pole. For any point $P = (x, y) \in S^1 \setminus \{S\}$, draw the straight line from $S$ through $P$ until it hits the horizontal axis $y = 0$. The same calculation, with the line $S + t\,(P - S) = \bigl(tx,\; -1 + t(1 + y)\bigr)$, reaches height $0$ when $t = 1/(1+y)$ and gives the coordinate
  \[
  v = \phi_2(x, y) = \frac{x}{1+y}.
  \]
  The domain is $U_2 = S^1 \setminus \{S\}$, covering every point except the South pole.
\end{enumerate}
Together, $U_1 \cup U_2 = S^1$. Every point on the circle has a home in at least one chart (Figure~\ref{geom:fig-stereographic}).

\subsection{Transition Maps: Where Calculus Lives}

When two charts $(U_\alpha, \phi_\alpha)$ and $(U_\beta, \phi_\beta)$ overlap, a point $p$ in $U_\alpha \cap U_\beta$ receives two competing coordinate descriptions, each a list of $n$ numbers: $x = \phi_\alpha(p)$ and $y = \phi_\beta(p)$.

The conversion between these descriptions is the \textbf{transition map}:
\[
\tau_{\alpha \beta} \;=\; \phi_\beta \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1} \colon \phi_\alpha(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta) \longrightarrow \phi_\beta(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta).
\]
Read it from right to left: start from the $\alpha$-coordinates $x$, use $\phi_\alpha^{-1}$ to go back to the point $p$ of $M$, then apply $\phi_\beta$ to get its $\beta$-coordinates $y$.

Let us compute the transition map for our circle charts. On the overlap $U_1 \cap U_2 = S^1 \setminus \{N, S\}$, every point has both a $u$-coordinate and a $v$-coordinate, and $x \neq 0$ (only the poles have $x = 0$). Because $(x, y)$ lies on the circle, $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, which means $1 - y^2 = x^2$. Multiplying the two coordinates:
\begin{align*}
u \cdot v &= \left(\frac{x}{1-y}\right) \left(\frac{x}{1+y}\right) \\
&= \frac{x^2}{1-y^2} \\
&= \frac{x^2}{x^2} \qquad \text{(since $1 - y^2 = x^2$ on the circle)} \\
&= 1.
\end{align*}
Hence $v = 1/u$.
The transition map converting $u$-coordinates to $v$-coordinates is $\tau_{12}(u) = 1/u$. Since $u = 0$ corresponds to the South pole (which is excluded from the overlap $U_1 \cap U_2$), this map is defined on $\R \setminus \{0\}$. The derivative of $1/u$ is $-1/u^2$, its second derivative is $2/u^3$, and derivatives of all orders exist. The transition map is infinitely differentiable, written $C^\infty$: derivatives of every order exist and are continuous. Figure~\ref{geom:fig-transition-graph} plots it.

\begin{formalbox}
A topological manifold $M$ is a \textbf{smooth manifold} if all its transition maps $\phi_\beta \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1}$ are $C^\infty$ smooth functions between open subsets of $\R^n$.
\end{formalbox}

Calculus on a manifold does not require an ambient embedding space. A function $f \colon M \to \R$ is defined to be smooth if its local representation $f \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1} \colon \phi_\alpha(U_\alpha) \to \R$ (the function $f$ written in the coordinates of chart $\alpha$) is smooth in the standard sense of multivariable calculus. This does not depend on which chart you use: on an overlap,
\[
f \circ \phi_\beta^{-1} = \bigl(f \circ \phi_\alpha^{-1}\bigr) \circ \bigl(\phi_\alpha \circ \phi_\beta^{-1}\bigr),
\]
and by the multivariable chain rule a composition of smooth functions is smooth. So if $f$ is smooth in chart $\alpha$, it is smooth in chart $\beta$ too.

\subsection{What Manifolds Achieve, and Where Smoothness Breaks Down}

The coordinate-free manifold perspective reshaped modern physics and mathematics:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{General Relativity}: The Einstein Field Equations,
  \[
  G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\, T_{\mu\nu},
  \]
  describe gravity without any ambient background space. On the left, $G_{\mu\nu}$ (the Einstein tensor) is built from the intrinsic curvature of four-dimensional spacetime; on the right, the stress-energy tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$ records the density and flow of the energy and momentum of matter and radiation. As before, $G$ is Newton's constant and $c$ the speed of light. This geometry predicted gravitational waves, black holes, and cosmological expansion.
  \item \textbf{Global Topology via Calculus}: Measurements made locally, with calculus, can detect the global shape of a manifold. De Rham's theorem says that the holes of a manifold can be counted by integrating \emph{differential forms}, the objects that get integrated along curves, over surfaces and over higher-dimensional pieces (for example $f\,dx + g\,dy$ integrated along a curve in the plane). In symbols, $H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \cong H^k(M; \R)$: the left side, built from forms and calculus, and the right side, built from topology alone, give the same count of $k$-dimensional holes. The Gauss--Bonnet theorem is a concrete case. For a closed surface (here \emph{closed} means compact with no boundary edge, like a sphere or a torus), integrating the Gaussian curvature $K$ over the whole surface gives $2\pi$ times the Euler characteristic $\chi(M) = V - E + F$ from Chapter~37:
  \[
  \int_M K \, dA = 2\pi \chi(M),
  \]
  where $dA$ stands for a small piece of area. For the unit sphere, $K = 1$ everywhere and the area is $4\pi$, so the integral is $4\pi = 2\pi \cdot 2$, matching $\chi = 2$. (The triangle of Figure~\ref{geom:fig-sphere-triangle} shows the same accounting on one eighth of the sphere: its extra angle $\pi/2$ equals $\int K\,dA$ over it.)
  \item \textbf{Gauge Theories in Particle Physics}: The Standard Model of particle physics is written in the language of fiber bundles. A \emph{fiber bundle} is a space built by attaching a copy of the same object to every point of a base space; here the base is spacetime and the attached object carries an internal symmetry group (a group in the sense of Chapter~21), such as $\mathrm{U}(1)$, the group of rotations of a circle, for electromagnetism, or $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ for the strong nuclear force. A \emph{connection} is a rule for comparing the copies at nearby points, and its curvature is what physicists measure as a force field.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
The smooth manifold framework has real limits:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Inability to Handle Physical Singularities}: Smooth manifolds require infinitely differentiable charts. They break down at physical singularities---such as the center of a black hole or the initial singularity of the Big Bang---where curvature grows without bound and spacetime ceases to be a smooth manifold.
  \item \textbf{Failure at Geometric Cusps and Self-Intersections}: Shapes defined by simple polynomial equations often have sharp points or self-crossings. The cuspidal cubic curve $y^2 = x^3$ has a sharp point (a \emph{cusp}) at the origin. It is still topologically a line (the map $t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)$ traces it once), but it is not smooth there: near the origin it is neither the graph of a differentiable function of $x$ (the two branches $y = \pm x^{3/2}$ give two values) nor of $y$ (the function $x = y^{2/3}$ has infinite slope at $y = 0$). The nodal curve $y^2 = x^2(x+1)$ crosses itself at the origin (a \emph{node}). Remove the crossing point from a small neighborhood of it and four separate pieces remain, whereas removing one point from an open interval leaves only two. So no neighborhood of the crossing is homeomorphic to an interval, and the curve is not even a topological manifold there. The tools of this chapter do not apply at such points.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mental check: Why one chart is never enough.} Explain why the 2-sphere $S^2 = \{(x, y, z) \in \R^3 : x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\}$ cannot be covered by a single chart $(U, \phi)$ with $U = S^2$ and $\phi(U)$ an open subset of $\R^2$. (Hint: Recall from Chapter~37 that $S^2$ is compact, but non-empty open subsets of $\R^2$ are never compact.)
  \item \textbf{Stereographic inversion.} In the stereographic projection of the circle $S^1$ from the North pole $N = (0, 1)$, we obtained the coordinate $u = x/(1-y)$ for any point $(x, y) \in S^1 \setminus \{N\}$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Invert this map algebraically to show that the point $(x, y)$ can be reconstructed from $u$ by the formulas
    \[
    x = \frac{2u}{u^2 + 1}, \qquad y = \frac{u^2 - 1}{u^2 + 1}.
    \]
    \item Verify directly that $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ for every real number $u \in \R$.
    \item What point on the circle corresponds to $u = 0$? What happens to $(x, y)$ as $u \to +\infty$ and as $u \to -\infty$?
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{When transition maps fail to be smooth.} Consider the real line $\R$ equipped with two charts: $(U_1, \phi_1)$ with $U_1 = \R$ and $\phi_1(x) = x$, and $(U_2, \phi_2)$ with $U_2 = \R$ and $\phi_2(x) = x^{1/3}$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Compute the transition map $\tau_{12} = \phi_2 \circ \phi_1^{-1}$ and its inverse $\tau_{21} = \phi_1 \circ \phi_2^{-1}$.
    \item Is $\tau_{12}$ differentiable at the origin $x = 0$?
    \item Does the collection of these two charts form a valid smooth atlas for $\R$? Explain why or why not.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Tangent vectors as derivations.} Let $M$ be a smooth manifold and $p \in M$, and write $C^\infty(M)$ for the set of smooth functions $M \to \R$. A derivation at $p$ is a linear map $v \colon C^\infty(M) \to \R$ (linear means $v(af + bg) = a\,v(f) + b\,v(g)$ for all numbers $a, b$) that satisfies the Leibniz product rule:
  \[
  v(fg) = f(p)\,v(g) + g(p)\,v(f) \quad \text{for all } f, g \in C^\infty(M).
  \]
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Prove that if $c \in \R$ is a constant function on $M$, then $v(c) = 0$. (Hint: Apply the product rule to the function $1 = 1 \cdot 1$, and then use linearity for $c = c \cdot 1$.)
    \item Explain in one intuitive paragraph why this algebraic condition captures the ordinary product rule of calculus without needing coordinate axes.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Intrinsic curvature: Cylinder versus sphere.} An ant crawls on a cylinder of radius $R$; another crawls on a sphere of radius $R$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item If you slit a hollow cylinder along a straight line parallel to its axis, you can unroll it completely flat onto a tabletop without stretching, tearing, or wrinkling. According to Gauss's \emph{Theorema Egregium}, what is the Gaussian curvature $K$ of the cylinder?
    \item Can you press a spherical orange peel flat onto a tabletop without tearing or squishing it? What does this imply about the Gaussian curvature of the sphere?
  \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
