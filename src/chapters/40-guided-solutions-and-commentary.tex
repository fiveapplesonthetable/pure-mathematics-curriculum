\section{Guided Solutions and Commentary}
\chapterline

A finished proof in a textbook often pulls key choices out of a hat---setting $\delta = \eps/3$, adding and subtracting a clever middle term, or picking exactly the right assumption to contradict. It is natural to wonder how anyone thought of those steps in the first place.

The process of discovering a proof usually proceeds in a different order from its presentation: testing small cases, drawing diagrams, and working backwards from the goal on scratch paper. Only after discovering the key relationship do you write down the forward deduction. This chapter separates proof construction into two distinct stages: the \textbf{discovery track} (exploratory search working backwards from definitions) and the \textbf{presentation track} (the forward deductive chain that certifies the result).

\begin{historybox}
The tension between how mathematics is discovered and how it is published is one of the oldest debates in the subject, captured by the contrasting styles of Leonhard Euler and Carl Friedrich Gauss.

In 1735 Leonhard Euler found the exact sum $1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + \dotsb = \pi^2/6$ (the Basel problem). He first added up terms numerically to twenty decimal places. He then took the sine function, which is zero at $0, \pm \pi, \pm 2\pi, \dots$, and factored it from those zeros as one factors a polynomial from its roots: $\sin x = x(1 - x^2/\pi^2)(1 - x^2/4\pi^2) \dotsm$. He published that exploratory scratchwork openly. More than a century passed before Karl Weierstrass supplied the rigorous analysis needed to justify such infinite products.

Carl Friedrich Gauss took the opposite approach in the early 1800s, adopting the motto \emph{Pauca sed matura} (``Few, but ripe'') and remarking: ``When a fine building is completed, the scaffolding should no longer be visible.'' In his 1801 treatise \emph{Disquisitiones Arithmeticae}, Gauss systematically removed scratchwork, false starts, and intermediate explorations, presenting only polished deductive proofs.

His contemporaries complained. In 1826, the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel remarked to Bernt Michael Holmboe that Gauss was like the fox who erases his footprints in the sand with his tail, leaving readers unable to learn how he discovered his theorems. Carl Jacobi made similar complaints. A century and a half later, in 1976, the philosopher Imre Lakatos's \emph{Proofs and Refutations} appeared in book form (after his death in 1974), arguing that mathematics grows through a back-and-forth of conjectures, counterexamples, and repaired proofs rather than through a straight line of deductions.

Philosophers now name the two activities separately: the \textbf{context of discovery} (rules of thumb, guesses and experiments) and the \textbf{context of justification} (rigorous proof). Computers have made the split sharper. Proof assistants---computer programs such as Lean or Coq that check every step of a proof all the way down to the axioms---can confirm a human proof exactly. Getting a computer to turn rough human scratchwork into such a checked proof, or to find proofs that explain \emph{why} a theorem is true, is still an open research problem.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Common Traps Between Scratchpad and Final Proof}

Because textbooks usually show only finished arguments, three habits trip up almost every beginner:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Myth of Proofs Found in Order}: Because published proofs run forward, learners often assume that proofs are found in the order they are written, and feel like failures when theirs are not. A written proof is a record of the path that worked, tidied up afterwards, not a record of the search.
  \item \textbf{The Backward Deduction Fallacy}: The most damaging beginner error is copying backwards scratchwork straight into the final proof. Take the limit $\lim_{x \to 2} 3x = 6$, proved by the $\eps$-$\delta$ method of Chapter~20, so $f(x) = 3x$ and $L = 6$. If you write:
  \[
  |f(x) - L| < \eps \implies |3x - 6| < \eps \implies 3|x - 2| < \eps \implies |x - 2| < \frac{\eps}{3},
  \]
  and end with ``Choose $\delta = \eps/3$,'' you have reversed the direction of logical flow: this chain starts by assuming the conclusion you are supposed to prove. In a forward proof, the hypothesis $|x - 2| < \delta$ must be the starting premise that drives forward to the conclusion $|f(x) - L| < \eps$. Figure~\ref{guide:fig-backward-forward} sets the two chains side by side, and Figure~\ref{guide:fig-eps-delta-graph} shows on the graph what the choice $\delta = \eps/3$ achieves.
  \item \textbf{Premature Formalization}: Writing formal symbolic lines before you have a mental picture or have checked simple cases usually produces symbol-pushing that goes nowhere. If the argument is unclear in plain language, adding quantifiers will not close the gap.
\end{itemize}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Before reading further, find the error in this common line from a student draft:
\[
\text{``We want to prove } P \implies Q\text{. Assume } Q \text{ is true\dots''}
\]
What has gone wrong? If you assume the target $Q$, you have assumed the very conclusion you were asked to establish. In an implication $P \implies Q$, you must assume the premise $P$ and deduce $Q$. Starting with $Q$ is circular reasoning (begging the question).
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Problem 1: Direct Proof and Parity}

Our first problem concerns \textbf{parity}---the basic distinction between whether an integer is even ($2k$ for some integer $k$) or odd ($2m + 1$ for some integer $m$).

\begin{theorem}
The sum of two odd integers is an even integer.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{The Mental Model \& False Starts.}
A common beginner impulse is to test examples: $3 + 5 = 8$, $7 + 11 = 18$. Examples build intuition, but they cannot prove an infinite claim about all possible pairs of odd numbers. 

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The Variable Collision Trap}: Watch out for the temptation to write:
\[
\text{``Let $a = 2k + 1$ and $b = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$.''}
\]
By using the same variable symbol $k$ for both numbers, you have accidentally forced $a$ and $b$ to be the identical number. You would only prove that $a + a = 2a$ is even, not that the sum of \emph{any} two independent odd numbers is even. Always give independent arbitrary objects independent variable names ($a = 2m + 1$ and $b = 2n + 1$).
\end{trapbox}

\paragraph{The Scratchpad.}
We need $a + b$ to look like $2 \cdot (\text{integer})$. Since $a$ is odd, $a = 2m + 1$. Since $b$ is odd, $b = 2n + 1$ for some integers $m, n$. Adding them gives $2m + 2n + 2 = 2(m + n + 1)$. The integers are \textbf{closed under addition}: adding two integers always gives an integer, never something outside the set (such as a fraction). So $m + n + 1$ is an integer, and $a + b$ has the required shape.

Figure~\ref{guide:fig-parity-pairing} draws this as dots: each odd integer is some pairs of dots plus one unpaired dot. Putting two odd integers together keeps all their pairs and makes one new pair from the two leftover dots, so nothing is left over.

With this picture in mind, the formal proof translates the pairing into algebra.

\begin{proof}
Let $a$ and $b$ be arbitrary odd integers. By definition of odd integers, there exist integers $m, n \in \Z$ such that
\[
a = 2m + 1 \quad \text{and} \quad b = 2n + 1.
\]
We compute the sum:
\begin{align*}
a + b &= (2m + 1) + (2n + 1) \\
&= 2m + 2n + 2 \\
&= 2(m + n + 1).
\end{align*}
Let $k = m + n + 1$. Since $m, n, 1 \in \Z$ and a sum of integers is an integer, $k \in \Z$. Thus $a + b = 2k$ with $k \in \Z$, which satisfies the definition of an even integer. Since $a$ and $b$ were arbitrary, the sum of any two odd integers is even.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Problem 2: Unwinding Quantifiers in Surjections}

Our second problem involves \textbf{surjectivity}---the property that a function's arrows hit every element of its target set (no element of the codomain is left without an incoming arrow)---and \textbf{function composition} ($g \circ f$), where two functions run in series, with $f$ acting first and $g$ acting on its output.

\begin{theorem}
If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are surjective functions, then the composition $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{The Mental Model \& False Starts.}
A beginner often starts by picking an element $a \in A$ and pushing forward: $f(a) \in B$, and then $g(f(a)) \in C$. But surjectivity is a promise about the \emph{target} set $C$, not the source set $A$. The definition states: for every $c \in C$, there exists some input $a \in A$ mapping to it. Starting at $A$ moves in the wrong direction; it proves only that $g \circ f$ maps $A$ into $C$, but gives no guarantee that every element of $C$ is hit.

The right move is to \emph{pull back}: start from an output and find an input that produces it. Figure~\ref{guide:fig-surj-flows} shows the two directions. The search pulls back from $C$ to $B$ to $A$; the written proof then runs forward from $A$ to $C$.

\paragraph{The Scratchpad.}
We must start with an arbitrary target $c \in C$. We need to locate some witness $a \in A$ satisfying $(g \circ f)(a) = c$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item What map touches $C$? The function $g: B \to C$. Because $g$ is surjective, every element in $C$ has at least one preimage in $B$. Thus, there exists some $b \in B$ with $g(b) = c$.
  \item Now we have $b \in B$. What map touches $B$? The function $f: A \to B$. Because $f$ is surjective, there exists some $a \in A$ with $f(a) = b$.
  \item Putting them together: $(g \circ f)(a) = g(f(a)) = g(b) = c$. The witness $a \in A$ has been discovered.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proof}
Let $c \in C$ be arbitrary. We must exhibit an element $a \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(a) = c$.

Because $g: B \to C$ is surjective, there exists an element $b \in B$ such that $g(b) = c$. 

Next, because $f: A \to B$ is surjective and $b \in B$, there exists an element $a \in A$ such that $f(a) = b$.

Then
\begin{align*}
(g \circ f)(a) &= g(f(a)) && \text{(definition of composition)}\\
&= g(b) && \text{(since $f(a) = b$)}\\
&= c && \text{(since $g(b) = c$).}
\end{align*}
Since $c \in C$ was arbitrary, we have demonstrated that every element of $C$ possesses a preimage in $A$ under $g \circ f$. Therefore $g \circ f$ is surjective.
\end{proof}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Compare this with injectivity. If you were tasked with proving that $g \circ f$ is \emph{injective} (meaning distinct inputs map to distinct outputs, or equivalently $(g \circ f)(a_1) = (g \circ f)(a_2) \implies a_1 = a_2$), what would be your starting premise? Here, you would assume $g(f(a_1)) = g(f(a_2))$ and peel off one function at a time, outer one first:
\begin{align*}
g(f(a_1)) &= g(f(a_2)) && \text{(the assumption)}\\
f(a_1) &= f(a_2) && \text{(because $g$ is injective)}\\
a_1 &= a_2 && \text{(because $f$ is injective).}
\end{align*}
Figure~\ref{guide:fig-inj-composition} shows the same fact as a picture: two different starting points stay different after each map. Surjectivity pulls preimages backward; injectivity unwraps function applications inward.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Problem 3: Type Discipline in Power Sets}

Our third problem requires \textbf{type discipline}---the mental habit of tracking what kind of mathematical object you are handling (an individual element, a set of elements, or a collection of sets). We deal with the \textbf{power set} $\pow(A)$, which is the set of all possible subsets of $A$. For example, if $A = \{1, 2\}$, its power set contains four elements: $\pow(A) = \{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2\}, \{1, 2\}\}$. Every element of $\pow(A)$ is itself a set.

\begin{theorem}
For any sets $A$ and $B$, if $A \subseteq B$, then $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$.
\end{theorem}

Figure~\ref{guide:fig-powerset-small} checks a small case first: with $A = \{1, 2\}$ and $B = \{1, 2, 3\}$, each of the four subsets of $A$ appears among the eight subsets of $B$.

\paragraph{The Mental Model \& False Starts.}
This problem traps beginners who lose track of types. A student will often write:
\[
\text{``Let $x \in A$. Since $A \subseteq B$, we have $x \in B$. Therefore $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$.''}
\]
See where the argument goes wrong. The premise $A \subseteq B$ was already given as an assumption; re-proving that $x \in A \implies x \in B$ establishes only what we were already told. It does not prove that $\pow(A)$ is a subset of $\pow(B)$. 

To prove that one set is a subset of another, you must pick an arbitrary element of the first set and prove it belongs to the second. Here, the first set is $\pow(A)$. An element of $\pow(A)$ is an entire subset $S \subseteq A$, rather than a point $x$.

Figure~\ref{guide:fig-type-hierarchy} stacks the three kinds of object in levels. Membership $\in$ connects an object to something one level up (a point is \emph{in} a set; a set is \emph{in} a power set), while inclusion $\subseteq$ compares two objects on the same level (two sets).

\paragraph{The Scratchpad.}
Write the Given shelf and the Goal shelf (Chapter~25) side by side:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Given}: $A \subseteq B$ (meaning for every element $x$, if $x \in A$, then $x \in B$).
  \item \textbf{Target}: $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$.
  \item \textbf{Unpack the target}: Let $S \in \pow(A)$ be arbitrary. We must show $S \in \pow(B)$.
  \item \textbf{Unpack the definition of power set}: 
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $S \in \pow(A)$ means $S \subseteq A$.
    \item $S \in \pow(B)$ means $S \subseteq B$.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The inner bridge}: We now have $S \subseteq A$ and $A \subseteq B$, and we want to prove $S \subseteq B$. To prove $S \subseteq B$, choose an arbitrary element $x \in S$. Because $S \subseteq A$, $x \in A$. Because $A \subseteq B$, $x \in B$. Since every $x \in S$ lands in $B$, we have $S \subseteq B$.
  \item \textbf{Pack back into the target type}: Since $S \subseteq B$, it follows that $S \in \pow(B)$. The chain is complete.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proof}
Assume $A \subseteq B$. To prove that $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$, let $S \in \pow(A)$ be arbitrary.

By definition of the power set, $S \in \pow(A)$ means that $S \subseteq A$.

We must show that $S \in \pow(B)$, which by definition requires showing that $S \subseteq B$.

Let $x \in S$ be arbitrary. Since $S \subseteq A$, we have $x \in A$. Next, because $A \subseteq B$ and $x \in A$, it follows that $x \in B$.

Because every element $x \in S$ is an element of $B$, we have established that $S \subseteq B$.

By definition of the power set, $S \subseteq B$ implies $S \in \pow(B)$. Since $S \in \pow(A)$ was arbitrary, we conclude that $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Problem 4: Proof by Contradiction and Density}

Our fourth problem uses the \textbf{density} of the real numbers: between any two different real numbers there is always another real number (for example, their average). Here we apply it between $0$ and a positive number.

\begin{theorem}
There is no smallest positive real number.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{The Mental Model \& False Starts.}
Why can't there be a smallest positive number? If someone claims that $0.0001$ is the smallest positive number, cut it in half: $0.00005$ is still positive, but smaller. If they name an even smaller number, cut that one in half too.

A common false start is asserting that ``zero is the smallest positive number.'' But zero is not positive: positive means strictly greater than zero ($x > 0$). Another false instinct is trying to name an infinitely small number, like ``$0.000\dots1$'' (infinitely many zeros, then a $1$). The real numbers contain no such infinitesimals (Chapter~20); the expression $0.000\dots1$ does not name any real number.

\paragraph{The Scratchpad.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Setup for contradiction}: Assume the claim is false. That means there \emph{does} exist a smallest positive real number, which we will name $r$.
  \item \textbf{Unpack what that means}:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $r > 0$ (it is positive), and
    \item For every real number $x > 0$, we have $r \le x$ (it is smaller than or equal to all positive reals).
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hunt for the counter-witness}: Can we construct a positive number $s$ with $s < r$?
  Try halving $r$: let $s = r/2$.
  \item \textbf{Check the properties of $s$}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Since $r > 0$, dividing by $2$ gives $s = r/2 > 0$, so $s$ is positive.
    \item Since $r/2 > 0$, adding $r/2$ to both sides of that inequality gives
    \[
    r = \frac{r}{2} + \frac{r}{2} > 0 + \frac{r}{2} = \frac{r}{2},
    \]
    so $s < r$.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The collision}: Condition (2) claimed that $r \le x$ for \emph{every} positive number $x$. Since $s$ is a positive number, condition (2) insists that $r \le s$. But our arithmetic showed $s < r$, which implies $r \le s < r$, so $r < r$. A number cannot be strictly less than itself.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{guide:fig-density-contradiction} shows the contradiction on the number line. If $r$ were the smallest positive real, the open interval $(0, r)$ (all numbers strictly between $0$ and $r$) would contain no real numbers at all, yet $s = r/2$ lies inside it.

\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a smallest positive real number. Call this number $r$.

By this assumption, $r$ satisfies two properties:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $r > 0$, and
  \item For every real number $x > 0$, we have $r \le x$.
\end{enumerate}
Now consider the candidate number $s = r/2$.

Because $r > 0$, dividing by the positive integer $2$ preserves the inequality, giving $s = r/2 > 0$. Thus $s$ is a positive real number.

At the same time, because $r > 0$, we have $r/2 < r$, which means $s < r$.

This contradicts condition (2), which asserted that $r$ is less than or equal to every positive real number, including $s$.

Because our assumption led to a contradiction, the assumption must be false. Therefore, there is no smallest positive real number.
\end{proof}

\subsection{What Two-Track Practice Builds---and Its Limits}

Making the two tracks of proof visible gives you three practical capabilities:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Proof Debugging}: You can diagnose exactly where an argument fails: did you collide variable names? Did you confuse an element with a subset? Did you reverse the direction of an implication?
  \item \textbf{Systematic Problem Solving}: When faced with an unfamiliar statement, you do not need an immediate breakthrough. Identifying the hypotheses and conclusion, unpacking definitions into precise logical statements, and analyzing the required structure reveals the steps needed.
  \item \textbf{Checkable Proofs}: Because the exploration stays on scrap paper and only the forward deduction is written up, a reader can check your proof line by line.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
The method has limits:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Templates Do Not Solve Research Problems}: Standard proof patterns work well when the result follows fairly directly from the definitions. On open research problems they often stall. Progress there often needs a new idea that seems foreign to the original problem---such as square roots of negative numbers, which sixteenth-century mathematicians needed to solve certain cubic equations even when all the answers were real, or coordinate axes, which turned Euclid's geometry problems into algebra.
  \item \textbf{Deductive Rules Do Not Produce Conjectures}: Logic checks whether a claim follows; it does not suggest which claims to try. As Henri Poincar\'e observed, logic is the instrument of proof, but intuition is the instrument of invention.
\end{itemize}

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Claim}: If $n$ is an integer such that $3n + 2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd. \\

    \begin{align*}
    3n + 2 &= 3(2k + 1) + 2\\
    &= 6k + 5\\
    &= 2(3k + 2) + 1,
    \end{align*}
    which is odd. Therefore, the claim is proven.''
    \item \emph{Claim}: The sum of any two rational numbers is rational. \\
    \emph{Student proof fragment}: ``Let $q_1$ and $q_2$ be rational numbers. Then $q_1 = a/b$ and $q_2 = a/b$ for integers $a, b$ with $b \neq 0$. Adding them gives $q_1 + q_2 = 2a/b$, which is a ratio of integers, so it is rational.''
    \item \emph{Claim}: If $A \subseteq B$, then $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$. \\
    \emph{Student proof fragment}: ``Let $x \in \pow(A)$. Since $A \subseteq B$, every element of $A$ is in $B$. Therefore $x \in B$, so $x \in \pow(B)$.''
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Routine Practice (Two-Track Proofs)}. For each of the following statements, write out both the scratchpad discovery work (unpacking definitions and identifying the candidate) and the polished forward proof.
  \begin{enumerate}

    \item Prove that for any two sets $A$ and $B$,
    \[
    \pow(A \cap B) = \pow(A) \cap \pow(B).

    \item Prove that the product of any two odd integers is an odd integer.

    \lim_{x \to 3} (4x - 5) = 7.
    \]
    Show your scratchpad algebra working backwards to find $\delta$ in terms of $\eps$, identify the potential backward deduction trap, and then write down the polished forward proof.

  \end{enumerate}
