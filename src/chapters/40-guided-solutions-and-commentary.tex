\section{Guided Solutions and Commentary}
\chapterline

A finished proof in a textbook often pulls key choices out of a hat---setting $\delta = \eps/3$, adding and subtracting a clever middle term, or picking just the right contradiction hypothesis. It is natural to wonder how anyone thought of those steps in the first place.

The process of discovering a proof usually proceeds in a different order from its presentation: testing small cases, drawing diagrams, and working backwards from the goal on scratch paper. Only after discovering the key relationship do you write down the forward deduction. This chapter separates proof construction into two distinct stages: the \textbf{discovery track} (exploratory search working backwards from definitions) and the \textbf{presentation track} (the forward deductive chain that certifies the result).

\begin{historybox}
The tension between how mathematics is discovered and how it is published is one of the oldest debates in the subject, captured by the contrasting styles of Leonhard Euler and Carl Friedrich Gauss.

In the 1730s, Leonhard Euler was remarkably candid. In his memoirs, he invited the reader directly into his workshop. In solving the famous Basel problem in 1735---finding the exact sum of the infinite series $1 + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{16} + \dots = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$---Euler calculated sums to twenty decimal places, treated an infinite polynomial with roots at $\pm \pi, \pm 2\pi, \dots$ like a high school quadratic, and documented his intuitive leaps long before Karl Weierstrass (the nineteenth-century mathematician who supplied calculus with its rigorous foundation) developed the formal theory of infinite product expansions (factoring an infinite series into a product of terms from its roots).

Carl Friedrich Gauss took the opposite approach in the early 1800s. Gauss was obsessed with perfection, adopting the motto \emph{Pauca sed matura} (``Few, but ripe''). He famously stated: ``When a fine building is completed, the scaffolding should no longer be visible.'' In his groundbreaking 1801 treatise \emph{Disquisitiones Arithmeticae}, Gauss systematically removed scratchwork, false starts, and intermediate explorations, presenting only polished deductive proofs.

This sparked commentary among his contemporaries. In 1826, the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel remarked to Bernt Michael Holmboe that Gauss was like the fox who erases his footprints in the sand with his tail, leaving readers unable to learn how he discovered his theorems. Carl Jacobi made similar complaints. A century and a half later, in 1976, philosopher Imre Lakatos published \emph{Proofs and Refutations}, arguing that mathematics grows not through immaculate deductive cascades, but through a dialogue of conjectures, counterexamples, and patched proofs.

This historical struggle clarified an essential distinction: the \textbf{context of discovery} (heuristics and experimentation) and the \textbf{context of justification} (rigorous proof) are different activities. An active area of research is automated proof synthesis. Modern interactive proof assistants---computer programs like Lean or Coq that verify every deduction down to basic axioms---can check human proofs with absolute precision. Yet teaching computers to translate human intuitive scratchwork into verified code, or to automatically generate proofs that explain \emph{why} a theorem is true rather than merely that it holds, remains an active research challenge in computer science and mathematical logic.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Common Traps Between Scratchpad and Final Proof}

Because textbooks usually show only finished arguments, three habits trip up almost every beginner:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Myth of the Effortless Deduction}: Because published proofs present deductions in forward sequence, learners often assume that proofs are found in the same order they are written. In practice, discovery involves testing examples, adjusting definitions, and working backwards on scratch paper. A proof is not a record of the thinking process; it is a structured argument established after an exploratory path has been verified.
  \item \textbf{The Backward Deduction Fallacy}: The single most dangerous beginner error is writing backwards scratchwork directly into the final proof. If you write:
  \[
  |f(x) - L| < \eps \impliedby |3x - 6| < \eps \impliedby 3|x - 2| < \eps \impliedby |x - 2| < \frac{\eps}{3},
  \]
  and end with ``Choose $\delta = \eps/3$,'' you have reversed the direction of logical flow. In a forward proof, the hypothesis $|x - 2| < \delta$ must be the starting premise that drives forward to the conclusion $|f(x) - L| < \eps$.
  \item \textbf{Premature Formalization}: Attempting to write formal symbolic lines before developing an intuitive mental model or checking simple cases often leads to mechanical manipulation without insight. If the underlying argument is unclear in plain language, adding formal quantifiers will not resolve the logical gap.
\end{itemize}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Before reading further, test your diagnostic instinct on this common line from a student draft:
\[
\text{``We want to prove } P \implies Q\text{. Assume } Q \text{ is true\dots''}
\]
What fatal error has occurred here? If you assume the target $Q$, you have assumed the very conclusion you were tasked with establishing! In an implication $P \implies Q$, you must assume the premise $P$ and deduce $Q$. Starting with $Q$ is the logical fallacy of affirming the consequent.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Problem 1: Direct Proof and Parity}

Our first problem concerns \textbf{parity}---the basic distinction between whether an integer is even ($2k$ for some integer $k$) or odd ($2m + 1$ for some integer $m$).

\begin{theorem}
The sum of two odd integers is an even integer.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{The Mental Model \& False Starts.}
A common beginner impulse is to test examples: $3 + 5 = 8$, $7 + 11 = 18$. Testing examples is essential for intuition, but numerical examples cannot certify an infinite claim about all possible pairs of odd numbers. 

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The Variable Collision Trap}: Watch out for the temptation to write:
\[
\text{``Let $a = 2k + 1$ and $b = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$.''}
\]
By using the same variable symbol $k$ for both numbers, you have accidentally forced $a$ and $b$ to be the identical number! You would only prove that $a + a = 2a$ is even, not that the sum of \emph{any} two independent odd numbers is even. Always give independent arbitrary objects independent variable names ($a = 2m + 1$ and $b = 2n + 1$).
\end{trapbox}

\paragraph{The Scratchpad.}
We need $a + b$ to look like $2 \cdot (\text{integer})$. Since $a$ is odd, $a = 2m + 1$. Since $b$ is odd, $b = 2n + 1$ for some integers $m, n$. Adding them gives $2m + 2n + 2 = 2(m + n + 1)$. Because integers are closed under addition, $m + n + 1$ is an integer. The bridge is complete.

Figure~\ref{guide:fig-parity-pairing} illustrates this pairing visually: each odd integer consists of pairs of dots with one unpaired dot. Bringing two odd integers together combines their pairs and pairs the two remaining dots, leaving no remainder.

With this picture in mind, the formal proof translates the pairing into algebra.

\begin{proof}
Let $a$ and $b$ be arbitrary odd integers. By definition of odd integers, there exist integers $m, n \in \Z$ such that
\[
a = 2m + 1 \quad \text{and} \quad b = 2n + 1.
\]
We compute the sum:
\begin{align*}
a + b &= (2m + 1) + (2n + 1) \\
&= 2m + 2n + 2 \\
&= 2(m + n + 1).
\end{align*}
Let $k = m + n + 1$. Since $m, n, 1 \in \Z$ and the integers are closed under addition, $k \in \Z$. Thus $a + b = 2k$ with $k \in \Z$, which satisfies the definition of an even integer. Since $a$ and $b$ were arbitrary, the sum of any two odd integers is even.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Problem 2: Unwinding Quantifiers in Surjections}

Our second problem involves \textbf{surjectivity}---the property that a function's arrows hit every single element in its target set (no elements in the codomain are left without an incoming arrow)---and \textbf{function composition} ($g \circ f$), where two functions run in series, with $f$ acting first and $g$ acting on its output.

\begin{theorem}
If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are surjective functions, then the composition $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{The Mental Model \& False Starts.}
A beginner often starts by picking an element $a \in A$ and pushing forward: $f(a) \in B$, and then $g(f(a)) \in C$. But surjectivity is a promise about the \emph{target} set $C$, not the source set $A$! The definition states: for every $c \in C$, there exists some input $a \in A$ mapping to it. Starting at $A$ moves in the wrong direction; it proves only that $g \circ f$ maps $A$ into $C$, but gives no guarantee that every dot in $C$ is actually hit.

\paragraph{The Scratchpad.}
We must start with an arbitrary target $c \in C$. We need to locate some witness $a \in A$ satisfying $(g \circ f)(a) = c$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item What map touches $C$? The function $g: B \to C$. Because $g$ is surjective, every element in $C$ has at least one preimage in $B$. Thus, there exists some $b \in B$ with $g(b) = c$.
  \item Now we have $b \in B$. What map touches $B$? The function $f: A \to B$. Because $f$ is surjective, there exists some $a \in A$ with $f(a) = b$.
  \item Putting them together: $(g \circ f)(a) = g(f(a)) = g(b) = c$. The witness $a \in A$ has been discovered.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proof}
Let $c \in C$ be arbitrary. We must exhibit an element $a \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(a) = c$.

Because $g: B \to C$ is surjective, there exists an element $b \in B$ such that $g(b) = c$. 

Next, because $f: A \to B$ is surjective and $b \in B$, there exists an element $a \in A$ such that $f(a) = b$.

Applying the definition of function composition:
\[
(g \circ f)(a) = g(f(a)) = g(b) = c.
\]
Since $c \in C$ was arbitrary, we have demonstrated that every element of $C$ possesses a preimage in $A$ under $g \circ f$. Therefore $g \circ f$ is surjective.
\end{proof}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Notice the contrast with injectivity. If you were tasked with proving that $g \circ f$ is \emph{injective} (meaning distinct inputs map to distinct outputs, or equivalently $(g \circ f)(a_1) = (g \circ f)(a_2) \implies a_1 = a_2$), what would be your starting premise? Here, you would assume $g(f(a_1)) = g(f(a_2))$, peel off the outer function $g$ using its injectivity to get $f(a_1) = f(a_2)$, and peel off $f$ to conclude $a_1 = a_2$. Surjectivity pulls preimages backward; injectivity unwraps function applications inward.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Problem 3: Type Discipline in Power Sets}

Our third problem requires \textbf{type discipline}---the mental habit of tracking what kind of mathematical object you are handling (an individual element, a set of elements, or a collection of sets). We deal with the \textbf{power set} $\pow(A)$, which is the set of all possible subsets of $A$. For example, if $A = \{1, 2\}$, its power set contains four elements: $\pow(A) = \{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2\}, \{1, 2\}\}$. Notice that every element of $\pow(A)$ is itself a set.

\begin{theorem}
For any sets $A$ and $B$, if $A \subseteq B$, then $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{The Mental Model \& False Starts.}
This problem is a classic trap for beginners who lose track of types. A student will frequently write:
\[
\text{``Let $x \in A$. Since $A \subseteq B$, we have $x \in B$. Therefore $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$.''}
\]
Look closely at what went wrong. The premise $A \subseteq B$ was already given as an assumption; re-proving that $x \in A \implies x \in B$ establishes only what we were already told! It does not prove that $\pow(A)$ is a subset of $\pow(B)$. 

To prove that one set is a subset of another, you must pick an arbitrary element of the first set and prove it belongs to the second. Here, the first set is $\pow(A)$. An element of $\pow(A)$ is not a point $x$; it is an entire subset $S \subseteq A$.

    \textbf{3. Apply Premise:} Since $A \subseteq B \implies S \subseteq B$ \hfill {\scriptsize\color{gray!80!black}(Level 1)}\\[2pt]
    \textbf{4. Pack to Target Type:} Therefore $S \in \pow(B)$ \hfill {\scriptsize\color{gray!80!black}(Level 1 $\to$ 2)}\\[4pt]
    \textbf{Conclusion:} $S \in \pow(A) \implies S \in \pow(B)$, so $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$ \checkmark
  };

  % Bottom error warning
  \node[trapbox, text width=7.0cm] (err) at (9.3, -0.2) {
    \textbf{Fatal Type Collision:} Writing ``Let $x \in A$\dots'' operates at Level 0 (elements), completely bypassing the subset $S$, and merely re-states the given premise $A \subseteq B$.
  };
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{The Tower of Types and the power set inclusion proof. Mathematical objects inhabit strictly separated levels: elements (Level 0), subsets (Level 1), and power sets (Level 2). Membership ($\in$) ascends one level, while subset inclusion ($\subseteq$) compares objects on the same level. Proving $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$ requires picking an arbitrary element $S \in \pow(A)$ (Level 1 subset), not an element $x \in A$ (Level 0 point).}\label{guide:fig-type-hierarchy}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{guide:fig-type-hierarchy}, the proof succeeds by picking an arbitrary element $S \in \pow(A)$ at Level 1, unpacking it to $S \subseteq A$, transferring along $A \subseteq B$, and repacking it into $S \in \pow(B)$.

\paragraph{The Scratchpad.}
Let us align the given shelf and the goal shelf:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Given}: $A \subseteq B$ (meaning for every element $x$, if $x \in A$, then $x \in B$).
  \item \textbf{Target}: $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$.
  \item \textbf{Unpack the target}: Let $S \in \pow(A)$ be arbitrary. We must show $S \in \pow(B)$.
  \item \textbf{Unpack the definition of power set}: 
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $S \in \pow(A)$ means $S \subseteq A$.
    \item $S \in \pow(B)$ means $S \subseteq B$.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The inner bridge}: We now have $S \subseteq A$ and $A \subseteq B$, and we want to prove $S \subseteq B$. To prove $S \subseteq B$, choose an arbitrary element $x \in S$. Because $S \subseteq A$, $x \in A$. Because $A \subseteq B$, $x \in B$. Since every $x \in S$ lands in $B$, we have $S \subseteq B$.
  \item \textbf{Pack back into the target type}: Since $S \subseteq B$, it follows that $S \in \pow(B)$. The chain is complete.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proof}
Assume $A \subseteq B$. To prove that $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$, let $S \in \pow(A)$ be arbitrary.

By definition of the power set, $S \in \pow(A)$ means that $S \subseteq A$.

We must show that $S \in \pow(B)$, which by definition requires showing that $S \subseteq B$.

Let $x \in S$ be arbitrary. Since $S \subseteq A$, we have $x \in A$. Next, because $A \subseteq B$ and $x \in A$, it follows that $x \in B$.

Because every element $x \in S$ is an element of $B$, we have established that $S \subseteq B$.

By definition of the power set, $S \subseteq B$ implies $S \in \pow(B)$. Since $S \in \pow(A)$ was arbitrary, we conclude that $\pow(A) \subseteq \pow(B)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Problem 4: Proof by Contradiction and Density}

Our fourth problem explores the \textbf{density} of the real numbers---the geometric property that between any two distinct numbers on the number line, you can always slip in another number. There are no gaps or isolated stepping stones in $\R$.

\begin{theorem}
There is no smallest positive real number.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{The Mental Model \& False Starts.}
Why can't there be a smallest positive number? If someone claims that $0.0001$ is the smallest positive number, you immediately cut it in half: $0.00005$ is still positive, but strictly smaller. If they name an even smaller number, you cut that one in half too. 

A common false start is asserting that ``zero is the smallest positive number.'' But zero is not positive! Positive means strictly greater than zero ($x > 0$). Another false instinct is trying to name an infinitely small number, like ``$0.000\dots1$.'' Standard real numbers do not contain such infinitesimals; every real number has a definite numerical value.

\paragraph{The Scratchpad.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Setup for contradiction}: Assume the claim is false. That means there \emph{does} exist a smallest positive real number, which we will name $r$.
  \item \textbf{Unpack what that means}:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $r > 0$ (it is positive), and
    \item For every real number $x > 0$, we have $r \le x$ (it is smaller than or equal to all positive reals).
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hunt for the counter-witness}: Can we construct a positive number $s$ with $s < r$?
  Try halving $r$: let $s = \frac{r}{2}$.
  \item \textbf{Check the properties of $s$}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Since $r > 0$, dividing by $2$ gives $s = \frac{r}{2} > 0$, so $s$ is positive.
    \item Since $r > 0$, subtracting $\frac{r}{2}$ gives $\frac{r}{2} < r$, so $s < r$.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The collision}: Condition (2) claimed that $r \le x$ for \emph{every} positive number $x$. Since $s$ is a positive number, condition (2) insists that $r \le s$. But our arithmetic showed $s < r$, which implies $r \le s < r$, so $r < r$. A number cannot be strictly less than itself!
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{guide:fig-density-contradiction} illustrates the contradiction: assuming a smallest positive real $r$ creates an interval $(0, r)$ that is supposed to contain no positive real numbers, yet the halved candidate $s = r/2$ lies strictly inside it.

\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a smallest positive real number. Call this number $r$.

By this assumption, $r$ satisfies two properties:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $r > 0$, and
  \item For every real number $x > 0$, we have $r \le x$.
\end{enumerate}
Now consider the candidate number $s = \frac{r}{2}$. 

Because $r > 0$, dividing by the positive integer $2$ preserves the inequality, giving $s = \frac{r}{2} > 0$. Thus $s$ is a positive real number.

At the same time, because $r > 0$, we have $\frac{r}{2} < r$, which means $s < r$.

This directly contradicts condition (2), which asserted that $r$ is less than or equal to every positive real number, including $s$.

Because our assumption led to a contradiction, the assumption must be false. Therefore, there is no smallest positive real number.
\end{proof}

\subsection{What Two-Track Practice Builds---and Its Limits}

Making the two tracks of proof visible gives you three practical capabilities:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Proof Debugging}: You can diagnose exactly where an argument fails: did you collide variable names? Did you confuse an element with a subset? Did you reverse the direction of an implication?
  \item \textbf{Systematic Problem Solving}: When faced with an unfamiliar statement, you do not need an immediate breakthrough. Identifying the hypotheses and conclusion, unpacking definitions into precise logical statements, and analyzing the required structure reveals the steps needed.
  \item \textbf{Auditable Rigor}: By separating exploratory intuition from forward deduction, your arguments become clean, convincing, and straightforward to verify.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
Yet guided commentary has natural limitations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Templates Cannot Solve Novel Research}: Following standard proof architectures works reliably when a theorem's truth is already encoded in the definitions. But in open research problems, standard templates often stall. Breakthroughs often require introducing an auxiliary concept that seems foreign to the original problem---such as inventing imaginary numbers to solve real cubic polynomials, or drawing coordinate axes to solve Euclidean geometry problems.
  \item \textbf{Deductive Rules Cannot Generate Conjectures}: Formal logic verifies truth; it does not discover it. As Henri Poincar\'e observed, logic is the instrument of proof, but intuition is the instrument of invention.
\end{itemize}
