\section{Definitions: The Machines Inside the Words}
\chapterline

In Chapter~5, we treated mathematical claims as geometric territories: a universal statement promises that every object stays inside a safe region, and negating it means hunting for a single witness that steps outside. This raises a question: what draws the boundary line in the first place? How do we determine whether a candidate number, function, or geometric shape belongs inside the territory or stands outside it?

In everyday language, definitions are largely descriptive. Dictionaries record how words are commonly used, capturing nuances, historical context, and loose associations. If someone asks what makes a cup a ``mug,'' one might mention a handle, a cylindrical ceramic shape, and its use for hot drinks. If a container lacks a handle or is made of glass, whether it counts as a mug remains open to informal debate. Everyday language tolerates imprecise boundaries.

In pure mathematics, a definition is an exact specification rather than a descriptive summary. It establishes precise criteria that classify mathematical objects unambiguously. Every object either satisfies all stated conditions and belongs to the defined category, or fails at least one condition and does not.

Figure~\ref{00:fig-definition-machine} in the opening section of the book showed this for the definition of an even integer: $n$ is even if $n$ is an integer and there exists an integer $k$ with $n = 2k$. Each condition acts as a gate that a candidate must pass; for example, $14 = 2 \times 7$ passes both gates, while $9$ is an integer but is not $2$ times any integer.

\begin{historybox}
Throughout the eighteenth century, calculus pioneers including Leonhard Euler and Joseph-Louis Lagrange treated definitions as descriptive summaries of geometric intuition. A \emph{continuous curve} was described informally as a line drawn across paper without lifting the quill, and mathematicians took for granted that any unbroken curve must possess a well-defined tangent line---an instantaneous slope or derivative---at almost every point. If you could sketch it without tears or leaps, smooth calculus tools were assumed to apply.

In 1872, Karl Weierstrass challenged this visual confidence by introducing a counterexample to the Berlin Academy: an infinite sum of cosine oscillations, adding infinitely many ripple waves of ever-smaller wavelengths and ever-steeper slopes. Figure~\ref{06:fig-weierstrass} draws the first few stages of a sum of this kind,
\[
\cos(\pi x) + \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(3\pi x) + \tfrac{1}{4}\cos(9\pi x) + \tfrac{1}{8}\cos(27\pi x) + \cdots,
\]
in which each new wave is half as tall as the one before and oscillates three times as fast. The resulting curve has no breaks or gaps, yet it is nowhere differentiable: it has no well-defined slope at any point. However far you zoom in, at any point, the curve keeps wiggling and never straightens out into a line. Charles Hermite recoiled with ``fright and horror from this lamentable plague of functions without derivatives,'' while Henri Poincar\'e complained that logic had invented monsters to mock common sense.

Bernard Bolzano (in 1817) and Weierstrass drew the lesson that the fault lay in trusting hand-drawn sketches. An ink line on paper has a physical thickness that hides tiny oscillations. Definitions could no longer be loose summaries of what a picture suggests; they had to become exact tests, written with quantifiers ($\forall$ and $\exists$) and inequalities. Continuity, for example, became the condition met in Chapter~3: for every allowed output error $\eps > 0$ there is an input distance $\delta > 0$ such that inputs within $\delta$ of a point give outputs within $\eps$ of the value there. Once continuity was defined by such bounds, a claim about it could be settled by calculation instead of by looking at a picture.

The same approach is used today in proof assistants such as Lean, Coq, and Isabelle (the proof-checking programs met in Chapter~3): each definition is written as an exact condition the program can test. An open research question is whether automated systems can learn to choose good definitions on their own, rather than only checking definitions written by people.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The two operations: unpacking and packaging}

Every definition can be used in two directions. Which one you need depends on whether the fact is something you are given or something you must prove:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Unpacking (Using the Definition)}: You are \emph{given} that an object satisfies a definition. 
The definition allows you to replace the term with its explicit mathematical conditions:
\begin{quote}
``Since $n$ is even, there exists some integer $k$ such that $n = 2k$.''
\end{quote}
Here, the word ``even'' is replaced by a concrete algebraic equation ($n = 2k$) that you can substitute, manipulate, or factor.

\item \textbf{Packaging (Applying the Definition)}: You have performed algebraic manipulations and want to \emph{conclude} that an object satisfies a definition.
The definition specifies the exact entrance criteria you must satisfy:
\begin{quote}
``Since $m = 2(j^2 + j)$ where $j^2 + j \in \Z$, the number $m$ is of the form $2k$ for an integer $k$. Therefore, $m$ is even.''
\end{quote}
Here $j$ is an integer from earlier in the proof. You check the one condition in the definition (that $m$ is $2$ times an integer) and conclude that $m$ is even.
\end{enumerate}

Much of early proof writing consists of three moves: unpack the hypotheses (the facts you are given), work with the algebra, and package the conclusion. Figure~\ref{06:fig-unpack-package-pipeline} shows the three moves in a proof that if $n$ is even, then $n^2$ is even.

\subsection{Divisibility: a definition to handle with care}

The next definition is easy to state and easy to misread:

\begin{definition}
Let $a, b \in \Z$. We say that $a$ \emph{divides} $b$, written $a \mid b$, if there exists an integer $k$ such that $b = ak$.
\end{definition}

In plain language, $a \mid b$ means that $b$ is an exact whole-number multiple of $a$. Think of a measuring rod of length $a$: copies of it laid end to end cover a segment of length $b$ exactly, with nothing left over. The integer $k$ (the number of copies) is the witness.

The order of the two numbers matters:
\begin{itemize}
\item $3 \mid 12$ is true because $12 = 3(4)$. Here $a = 3$, $b = 12$, and the witness integer is $k = 4$.
\item $12 \mid 3$ is false because there is no integer $k$ satisfying $3 = 12k$ (the only real solution is $k = 1/4$, which is not an integer).
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{06:fig-divisibility-tiling} shows this measuring-rod model: $a$ divides $b$ exactly when rods of length $a$ cover the segment from $0$ to $b$ with no gap and no overlap. We write $a \nmid b$ for ``$a$ does not divide $b$.''

\begin{trapbox}
Do not confuse the vertical bar $a \mid b$ with the forward slash $a / b$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $a \mid b$ is a \textbf{statement} (an assertion that can be true or false).
\item $a / b$ is a \textbf{term} (an algebraic expression denoting a rational number).
\end{itemize}
Writing ``if $a / b$ then\ldots'' is a grammatical type error (Chapter~2): the slot after ``if'' needs a statement, and $a / b$ is a number.
\end{trapbox}

Beginners often stumble on cases involving zero and negative numbers, because it is tempting to read $a \mid b$ as the division $b \div a$. Go back to the definition instead. It says that $a \mid b$ exactly when $b$ is one of the multiples $\dots, -2a, -a, 0, a, 2a, \dots$ of $a$; Figure~\ref{06:fig-divisibility-multiples} marks these multiples on number lines for $a = 3$, $a = -4$ and $a = 0$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Does $3 \mid 0$?} The definition asks: does there exist an integer $k$ such that $0 = 3k$? Setting $k = 0$ works, since $0 = 3(0)$. Thus, $3 \mid 0$ is true. In fact, every integer $a$ divides $0$, because $0 = a \cdot 0$.
\item \textbf{Does $0 \mid 3$?} The definition asks: does there exist an integer $k$ such that $3 = 0 \cdot k$? Since $0 \cdot k = 0$ for every integer $k$, no such witness exists. So $0 \nmid 3$.
\item \textbf{Negative divisors:} Does $-4 \mid 12$? Yes, because $12 = (-4)(-3)$, and $-3 \in \Z$. The definition allows negative integers for $a$, $b$ and $k$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Before reading further, test these three candidate pairs mentally against the definition of divisibility:
(1) Does $6 \mid 24$? \quad (2) Does $24 \mid 6$? \quad (3) Does $0 \mid 0$?

\emph{Answers}: (1) True: $24 = 6(4)$. (2) False: $6 = 24k$ has no integer solution. (3) True under the formal definition: $0 = 0 \cdot k$ holds for any integer $k$ (for instance, $k = 1$). So $0 \mid 0$ is a true statement, while the fraction $0/0$ is undefined: the two symbols mean different things.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Why mathematicians demand minimal, non-redundant criteria}

Why are definitions designed the way they are? Consider how you might describe a square in everyday speech:
\begin{quote}
``A four-sided polygon with equal sides, four right angles, opposite sides parallel, and equal-length perpendicular diagonals that bisect each other.''
\end{quote}
(Here \emph{perpendicular} means meeting at a right angle, and the diagonals \emph{bisect} each other when each cuts the other into two equal halves.) That description is accurate, but redundant: most of its items follow from the others. If every one of them were part of the definition, proving that a shape is a square would mean checking every item on the list.

Mathematicians prefer \textbf{minimal, non-redundant definitions}, which contain only conditions that do not follow from the others:
\begin{definition}
A \emph{square} is a quadrilateral (a four-sided polygon) with four equal sides and one right angle.
\end{definition}
The remaining geometric properties---such as parallel opposite sides, three additional right angles, and perpendicular diagonals that bisect each other---are not part of the definition. They are \emph{theorems} deduced from these two conditions. Figure~\ref{06:fig-square-minimal} shows the argument. A diagonal splits a quadrilateral with four equal sides into two triangles whose three sides match in pairs, so the triangles are \emph{congruent} (identical in shape and size; this is the Side-Side-Side rule). Their matching angles are equal, which makes opposite sides parallel. When two sides are parallel, the two corners along a side that crosses them add up to $180^\circ$, so each corner next to the given $90^\circ$ corner is $180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$ as well. Both conditions are needed: Figure~\ref{06:fig-square-minimal} also shows a shape with four equal sides and no right angle, which is not a square. Figure~\ref{06:fig-square-dependencies} sums up which facts are the definition and which are theorems proved from it.

Keeping definitions minimal simplifies proofs:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Less to check}: To prove an object qualifies, you verify only the few defining conditions.
\item \textbf{Nothing lost}: Once those are checked, every theorem about the class applies, including the properties left out of the definition.
\end{itemize}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The Variable Collision Trap}: Chapter~3 warned against reusing a variable name. The danger is greatest when a proof has several hypotheses: unpack each one with a \emph{fresh variable name}.

Suppose you are given that $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$. A frequent beginner mistake is to write:
\begin{center}
``$b = ak$ and $c = bk$.'' \quad \textbf{(Incorrect.)}
\end{center}
By reusing the letter $k$, you have accidentally claimed that the two multipliers are identical: $b/a = c/b$. For instance, $2 \mid 6$ (where the factor is $3$) and $6 \mid 24$ (where the factor is $4$). Reusing $k$ would force $3 = 4$, as Figure~\ref{06:fig-variable-collision} shows.

\textbf{Rule}: Every time you unpack a definition that says ``there exists'' ($\exists$), introduce a new, unused variable ($j, m, k_1, k_2$).
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Worked proof: divisibility is transitive}

A \emph{relation} is a condition on a pair of objects, such as $a \mid b$ or $a < b$. A relation is \emph{transitive} if it passes along chains: whenever $a$ relates to $b$, and $b$ relates to $c$, then $a$ relates directly to $c$. For example, ``is an ancestor of'' is transitive: an ancestor of your parent is also your ancestor. ``Is the parent of'' is not transitive: your parent's parent is your grandparent, not your parent.

The following complete proof uses unpacking and packaging.

\begin{theorem}\label{06:thm-divisibility-transitive}
Let $a, b, c \in \Z$. If $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$. 

\emph{(Unpacking step)}: By the definition of divisibility applied to $a \mid b$, there exists an integer $j$ such that $b = aj$. Similarly, by definition applied to $b \mid c$, there exists an integer $m$ such that $c = bm$. We use two different letters, $j$ and $m$, to avoid the Variable Collision Trap.

\emph{(Algebra)}: We want to show that $c$ is an integer multiple of $a$. Substituting the expression for $b$ into the equation for $c$, we have
\begin{align*}
c &= bm   && \text{(from $b \mid c$)}\\
  &= (aj)m && \text{(substitute $b = aj$)}\\
  &= a(jm) && \text{(regroup the product)}.
\end{align*}
The last step uses the associative law of multiplication: $(xy)z = x(yz)$ for all numbers $x, y, z$.

\emph{(Packaging step)}: Since $j$ and $m$ are integers, their product $k = jm$ is also an integer. (Mathematicians say the integers are \emph{closed} under multiplication: multiplying two integers never takes you outside the integers.) Thus, we have expressed $c$ in the form $c = ak$ for some $k \in \Z$. By definition of divisibility, this means $a \mid c$.
\end{proof}

Figure~\ref{06:fig-transitivity-tiling} shows this substitution geometrically: the total segment $c$ is built from $m$ blocks of length $b$, and each block of length $b$ is composed of $j$ rods of length $a$.

\subsection{From single machines to deductive chains}

Up to now, we have tested definitions one at a time. Most theorems, however, connect multiple definitions through conditional claims, such as: ``If an integer $n$ is a multiple of $4$, then $n$ is even.''

In Theorem~\ref{06:thm-divisibility-transitive}, the path from hypothesis to conclusion was direct. But consider the claim: ``If $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.'' Unpacking the hypothesis gives $n^2 = 2k$, which yields $n = \sqrt{2k}$---an expression that does not readily reveal the integer factors of $n$.

Chapter~7 (\emph{If-Then Statements and Proof Strategy}) looks closely at if-then statements and gives other ways to prove them. One is to prove an equivalent claim that is easier to attack, here ``if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd.'' Figure~\ref{06:fig-two-routes} compares the stuck direct attempt with this second route.

\subsection{Exercises}

\subsubsection*{Part A: Quick Mental Checks}
\begin{enumerate}
\item For each pair of integers $(a, b)$, decide whether $a \mid b$ is true or false:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $(7, 42)$
\item $(42, 7)$
\item $(-3, 15)$
\item $(6, 0)$
\item $(0, 6)$
\end{enumerate}

\item Find the error in this student's attempt to prove that the sum of two even integers is even:
\begin{quote}
``Let $x$ and $y$ be even integers. By definition of even, $x = 2k$ and $y = 2k$. Adding them gives $x + y = 2k + 2k = 4k = 2(2k)$. Since $2k$ is an integer, $x + y$ is of the form $2m$, so $x + y$ is even.''
\end{quote}

\item Determine whether each of the following is a \textbf{statement} (an assertion that can be true or false) or a \textbf{term} (an expression denoting a number or object):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $a \mid b$
\item $a / b$
\item $n = 2k$
\item $2k + 1$
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part B: Direct Unpacking and Packaging Practice}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item An integer $n$ is \emph{odd} if there exists an integer $k$ such that $n = 2k + 1$. Prove that the product of two odd integers is odd. Explicitly identify where you unpack the definition of odd and where you package it.
\item Prove that if $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$, then $a \mid (b + c)$.
\item Prove that if $a \mid b$, then $a \mid (bx)$ for every integer $x$.
\item An integer $n$ is defined to be a \emph{multiple of $4$} if there exists an integer $k$ such that $n = 4k$. Prove that if $n$ is a multiple of $4$, then $n$ is even.
\item A real number $x$ is \emph{rational} ($x \in \Q$) if there exist integers $p, q \in \Z$ with $q \neq 0$ such that $x = p/q$. Prove that if $x, y \in \Q$, then $x + y \in \Q$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Part C: Deeper Exploration}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item Suppose $a, b \in \Z$ are non-zero integers such that $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$. Prove that $a = b$ or $a = -b$. Where in your argument does the fact that $a$ and $b$ are integers (rather than arbitrary real numbers) play an essential role?
\item An integer $p > 1$ is defined to be \emph{prime} if its only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$. Why do mathematicians deliberately demand $p > 1$, excluding the number $1$ from being prime? (Consider what would happen to the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic---which promises that every integer greater than $1$ has a \emph{unique} factorization into primes---if $1$ were included).
\end{enumerate}
