\section{Definitions: The Machines Inside the Words}
\chapterline

In everyday language, a definition is descriptive. A dictionary tells you how people commonly use a word, capturing shades of meaning, historical context, and loose associations.

In mathematics, a definition is not a summary. It is an \textbf{industrial sorting machine}. It draws a sharp boundary through the universe of mathematical objects: every object either meets the exact criteria and enters the defined class, or fails at least one criterion and is rejected. There is no middle ground, no poetic leeway, and no room for negotiation.

\begin{historybox}
Throughout the eighteenth century, calculus pioneers like Leonhard Euler and Joseph-Louis Lagrange treated definitions as descriptive summaries of geometric intuition. A continuous curve was explained as ``a line drawn across paper without lifting the quill,'' and mathematicians took for granted that any continuous curve must naturally possess a well-defined tangent line almost everywhere.

In 1872, Karl Weierstrass shattered this visual intuition by introducing an astonishing object to the Berlin Academy: an infinite sum of cosine oscillations that is continuous everywhere, yet differentiable nowhere. It is a curve with no breaks or jumps, yet consists entirely of jagged corners at every magnification level. Charles Hermite recoiled with ``fright and horror from this lamentable plague of functions without derivatives,'' while Henri Poincar\'e complained that logic had invented monsters to mock common sense.

Yet Bernard Bolzano (in 1817) and Weierstrass realized that the fault lay not in the functions, but in relying on human drawings. A sketch on paper has physical ink width and cannot represent infinite microscopic detail. Definitions could no longer be descriptive summaries; they had to become rigid algebraic sorting machines governed by quantifiers. By defining concepts through exact inequality contracts, mathematics permanently severed proof from sensory illusion.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The two operations: unpacking and packaging}

Every mathematical definition operates as a two-way bidirectional pipeline. Depending on where you stand in a proof, you will use it in one of two modes:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Unpacking (Opening the Box)}: You are \emph{given} that an object satisfies a definition. 
The definition allows you to break open the vocabulary word and extract its mechanical guts.
\begin{quote}
``Since $n$ is even, there exists some integer $k$ such that $n = 2k$.''
\end{quote}
Here, the word ``even'' disappears, and you gain a concrete algebraic tool ($n = 2k$) that you can substitute, manipulate, or factor.

\item \textbf{Packaging (Closing the Box)}: You have performed algebraic manipulations and want to \emph{claim} that an object satisfies a definition.
The definition specifies the exact entrance criteria you must satisfy.
\begin{quote}
``Since $m = 2(j^2 + j)$ where $j^2 + j \in \Z$, the number $m$ is of the form $2k$ for an integer $k$. Therefore, $m$ is even.''
\end{quote}
Here, you verify the entrance requirements, close the box, and affix the formal label ``even.''
\end{enumerate}

Early proof writing is largely the discipline of alternating cleanly between unpacking your hypotheses and packaging your conclusions.

\subsection{Divisibility: a definition to handle with care}

Let us inspect a standard arithmetic definition that beginners often mangle:

\begin{definition}
Let $a, b \in \Z$. We say that $a$ \emph{divides} $b$, written $a \mid b$, if there exists an integer $k$ such that $b = ak$.
\end{definition}

Notice the orientation of the symbols:
\begin{itemize}
\item $3 \mid 12$ is true because $12 = 3(4)$. Here $a = 3$, $b = 12$, and $k = 4$.
\item $12 \mid 3$ is false because there is no integer $k$ satisfying $3 = 12k$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{trapbox}
Do not confuse the vertical bar $a \mid b$ with the forward slash $a / b$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $a \mid b$ is a \textbf{statement} (an assertion that can be true or false).
\item $a / b$ is a \textbf{term} (a rational number).
\end{itemize}
Writing ``if $a / b$ then\ldots'' is a grammatical type error: you cannot put a number into the condition slot of an if-then statement.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Why mathematicians demand minimal, non-redundant criteria}

Why are definitions designed the way they are? Consider how you might define a square in everyday speech:
\begin{quote}
``A four-sided polygon with equal sides, four right angles, opposite sides parallel, and equal-length perpendicular diagonals that bisect each other.''
\end{quote}
That description is completely accurate. But as a mathematical definition, it is an engineering nightmare. 

If that laundry list were the official definition of a square, then every single time you wanted to prove that a geometric shape is a square, you would be legally required to prove all seven of those properties!

Mathematicians prefer \textbf{minimal, non-redundant definitions}:
\begin{definition}
A \emph{square} is a quadrilateral with four equal sides and one right angle.
\end{definition}
That is all. All the other wonderful properties---the parallel opposite sides, the other three right angles, the bisecting perpendicular diagonals---are not part of the definition. They are \emph{theorems} that follow from the minimal definition.

By keeping definitions minimal, mathematicians minimize the burden of proof required to enter the club, while maximizing the structural consequences that follow once inside.

\subsection{Worked proof: divisibility is transitive}

Let us watch the unpacking and packaging machine in action on a complete, formal proof.

\begin{theorem}
Let $a, b, c \in \Z$. If $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$. 

\emph{(Unpacking step)}: By the definition of divisibility applied to $a \mid b$, there exists an integer $j$ such that $b = aj$. Similarly, by definition applied to $b \mid c$, there exists an integer $m$ such that $c = bm$.

\emph{(Algebraic core)}: We want to show that $c$ is an integer multiple of $a$. Substituting the expression for $b$ into the equation for $c$, we have:
\[
c = bm = (aj)m = a(jm).
\]
By the associative property of integer multiplication, $c = a(jm)$. 

\emph{(Packaging step)}: Since $j$ and $m$ are integers, their product $k = jm$ is also an integer. Thus, we have expressed $c$ in the form $c = ak$ for some $k \in \Z$. By definition of divisibility, this means $a \mid c$.
\end{proof}

Every step is accounted for: we opened the definitions to reveal the algebra, manipulated the symbols lawfully, and closed the definition to reach the destination.

\subsection{What definition machines unlocked---and how to chain them}

Turning mathematical vocabulary into explicit sorting machines unlocked the modern standard of rigor: you no longer argue whether an object ``feels'' continuous, symmetric, or prime; you simply run it through the gate conditions and check whether the algebra holds. By keeping definitions minimal and non-redundant, mathematicians minimized the burden of proof required to establish a property while maximizing the structural theorems that follow from it.

Yet inspecting definitions in isolation is only the beginning. Most mathematical claims do not state that an isolated number is even; they state conditional promises connecting one definition machine to another: ``If an integer $n$ is a multiple of $4$, then $n$ is even.'' How do you navigate the path from the unpacked input to the packaged output, especially when a direct route is blocked? 

In Chapter~7 (\emph{If-Then Statements and Proof Strategy}), we examine the architecture of conditional statements, mastering the three primary battle plans---Direct Proof, Contrapositive Proof, and Proof by Contradiction---for chaining definition machines into airtight deductive paths.

\subsection{Exercises}

Prove each claim directly from the definitions of even, odd, and divisibility. Explicitly point out where you unpack a definition and where you package it:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The product of two odd integers is odd.
\item If $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$, then $a \mid (b + c)$.
\item If $a \mid b$, then $a \mid (bx)$ for every integer $x$.
\item An integer $n$ is defined to be a \emph{multiple of $4$} if $\exists k \in \Z, n = 4k$. Prove that if $n$ is a multiple of $4$, then $n$ is even.
\item A real number $x$ is \emph{rational} ($x \in \Q$) if there exist $p, q \in \Z$ with $q \neq 0$ such that $x = p/q$. Prove that if $x, y \in \Q$, then $x + y \in \Q$.
\end{enumerate}
