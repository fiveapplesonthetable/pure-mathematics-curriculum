\section{Second Pass: Algebraic Geometry as Translation}
\chapterline

In Chapter~38, we saw how differential geometry handles curved spaces by patching together smooth coordinate charts. That method needs every small piece of the space to look like a flat piece of $\R^n$. But when a shape has a sharp cusp (like $y^2 = x^3$) or crosses itself (like $y^2 = x^2(x+1)$), as in Figure~\ref{geom:fig-singularities} of Chapter~38, derivatives at the bad point blow up or do not exist, and the manifold method no longer applies there.

The polynomial equations defining these shapes, however, cause no trouble at all. The equation $y^2 = x^3$ is a short polynomial in two variables, and we can add, multiply and factor it like any other. Algebraic geometry studies shapes through the polynomials that define them, without asking for smooth charts.

This chapter uses the tools built across this book---sets, functions, equivalence relations, quotients, rings, and ideals---to set up a two-way translation between \emph{commutative algebra} (the study of rings in which multiplication is commutative, $fg = gf$, such as polynomial rings) and geometry. The idea to carry away: \emph{every shape defined by polynomial equations can be studied through a ring of functions on it, and statements about the ring can be read back as statements about the shape.}

\begin{historybox}
In the late nineteenth century, mathematicians studying polynomial invariants---algebraic quantities that stay unchanged under coordinate rotations and reflections, like the discriminant of a quadratic polynomial---ran into the limits of hand computation. The goal was a \emph{finite generating set}: finitely many invariants from which every other invariant can be built by adding and multiplying. Paul Gordan, the leading expert in invariant theory, carried out long calculations that produced such sets in specific cases, but cases with more variables were out of reach.

Between 1888 and 1893, David Hilbert avoided those computations entirely. Rather than computing invariants by hand, Hilbert gathered all polynomials vanishing on a geometric set into an \emph{ideal}---a collection of polynomials that is closed under addition and absorbs multiplication by any polynomial in the ring. By showing that an endless sequence of genuinely new polynomial constraints is impossible, Hilbert proved his \emph{Basis Theorem}: every ideal in a polynomial ring (with coefficients in a field) has a finite generating set, meaning finitely many polynomials $f_1, \dots, f_m$ such that every element of the ideal has the form $g_1 f_1 + \dots + g_m f_m$ for some polynomials $g_i$. Because the proof argued by contradiction rather than explicit construction, Gordan reportedly exclaimed: ``Das ist nicht Mathematik. Das ist Theologie!'' (``This is not mathematics; it is theology!''---though Gordan later conceded that the theology worked). Hilbert followed this with his \emph{Nullstellensatz} (the ``theorem of the zeros''), which set up the dictionary between ideals and geometric shapes studied in this chapter.

In 1921, Emmy Noether isolated the property that makes Hilbert's argument work: the \emph{ascending chain condition} on ideals, which requires that every nested sequence $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ must eventually stabilize and stop growing. Rings with this property are now called \emph{Noetherian rings}.

The next big step came in the late 1950s and 1960s, when Alexander Grothendieck wrote \emph{\'El\'ements de g\'eom\'etrie alg\'ebrique} (EGA). Grothendieck replaced sets of geometric points with the \emph{prime spectrum} $\operatorname{Spec}(R)$---a geometric space whose points are the prime ideals of a commutative ring $R$. (An ideal $P$ is \emph{prime} if $fg \in P$ forces $f \in P$ or $g \in P$, just as a prime number $p$ dividing $ab$ must divide $a$ or $b$; prime ideals are defined again later in this chapter.) Because every commutative ring has a spectrum, even the integers $\Z$ became a geometric object. This opened up \emph{arithmetic geometry}, the use of geometry to study equations whose solutions must be whole or rational numbers, and set the stage for Andrew Wiles's 1994 proof of Fermat's Last Theorem.

Open questions remain, including the \emph{Hodge Conjecture} (a Millennium Prize Problem asking, roughly, which shapes that topology detects inside a complex variety can be cut out by polynomial equations) and \emph{resolution of singularities in positive characteristic} (replacing a shape with cusps and self-crossings by a smooth one, when the numbers used wrap around like clock arithmetic).
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The Two Worlds: Varieties and Ideals}

To see how algebra and geometry translate into each other, consider a field $k$---a number system where you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide by non-zero elements, such as the real numbers $\R$ or the complex numbers $\C$. Let $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ be the polynomial ring in $n$ variables, which collects every polynomial in $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with coefficients in $k$. The geometric setting is affine $n$-space $k^n$ (often written $\mathbb{A}^n(k)$, or $\mathbb{A}^n$ when $k$ is understood), the space of coordinate points $p = (p_1, \dots, p_n)$ with each $p_i \in k$. As in Chapter~24, ``affine'' means ordinary coordinate space, with no extra points at infinity added.

The bridge runs in two opposite directions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{From Algebra to Geometry (The Zero Locus $V$)}: Given any set of polynomials $S \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, its geometric zero locus is the set of points where every polynomial in $S$ vanishes simultaneously:
  \[
  V(S) = \{p \in k^n : f(p) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in S\}.
  \]
  Such a set is called an \textbf{affine algebraic variety} (or affine algebraic set). For example, $V(x^2 + y^2 - 1) \subset \R^2$ is the unit circle.
  \item \textbf{From Geometry to Algebra (The Vanishing Ideal $I$)}: Given any geometric shape $X \subseteq k^n$, its vanishing ideal collects every polynomial that evaluates to zero everywhere on $X$:
  \[
  I(X) = \{f \in k[x_1, \dots, x_n] : f(p) = 0 \text{ for all } p \in X\}.
  \]
\end{itemize}

Why does $I(X)$ form an ideal in the ring $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$? Recall the two defining requirements of an ideal: closure under addition and absorption of multiplication.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item If $f$ and $g$ vanish everywhere on $X$, their sum $(f+g)(p) = f(p) + g(p) = 0 + 0 = 0$ also vanishes on $X$.
  \item If $f$ vanishes on $X$ and $h \in k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is \emph{any} polynomial, the product evaluates to $(h \cdot f)(p) = h(p) \cdot 0 = 0$ on $X$.
\end{enumerate}

For a concrete example, take the single point $p = (2, 3) \in \R^2$. The two simplest polynomials vanishing at $p$ are $x - 2$ and $y - 3$. But any multiple like $(x-2)h(x, y)$ or sum $(x-2)g(x, y) + (y-3)h(x, y)$ also vanishes at $(2, 3)$. We write $(f_1, \dots, f_m)$ for the \emph{ideal generated by} $f_1, \dots, f_m$: the set of all combinations $g_1 f_1 + \dots + g_m f_m$ with polynomial coefficients $g_i$. It is the smallest ideal containing all the $f_i$. The vanishing ideal of this single point is the ideal generated by the two linear coordinate equations:
\begin{align*}
I(\{(2, 3)\}) &= (x - 2,\; y - 3)\\
&= \{\, a(x, y)\,(x - 2) + b(x, y)\,(y - 3) \;:\; a, b \in \R[x, y] \,\}.
\end{align*}
(That every polynomial vanishing at $(2,3)$ lies in this ideal takes a short argument: rewrite $f$ in powers of $x-2$ and $y-3$; every term except the constant contains one of these factors, and the constant term equals $f(2,3) = 0$.)

\subsection{The Italian School and Why Ring Theory Was Needed}

Three lessons, one historical and two technical, explain why modern algebraic geometry leans so heavily on commutative algebra:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Crisis of the Italian School}: In the early twentieth century, the Italian school of algebraic geometry (led by Guido Castelnuovo, Federigo Enriques, and Francesco Severi) made major intuitive breakthroughs in classifying algebraic surfaces. However, their arguments relied on geometric pictures and rules of thumb rather than proofs from the algebra, and by the 1930s their advanced theorems were plagued by conflicting definitions and unstated assumptions; some were false. Oscar Zariski and Andr\'e Weil recognized that geometry needed the firm foundation of commutative ring theory, and over the following decades the subject was rebuilt on it, later adding sheaf theory (a systematic way of keeping track of functions defined on small overlapping patches) and other algebraic tools.
  \item \textbf{The Blindness of Sets of Points to Nilpotents}: For centuries, geometry was conceived as the study of sets of points. But consider the ideals $J_1 = (x)$ and $J_2 = (x^2)$ in the single-variable ring $\C[x]$. Geometrically, both have the identical zero locus: $V(J_1) = V(J_2) = \{0\}$. A purely point-based geometry cannot tell them apart. Yet algebraically, the quotient ring $\C[x]/(x^2)$ contains a non-zero \emph{nilpotent} element---the class of $x$, which is not zero but whose square is zero ($x^2 = 0$ in the quotient). This nilpotent element behaves like an infinitely small step away from the point, a tangent direction, so the point carries extra ``thickness'' recorded by the algebra. Figure~\ref{alggeom:fig-tangent-double-point} shows where such a thickened point comes from: two intersection points sliding together into one. Grothendieck's \emph{schemes} (geometric objects built directly from rings, so that nilpotents are kept rather than discarded) showed that rings retain information about multiplicity and tangency that point sets throw away.
  \item \textbf{Failure Over Real Numbers}: The Nullstellensatz (stated later in this chapter) requires an \emph{algebraically closed} field---a number system like $\C$ where every non-constant single-variable polynomial has a root. Over the real numbers $\R$, the ideal $J = (x^2 + 1) \subset \R[x]$ has an empty zero locus: $V(J) = \emptyset$, because the graph of $x^2 + 1$ never reaches the $x$-axis (Figure~\ref{alggeom:fig-reals-empty}). The polynomials vanishing on the empty set are all polynomials: $I(V(J)) = \R[x]$, which is much larger than the radical $\sqrt{J} = (x^2 + 1)$ (the radical, defined later in this chapter, is what the Nullstellensatz predicts). The dictionary between ideals and varieties works cleanly over $\C$ because every non-constant polynomial has roots there, rather than having its solutions hidden outside the number system.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Rosetta Stone: The Algebraic Geometry Dictionary}

The maps $V(-)$ and $I(-)$ (the dash marks where the input goes) pair algebraic operations on polynomial ideals with geometric operations on shapes, in the way the Rosetta Stone paired the same text in two scripts.

Here is how the basic operations translate:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Order-Reversing Duality}: An ideal represents a list of equations that must evaluate to zero. If you make the ideal larger ($I_1 \subseteq I_2$), you impose \emph{more} polynomial constraints on the points. More constraints make it harder to qualify, so the solution set can only shrink or stay the same:
  \[
  I_1 \subseteq I_2 \implies V(I_1) \supseteq V(I_2).
  \]
  The converse fails: $V(x) = V(x^2) = \{0\}$, yet $(x) \not\subseteq (x^2)$.
  \item \textbf{Sum of Ideals Becomes Intersection of Shapes}: In algebra, the sum ideal $I + J$ consists of all elements $f + g$ with $f \in I$ and $g \in J$. For a point $p$ to make every element of $I + J$ vanish, it must in particular satisfy all equations in $I$ (set $g = 0$) and all equations in $J$ (set $f = 0$). Thus $p$ must lie in both $V(I)$ and $V(J)$:
  \[
  V(I + J) = V(I) \cap V(J).
  \]
  \item \textbf{Product of Ideals Becomes Union of Shapes}: The product ideal $I \cdot J$ is generated by products $f \cdot g$ where $f \in I$ and $g \in J$. In any field, a product of numbers vanishes if and only if at least one factor vanishes: $f(p) \cdot g(p) = 0 \iff f(p) = 0 \text{ or } g(p) = 0$. Therefore, a point $p$ satisfies the equations of $I \cdot J$ if and only if it lies in $V(I)$ \emph{or} in $V(J)$:
  \[
  V(I \cdot J) = V(I) \cup V(J).
  \]
\end{enumerate}

For a picture, consider the two coordinate axes in the plane $\R^2$ (Figure~\ref{alggeom:fig-product-sum}). Let $I = (y)$ be the ideal cutting out the horizontal $x$-axis, and let $J = (x)$ be the ideal cutting out the vertical $y$-axis.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Their product is $I \cdot J = (xy)$. The equation $xy = 0$ is satisfied whenever $x = 0$ or $y = 0$. This gives the \textbf{union} of the two axes, a cross.
  \item Their sum is $I + J = (x, y)$. The equations $x = 0$ and $y = 0$ must hold at the same time, pinning the solution down to the single origin $(0, 0)$, the \textbf{intersection} of the two axes.
\end{itemize}

Beyond sums and products, the dictionary pairs the basic building blocks of algebra with those of geometry:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prime Ideals Correspond to Irreducible Varieties}: A proper ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ (one that is not the whole ring; the letter $\mathfrak{p}$ is a Gothic ``p'') is \emph{prime} if $f \cdot g \in \mathfrak{p} \implies f \in \mathfrak{p} \text{ or } g \in \mathfrak{p}$. Geometrically, this means the variety $V(\mathfrak{p})$ cannot be split into the union of two strictly smaller algebraic varieties; it is \textbf{irreducible}, like a circle or parabola, as opposed to the reducible cross $xy = 0$, which is the union of the two axes. Algebra sees the split directly: $x \cdot y \in (xy)$, yet neither $x$ nor $y$ lies in $(xy)$, so $(xy)$ is not prime (Figure~\ref{alggeom:fig-irreducible}).
  \item \textbf{Maximal Ideals Correspond to Geometric Points}: An ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ is \emph{maximal} if it is proper and no strictly larger proper ideal contains it. When $k$ is algebraically closed (such as $\C$), the maximal ideals of $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ are exactly those of the form $\mathfrak{m}_p = (x_1 - p_1, \dots, x_n - p_n)$, which correspond to single geometric points $p = (p_1, \dots, p_n)$. (This is one form of the Nullstellensatz, discussed below. Over $\R$ there are extra maximal ideals with no real points, such as $(x^2 + 1) \subset \R[x]$.)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Coordinate Ring: Describing a Shape by Its Functions}

What is a geometric shape? To a topologist, it is a set of points equipped with open neighborhoods. To an algebraic geometer, a variety $V$ is determined by the \textbf{polynomial functions on it}.

Consider the circle $C = V(x^2 + y^2 - 1) \subset \R^2$. On the whole plane, the polynomial $x^2 + y^2$ and the constant polynomial $1$ are different functions: at $(0,0)$ one gives $0$ and the other $1$. But at every point of the circle they give the same value. As functions on the circle, they are the same function, and we want our algebra to treat them as equal.

To keep only what a polynomial does on $V$, send each polynomial to its restriction to $V$:
\[
\Phi: k[x_1, \dots, x_n] \to \operatorname{Fun}(V, k), \quad \Phi(f) = f|_V.
\]
Here $\operatorname{Fun}(V, k)$ is the ring of all functions from $V$ to $k$, added and multiplied point by point, and $f|_V$ (``$f$ restricted to $V$'') is the function that takes the same values as $f$ but only accepts points of $V$. The map $\Phi$ is a ring homomorphism (it respects addition and multiplication, Chapter~32), called the \emph{evaluation homomorphism}. What is the kernel of $\Phi$, the set of polynomials sent to the zero function? A polynomial $f$ restricts to the zero function on $V$ if and only if $f(p) = 0$ for every $p \in V$. That is the exact definition of the vanishing ideal $I(V)$.

The First Isomorphism Theorem, which we met for sets in Chapter~33 and for groups in Chapter~36, holds for rings in the same form: the image of a ring homomorphism is isomorphic to the domain divided by the kernel. So the image of $\Phi$ is isomorphic to the quotient ring:
\[
k[V] \;=\; k[x_1, \dots, x_n] / I(V).
\]
This quotient is the \textbf{coordinate ring} of the variety $V$. Two polynomials $f$ and $g$ define the same function on $V$ if and only if their difference lies in $I(V)$:
\[
f \equiv g \pmod{I(V)} \iff (f - g)(p) = 0 \text{ for all } p \in V.
\]
Figure~\ref{alggeom:fig-coordinate-ring-triangle} draws the three rings and the maps between them.

The coordinate ring tells us what kind of shape the variety is. Consider the standard parabola $Y = V(y - x^2) \subset \R^2$. Its vanishing ideal is generated by $y - x^2$. In the coordinate ring $\R[x, y]/(y - x^2)$, the relation $y \equiv x^2$ allows us to replace every appearance of $y$ with $x^2$:
\[
x^3 + 2y - 5 \;\equiv\; x^3 + 2x^2 - 5.
\]
Every polynomial function on the parabola simplifies to a polynomial in $x$ alone. The coordinate ring $\R[Y]$ is isomorphic to $\R[x]$, the coordinate ring of the line $\mathbb{A}^1$. In the language of algebraic geometry, the parabola and the line are the same variety, placed differently: the parabola is a copy of the line bent into the plane.

In contrast, for the circle $C = V(x^2 + y^2 - 1)$, the coordinate ring $\R[x, y]/(x^2 + y^2 - 1)$ is not isomorphic to the single-variable polynomial ring $\R[x]$. One sign of this: an isomorphism would give a polynomial substitution $x = p(t),\, y = q(t)$ tracing out the circle, with $p$ and $q$ not both constant. But then $p(t)^2 + q(t)^2 = 1$ for all $t$, which is impossible: the highest-degree terms of $p^2$ and $q^2$ have positive coefficients and cannot cancel. So the difference between the circle and the line can be detected inside their coordinate rings, without looking at the surrounding plane.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} In the coordinate ring of the unit circle, $\R[x, y]/(x^2 + y^2 - 1)$, simplify the polynomial $f(x, y) = x^3 + x y^2 + 4x - 7$. Since $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ along the circle,
\begin{align*}
f(x, y) &= x(x^2 + y^2) + 4x - 7 && \text{(factor $x$ out of the first two terms)}\\
&\equiv x \cdot 1 + 4x - 7 && \text{(replace $x^2 + y^2$ by $1$)}\\
&= 5x - 7.
\end{align*}
The equation of the shape acts as a rewrite rule: wherever $x^2 + y^2$ appears, replace it by $1$.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Hilbert's Nullstellensatz: Completing the Bridge}

Does this dictionary have any blind spots? What happens if you take an ideal $J$, compute its geometric variety $V(J)$, and then compute the vanishing ideal $I(V(J))$? Do you get back the exact ideal $J$ you started with?

Not always. Consider $J = (x^2) \subset \C[x]$. The only point where $x^2 = 0$ is $x = 0$. So the geometric variety is the single point $V(J) = \{0\}$. But what polynomials vanish at $0$? The ideal of all polynomials vanishing at $0$ is $(x)$, not $(x^2)$. The geometry saw where the polynomial was zero, but could not detect the \emph{multiplicity} of the root: $x^2 = x \cdot x$ has the root $0$ twice over, while $x$ has it once.

David Hilbert settled exactly how much is lost, in 1893, with his \textbf{Nullstellensatz} (Theorem of the Zeros).

First, we define the \textbf{radical} of an ideal $J$:
\[
\sqrt{J} = \{f \in k[x_1, \dots, x_n] : f^m \in J \text{ for some integer } m \ge 1\}.
\]
Why does the radical appear naturally? Take $f$ with $f^m \in J$ and any point $p \in V(J)$. Then
\[
f^m \in J \implies f(p)^m = 0 \implies f(p) = 0.
\]
The first step holds because every element of $J$ vanishes at $p$; the second because in a field a power of a number is zero only if the number itself is zero. Therefore, any polynomial in $\sqrt{J}$ must vanish on $V(J)$, proving the easy inclusion $\sqrt{J} \subseteq I(V(J))$.

Hilbert proved the reverse direction:

\begin{theorem}[Hilbert's Nullstellensatz]
Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field (such as $\C$), and let $J \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ be an ideal. Then:
\[
I(V(J)) = \sqrt{J}.
\]
\end{theorem}

Figure~\ref{alggeom:fig-nullstellensatz-loop} traces the round trip for $J = (x^2)$. Points cannot tell $x^2 = 0$ apart from $x = 0$, so the round trip $I(V(J))$ returns to the radical $\sqrt{J} = (x)$ instead of to $J$.

Call an ideal \emph{radical} if $\sqrt{J} = J$, that is, if it already contains every polynomial some power of which lies in it; $(x)$ is radical, $(x^2)$ is not. As the bottom panel of Figure~\ref{alggeom:fig-nullstellensatz-loop} shows, over an algebraically closed field like $\C$, the maps $V$ and $I$ give a bijection between radical ideals and algebraic varieties: every radical ideal corresponds to a unique variety, and every variety corresponds to a unique radical ideal.

A direct consequence is the \textbf{Weak Nullstellensatz}, which tells us when a system of equations has no solution:
\begin{quote}
\emph{A system of polynomial equations $f_1 = 0, \dots, f_m = 0$ has no common solution in $\C^n$ ($V(f_1, \dots, f_m) = \emptyset$) if and only if $1 \in (f_1, \dots, f_m)$, meaning there exist polynomials $g_1, \dots, g_m \in \C[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ such that:}
\[
g_1 f_1 + g_2 f_2 + \dots + g_m f_m = 1.
\]
\end{quote}
This gives an algebraic certificate of inconsistency: to prove that a geometric variety is empty, you never need to search across infinitely many points in $\C^n$; you only need to find a finite polynomial combination that adds up to $1$. To see why the Weak Nullstellensatz follows, write $J = (f_1, \dots, f_m)$. If $V(J) = \emptyset$, then every polynomial vanishes on $V(J)$ (there are no points to check), so $I(V(J))$ is the whole ring and contains $1$. The Nullstellensatz then gives
\[
1 \in I(V(J)) = \sqrt{J} \quad\implies\quad 1 = 1^r \in J \ \text{ for some } r \ge 1.
\]
Conversely, if $g_1 f_1 + \dots + g_m f_m = 1$ and $p$ were a common zero, evaluating at $p$ would give $0 = 1$.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Consider the two equations $x = 0$ and $xy - 1 = 0$ in $\C[x, y]$. Geometrically, if $x = 0$, then $xy - 1 = 0 \cdot y - 1 = -1 \neq 0$, so the solution set is empty: $V(x, xy - 1) = \emptyset$. (In the real plane, Figure~\ref{alggeom:fig-hyperbola-axis} shows the picture: the hyperbola $xy = 1$ approaches the $y$-axis but never touches it.) What polynomial combination certifies this? Multiply the first polynomial by $y$, the second by $-1$, and add:
\[
y \cdot x + (-1) \cdot (xy - 1) = xy - xy + 1 = 1.
\]
This identity is the concrete certificate that no solution exists.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{What the Algebraic Dictionary Makes Possible---and Its Limits}

Why did twentieth-century mathematics invest so heavily in this dictionary? Because translating geometric questions into commutative algebra let mathematicians solve problems that had resisted purely geometric or calculus-based methods:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Number Theory via Geometry}: A \emph{Diophantine equation} is a polynomial equation whose solutions must be whole numbers or fractions, such as $x^n + y^n = z^n$. Such an equation can be treated as a variety, and geometric facts about the variety then limit its solutions. Gerd Faltings' 1983 proof of the Mordell Conjecture says that a curve of genus $g \ge 2$ has only finitely many points with rational coordinates; here the \emph{genus} is the number of holes of the curve when its complex points are viewed as a surface, as in Chapter~22. Andrew Wiles' 1994 proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, which goes through \emph{elliptic curves} (curves of the form $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$), also rests on this translation.
  \item \textbf{B\'ezout's Theorem on Intersections}: The \emph{degree} of a plane curve is the degree of its defining polynomial: a line has degree $1$, a circle or parabola degree $2$. Two plane curves of degrees $d_1$ and $d_2$ with no common component (they do not share a whole piece of curve) meet in exactly $d_1 \cdot d_2$ points of the complex projective plane $\mathbb{P}^2$ (Chapter~24), counted with \emph{multiplicity}, so that a point of tangency counts more than once, as in Figure~\ref{alggeom:fig-tangent-double-point}. Counting intersections becomes multiplying degrees; Figure~\ref{alggeom:fig-bezout} shows two cases.
  \item \textbf{Handling Singularities}: A \emph{singular point} is a point such as a cusp or a self-crossing where the shape fails to be smooth. Unlike manifolds, algebraic varieties are allowed to have singular points, such as the cusp of $y^2 = x^3$ and the double point of $y^2 = x^2(x+1)$, and the coordinate ring describes them just as well as smooth points. Heisuke Hironaka proved in 1964 (work that earned him a Fields Medal) that, over fields such as $\C$, every variety can be \emph{resolved}: replaced by a smooth variety that maps onto it and differs from it only near the singular points.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope and Limitations.}
Algebraic geometry has costs as well:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Heavy Algebraic Prerequisites}: The modern language of schemes, sheaves, and cohomology (algebraic tools that measure how local information fits together globally) requires a great deal of commutative ring theory before one can compute basic geometric properties.
  \item \textbf{The Coarseness of the Zariski Topology}: The natural topology for varieties is the \emph{Zariski topology}, whose closed sets are the zero loci $V(S)$. On an irreducible variety it is not Hausdorff (Chapter~37): any two non-empty open sets intersect, so there are no small, separate neighborhoods around different points. On the line $\mathbb{A}^1$, for example, the zero locus of a non-zero polynomial in one variable is a finite set of roots, so the open sets are the line with finitely many points removed, and any two of them overlap almost everywhere (Figure~\ref{alggeom:fig-zariski-line}). Tools from calculus that rely on small balls around a point, such as the implicit function theorem (which says that near a suitable point, an equation can be solved for one variable in terms of the others), do not carry over directly, and algebraic geometers had to build algebraic substitutes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Let $V = V(y - x^2) \subset \R^2$ be the standard parabola.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Consider the evaluation map $\phi: \R[x, y] \to \R[x]$ given by $\phi(f(x, y)) = f(x, x^2)$. Prove that $\phi$ is a surjective ring homomorphism with $\ker \phi = (y - x^2)$.
    \item Deduce that the coordinate ring $\R[V] = \R[x, y]/(y - x^2)$ is isomorphic to the single-variable polynomial ring $\R[x]$.
    \item What does this isomorphism tell you geometrically about the relationship between a parabola and a line?
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Let $J = (x^2 + y^2) \subset \R[x, y]$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Find the real zero locus $V(J) \subset \R^2$.
    \item Show that the vanishing ideal of this zero locus is $I(V(J)) = (x, y)$.
    \item Show that $\sqrt{J} = (x^2 + y^2)$ in $\R[x, y]$. Why does $I(V(J)) \neq \sqrt{J}$, and how does this illustrate the failure of the Nullstellensatz over $\R$?
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Prove that for any two ideals $I, J \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$,
  \[
  V(I \cdot J) = V(I) \cup V(J).
  \]
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Show that $V(I) \cup V(J) \subseteq V(I \cdot J)$.
    \item Show the reverse inclusion $V(I \cdot J) \subseteq V(I) \cup V(J)$ by showing that if a point $p \in V(I \cdot J)$ does not lie in $V(I)$, it must satisfy every polynomial in $J$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mental check}: Consider the ideal $I = (x - 3, y + 4) \subset \C[x, y]$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Identify the geometric variety $V(I) \subset \C^2$. Is it a curve, a surface, or a point?
    \item Is $I$ a prime ideal? Is it a maximal ideal? Explain by identifying the quotient ring $\C[x, y]/I$.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Weak Nullstellensatz Certificate}: Consider the two polynomials $f_1(x, y) = x - y$ and $f_2(x, y) = x^2 - y^2 - 1$ in $\C[x, y]$.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Explain geometrically why the system $f_1 = 0$ and $f_2 = 0$ has no common solutions in $\C^2$, so $V(f_1, f_2) = \emptyset$. (Hint: substitute $x = y$ into $f_2$.)
    \item Find explicit polynomials $g_1(x, y), g_2(x, y) \in \C[x, y]$ such that $g_1 f_1 + g_2 f_2 = 1$, providing the algebraic certificate of inconsistency. (Hint: factor $x^2 - y^2$.)
  \end{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Order-Reversing Duality}: Prove that if $I \subseteq J \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ are ideals, then their zero loci satisfy $V(J) \subseteq V(I)$. Explain in plain language why adding more algebraic constraints shrinks the geometric solution set.
\end{enumerate}
