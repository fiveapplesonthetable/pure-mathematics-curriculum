\section{Relations and Equivalence}
\chapterline

In everyday life, we constantly decide that two different things are ``the same'' for a specific purpose. 

A one-dollar bill printed in 2017 and a crisp bill printed in 2024 are made of different atoms and carry different serial numbers. But if you buy a coffee, the cashier does not inspect the serial numbers. For the purpose of commerce, both bills are identical.

Similarly, on an analog clock, the numbers $1$, $13$, and $25$ represent entirely different quantities of hours. Yet when reading the hour hand, all three point to the exact same position on the dial. The differences of $12$ hours have been deliberately collapsed.

In mathematics, the tool that formalizes this act of purposeful simplification is the \textbf{equivalence relation}.

\begin{historybox}
The conceptual origin of equivalence relations traces to Carl Friedrich Gauss. In his 1801 work, \emph{Disquisitiones Arithmeticae}, Gauss introduced modular arithmetic and the congruence symbol $a \equiv b \pmod n$. 

Before Gauss, remainders were treated as incidental byproducts of division. Gauss recognized that numbers leaving the same remainder modulo $n$ can be grouped together as a single entity: an \emph{equivalence class}. 

Contemporaries questioned the new notation, asking why one could not simply write $a - b = kn$. Gauss saw that congruence behaves like equality, allowing arithmetic directly on the classes: $[a] + [b] = [a+b]$ and $[a][b] = [ab]$. 

Later mathematicians such as Dedekind, Poincar\'e, and Noether applied this principle throughout algebra and geometry: to ignore extraneous detail, factor out symmetries, or glue boundaries together, define an equivalence relation and pass to the quotient.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Relations in General}

Before studying equivalence, we need the general concept of a relation between elements of a set.

\begin{definition}[Relation]
A \textbf{binary relation} $R$ on a set $A$ is a subset of the Cartesian product:
\[
R \subseteq A \times A.
\]
If $(a, b) \in R$, we write $a \sim b$ or $a \, R \, b$, and say that ``$a$ is related to $b$.''
\end{definition}

The Cartesian product $A \times A$ is the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ with $a, b \in A$---think of a two-dimensional grid where both rows and columns are indexed by elements of $A$. A binary relation simply marks which cells in that grid are activated. 

For instance, let $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$. The strict order relation $<$ on $A$ is the subset of pairs where the first number is strictly less than the second:
\[
R_{<} = \{(1, 2), (1, 3), (2, 3)\} \subseteq A \times A.
\]
Because $(1, 2) \in R_{<}$, we write $1 < 2$. Because $(3, 1) \notin R_{<}$, we say $3 \not< 1$.

Relations appear across every corner of mathematics:
\begin{itemize}
\item Arithmetic orders: $x < y$ or $x \le y$ on the real numbers $\R$.
\item Divisibility: $a \mid b$ on the integers $\Z$ ($a$ divides $b$ with no remainder).
\item Geometric connectivity: two points in a space connected by a continuous path.
\item Set containment: $S \subseteq T$ on the power set $\pow(U)$ (the family of all subsets of $U$).
\end{itemize}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If a set $A$ contains $n$ elements, the Cartesian product $A \times A$ contains $n^2$ ordered pairs. Because a relation is \emph{any} subset of $A \times A$, there are $2^{n^2}$ distinct relations on $A$! For a 3-element set, that is $2^9 = 512$ possible relations; for $4$ elements, $2^{16} = 65{,}536$. Most of these subsets are arbitrary scatterings of pairs. Three axioms select the ones that behave like equality.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{The Three Axioms of Equivalence}

To partition a set into classes of equivalent elements, a relation must satisfy three conditions.

\begin{definition}[Equivalence Relation]
A relation $\sim$ on a set $A$ is an \textbf{equivalence relation} if it satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Reflexivity:} For all $a \in A$,
\[
a \sim a.
\]
(Every object is related to itself.)
\item \textbf{Symmetry:} For all $a, b \in A$,
\[
a \sim b \implies b \sim a.
\]
(The relation is symmetric: if $a$ relates to $b$, then $b$ relates to $a$.)
\item \textbf{Transitivity:} For all $a, b, c \in A$,
\[
(a \sim b \text{ and } b \sim c) \implies a \sim c.
\]
(Connections chain together: if $a$ relates to $b$ and $b$ relates to $c$, then $a$ relates to $c$.)
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Reflexivity ensures every element belongs to at least one class (its own). Symmetry ensures that being related is a mutual condition rather than a one-way property. Transitivity ensures that relatedness chains consistently: if $a$ relates to $b$, and $b$ relates to $c$, then $a$ and $c$ share membership in the same class.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The Trap of Naive Similarity:}
It is tempting to think that any intuitive notion of ``being similar'' defines an equivalence relation. Consider the relation on $\R$ defined by $|x - y| \le 1$ (``$x$ and $y$ are within distance 1 of each other''). 
Reflexivity holds ($|x - x| = 0 \le 1$), and symmetry holds ($|y - x| = |x - y| \le 1$). 
Yet transitivity fails completely: $0 \sim 0.8$ and $0.8 \sim 1.5$, but $|0 - 1.5| = 1.5 > 1$, so $0 \not\sim 1.5$!
Without transitivity, elements cannot be partitioned into disjoint subsets. A point can be close to two other points that are far from each other, so the relation does not partition the set into equivalence classes.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The False Proof of Reflexivity:}
A famous trap tempts many beginners: ``Why do we list reflexivity as an independent axiom? Doesn't symmetry and transitivity already prove it? If $a \sim b$, then by symmetry $b \sim a$. Then by transitivity, since $a \sim b$ and $b \sim a$, we must have $a \sim a$!''

Where does this deduction collapse? It begins with the unstated premise that \emph{there exists some $b$ such that $a \sim b$}. If an element $a$ is completely isolated and relates to nothing at all, the deduction cannot even begin!

For example, consider the relation on $\R$ defined by $x \sim y \iff xy > 0$. 
This relation is symmetric: if $xy > 0$, then $yx = xy > 0$.
It is transitive: if $xy > 0$ and $yz > 0$, then $x$ and $y$ have the same sign, and $y$ and $z$ have the same sign, so $x$ and $z$ must have the same sign, giving $xz > 0$.
Yet it is \emph{not} reflexive: for $x = 0$, we have $0 \cdot 0 = 0 \not> 0$, so $0 \not\sim 0$! The number $0$ satisfies $0 \cdot y = 0 \not> 0$ for every $y$, so it relates to no element, not even itself. Reflexivity is required to guarantee that every element belongs to an equivalence class.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Equivalence Classes and Quotients}

Once you have an equivalence relation on $A$, the set breaks apart into clusters called \emph{equivalence classes}.

\begin{definition}[Equivalence Class]
Let $\sim$ be an equivalence relation on $A$. For any $a \in A$, the \textbf{equivalence class} of $a$, denoted $[a]$ or $\bar{a}$, is the set of all elements related to $a$:
\[
[a] = \{x \in A : x \sim a\}.
\]
The element $a$ is called a \textbf{representative} of the class $[a]$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations]
Let $\sim$ be an equivalence relation on $A$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item For any $a, b \in A$, $a \sim b \iff [a] = [b]$.
\item Any two equivalence classes are either strictly disjoint or completely identical:
\[
[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset \implies [a] = [b].
\]
\item The collection of all equivalence classes forms a \textbf{partition} of $A$: they are pairwise disjoint and their union is all of $A$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We prove the three assertions in sequence, observing how each of the three axioms does indispensable work.

\textbf{Part (a): $a \sim b \iff [a] = [b]$.}

($\implies$) Assume $a \sim b$. We show that the sets $[a]$ and $[b]$ are equal by showing each is a subset of the other.
Let $x \in [a]$. By definition of an equivalence class, $x \sim a$. Because $x \sim a$ and $a \sim b$, transitivity guarantees that $x \sim b$, meaning $x \in [b]$. Hence $[a] \subseteq [b]$.
Conversely, because $a \sim b$, symmetry gives $b \sim a$. Repeating the exact same transitivity argument with $b \sim a$ shows that any $y \in [b]$ satisfies $y \sim b$ and $b \sim a$, so $y \sim a$, giving $[b] \subseteq [a]$.
Since both inclusions hold, $[a] = [b]$.

($\impliedby$) Assume $[a] = [b]$. By reflexivity, $b \sim b$, which means $b \in [b]$. Because $[b] = [a]$, we have $b \in [a]$. By definition of the class $[a]$, this means $b \sim a$. Finally, symmetry gives $a \sim b$.

\textbf{Part (b): $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset \implies [a] = [b]$.}

Assume the intersection is non-empty, so there exists some shared element $c \in [a] \cap [b]$.
Because $c \in [a]$, we have $c \sim a$, and symmetry yields $a \sim c$.
Because $c \in [b]$, we have $c \sim b$.
By transitivity, from $a \sim c$ and $c \sim b$, we conclude that $a \sim b$.
Applying Part~(a) to $a \sim b$, we immediately get $[a] = [b]$. 
Therefore, equivalence classes can never partially overlap; if they share an element, they are identical.

\textbf{Part (c): Equivalence classes partition $A$.}

A partition of a set $A$ is a family of non-empty, pairwise disjoint subsets whose union equals $A$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item \emph{Every class is non-empty and the classes cover $A$:} Let $x \in A$. By reflexivity, $x \sim x$, which means $x \in [x]$. Thus no equivalence class is empty, and every element of $A$ belongs to at least one class. Taking the union over all representatives:
\[
\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A.
\]
\item \emph{The classes are pairwise disjoint:} By Part~(b), for any two elements $a, b \in A$, either $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$ (the classes are disjoint) or $[a] = [b]$ (they are the exact same class).
\end{enumerate}
Therefore, the collection of distinct equivalence classes forms a true partition of $A$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Quotient Set and Projection]
The collection of all equivalence classes of $A$ under $\sim$ is called the \textbf{quotient set}, denoted $A / \!\sim$:
\[
A / \!\sim \; = \{[a] : a \in A\}.
\]
The function $\pi: A \to A / \!\sim$ given by $\pi(a) = [a]$ is called the \textbf{canonical projection map}.
\end{definition}

In the quotient space, each entire equivalence class has collapsed into a single mathematical point (Figure~\ref{15:fig-partition-and-quotient}).

Observe the direct connection to Chapter~14: the canonical projection $\pi: A \to A / \!\sim$ is always a \emph{surjective} function, and the preimage of any class point under $\pi$ is the equivalence class itself:
\[
\pi^{-1}(\{[a]\}) = \{x \in A : \pi(x) = [a]\} = \{x \in A : [x] = [a]\} = [a].
\]
In Chapter~14, every function gave us a partition of its domain into fibers. Here we discover the converse: every equivalence relation defines a canonical function whose fibers are the equivalence classes.

\subsection{A Canonical Example: Modular Arithmetic}

Let $n$ be a positive integer. We say two integers $a, b \in \Z$ are \emph{congruent modulo $n$}, written $a \equiv b \pmod n$, if their difference is a multiple of $n$:
\[
a \equiv b \pmod n \iff n \mid (a - b).
\]

\begin{theorem}
Congruence modulo $n$ is an equivalence relation on $\Z$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We check all three axioms systematically.

\textbf{1. Reflexivity:} Let $a \in \Z$. Then $a - a = 0 = n \cdot 0$. Because $0$ is an integer, $n \mid (a - a)$, so $a \equiv a \pmod n$.

\textbf{2. Symmetry:} Let $a, b \in \Z$ and assume $a \equiv b \pmod n$. 
By definition, $n \mid (a - b)$, meaning $a - b = n k$ for some integer $k$. 
Multiplying both sides by $-1$:
\[
b - a = -(a - b) = -nk = n(-k).
\]
Since $-k \in \Z$, $n \mid (b - a)$, which proves $b \equiv a \pmod n$.

\textbf{3. Transitivity:} Let $a, b, c \in \Z$, and assume $a \equiv b \pmod n$ and $b \equiv c \pmod n$.
Then $a - b = n j$ and $b - c = n k$ for integers $j, k \in \Z$. 
Adding the two equations:
\[
a - c = (a - b) + (b - c) = n j + n k = n(j + k).
\]
Since $j + k$ is an integer, $n \mid (a - c)$, confirming $a \equiv c \pmod n$.

Since reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity all hold, congruence modulo $n$ is an equivalence relation.
\end{proof}

The quotient set $\Z / \equiv_n$ is denoted $\Z_n$ (or $\Z/n\Z$) and contains exactly $n$ equivalence classes:
\[
\Z_n = \{[0], [1], [2], \dots, [n-1]\}.
\]
Every integer belongs to exactly one of these $n$ buckets, corresponding to its remainder upon division by $n$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Arithmetic on Classes: Well-Defined Operations}

A key feature of Gauss's quotient is that we can perform arithmetic with the classes themselves. We define addition and multiplication of equivalence classes by:
\[
[a] + [b] = [a + b] \quad \text{and} \quad [a] \cdot [b] = [a \cdot b].
\]
A class like $[2]$ in $\Z_5$ is an infinite set:
\[
[2] = \{\dots, -8, -3, 2, 7, 12, 17, \dots\}.
\]
To compute $[2] + [4]$, the definition tells us to pick the representative $2$ and the representative $4$, add them to get $6$, and take the class $[6] = [1]$. 

What if we pick different representatives from the same classes---say $7 \in [2]$ and $9 \in [4]$? We compute $7 + 9 = 16$. Because $16$ leaves remainder $1$ when divided by $5$, $[16] = [1]$, giving the exact same class.

Whenever an operation on equivalence classes is defined in terms of chosen representatives, we must prove that the outcome is \textbf{well-defined}---that it depends only on the classes themselves, not on which representatives are chosen.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{lemma}[Well-Definedness of Modular Arithmetic]
Let $n \ge 1$. If $a \equiv a' \pmod n$ and $b \equiv b' \pmod n$, then:
\[
a + b \equiv a' + b' \pmod n \quad \text{and} \quad ab \equiv a'b' \pmod n.
\]
Consequently, $[a + b] = [a' + b']$ and $[ab] = [a'b']$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Because $a \equiv a' \pmod n$ and $b \equiv b' \pmod n$, there exist integers $j, k \in \Z$ such that
\[
a' = a + jn \quad \text{and} \quad b' = b + kn.
\]
For addition, we sum the two expressions:
\[
a' + b' = (a + jn) + (b + kn) = (a + b) + n(j + k).
\]
Because $j + k$ is an integer, $n \mid ((a' + b') - (a + b))$, which proves $a' + b' \equiv a + b \pmod n$.

For multiplication, we compute the product:
\[
a' b' = (a + jn)(b + kn) = ab + akn + bjn + jkn^2 = ab + n(ak + bj + jkn).
\]
Because $ak + bj + jkn \in \Z$, the difference $a'b' - ab$ is a multiple of $n$. Hence $a'b' \equiv ab \pmod n$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Applications: Constructing New Mathematical Spaces}

Equivalence relations and quotient sets allow us to construct new mathematical structures from existing ones by identifying elements that share desired properties:

\textbf{1. Constructing the Rational Numbers $\Q$:}
How do you define fractions if you only have the integers $\Z$? You cannot define $p/q$ as the result of division, because division does not exist yet! 

Instead, start with the set of all pairs of integers where the second number is non-zero: $S = \Z \times (\Z \setminus \{0\})$. We think of the pair $(p, q)$ as representing the fraction $p/q$. But we know that $\frac{1}{2}$, $\frac{2}{4}$, and $\frac{-3}{-6}$ represent the same value. Cross-multiplication tells us when two pairs should agree:
\[
(p, q) \sim (r, s) \iff ps = qr.
\]
This relation $\sim$ is an equivalence relation on $S$. The rational number that we write as ``$1/2$'' is defined to be the equivalence class:
\[
[(1, 2)] = \{(1, 2), (2, 4), (3, 6), (-1, -2), \dots\}.
\]
The set of all rational numbers $\Q$ is the quotient set $S / \!\sim$.

\textbf{2. Constructing Geometric Spaces by Gluing:}
Take a unit square $X = [0, 1] \times [0, 1] \subset \R^2$.
Declare that opposite left and right boundary points are equivalent: $(0, y) \sim (1, y)$ for all $y \in [0, 1]$. In the quotient space $X / \!\sim$, the left and right edges are identified, producing a cylinder.

Now declare that the top and bottom circles are also equivalent: $(x, 0) \sim (x, 1)$ for all $x \in [0, 1]$. In the new quotient space, the cylinder joins its ends together, forming a 2-dimensional torus. 

The torus is not formed by physically bending a sheet in three dimensions, but set-theoretically by identifying boundary points on a polygon.

\textbf{3. Completing the Real Numbers $\R$:}
In Chapter~20, we will see that the rational numbers $\Q$ have gaps where numbers like $\sqrt{2}$ and $\pi$ should sit. Cantor and Dedekind constructed the real numbers $\R$ by taking Cauchy sequences of rational numbers and declaring two sequences equivalent if their difference converges to zero. A single real number is an equivalence class of rational approximations.

\subsection{The Boundary of Equivalence: Why We Need Orders}

Equivalence relations partition sets into classes of peers, but they cannot model \textbf{hierarchy} or directed relationships:
\begin{itemize}
\item How do we rank items by magnitude: which number is larger ($x \le y$)?
\item How do we model prerequisites: which task must finish before another can begin?
\item How do we describe nesting: which set contains another ($S \subseteq T$)?
\item How do we capture divisibility: $2 \mid 6$ and $6 \mid 12$, but $12$ does not divide $6$?
\end{itemize}

All four of these relationships have a built-in direction; none of them is symmetric.

To model hierarchy, we must break symmetry. If $a$ precedes $b$, we cannot allow $b$ to precede $a$ unless $a$ and $b$ are the same object. In Chapter~16, we turn to \textbf{Orders: Another Kind of Relation}, trading symmetry for \emph{antisymmetry} to explore posets, chains, and lattices.
