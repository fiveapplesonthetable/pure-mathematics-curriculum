\section{Mathematical Sentences: Statements, Predicates, and Claims}
\chapterline

In Chapter~1, we saw that numerical calculation and diagrams have limits: pictures can mislead, and checking finite cases cannot survey an infinite collection. To build arguments that hold without exception, we need a precise medium: mathematical language.

Yet mathematical language is not simply everyday speech written in chalk. In everyday conversation, words shift their meanings, statements can be figurative or approximate, and a word as common as ``is'' serves different functions from one sentence to the next. Before constructing proofs, we need to understand the structure of mathematical sentences: what kinds of objects exist, which expressions refer to objects, which expressions make assertions, and which assertions evaluate to true or false.

\begin{historybox}
For more than two thousand years, formal logic was synonymous with Aristotle's syllogisms (c.~350 BCE in the \emph{Prior Analytics}), which classified arguments into rigid verbal templates like ``All humans are mortal; Socrates is human; therefore Socrates is mortal.'' A syllogism is a two-premise deduction where general categories are combined to force a conclusion. Scholars had only natural language and rhetorical rules to guide them. But as mathematics advanced toward calculus and abstract geometry, Aristotle's grammar broke down completely. It could not express even simple relational deductions---arguments that depend on how two objects relate to one another---such as Augustus De Morgan's 1860 challenge: ``All horses are animals; therefore every head of a horse is a head of an animal.''

Everyday grammar also blurs distinct logical relationships. In ordinary speech, the word ``is'' performs three different roles: it asserts identity (``a triangle \emph{is} a three-sided polygon''), declares a property or membership (``seven \emph{is} prime''), or claims universal containment (``a dog \emph{is} an animal''). Conflating these three roles caused centuries of philosophical confusion and made rigorous proofs of infinite processes impossible.

In 1879, the German mathematician Gottlob Frege published his \emph{Begriffsschrift} (``Concept Script''). Frege replaced everyday subject-predicate grammar with the mathematical concept of a function. An open condition like ``\ldots is prime'' is a predicate function $P(x)$ that accepts an input object and outputs an unambiguous truth value: True or False. 

Frege's work was initially met with skepticism; contemporary mathematicians found his two-dimensional notation unwieldy. In addition, Frege's system allowed unrestricted set formation, which Bertrand Russell showed was inconsistent in 1901 with Russell's Paradox---the realization that the set of all sets that do not contain themselves can neither contain nor omit itself without contradiction. In the 1910s, L.E.J.~Brouwer challenged the Law of the Excluded Middle (the assumption that every statement is automatically either True or False), arguing that a statement is only established if one can explicitly construct a witness.

Despite these debates, Frege's functional breakthrough gave mathematics its modern taxonomy of terms, predicates, and statements. In 1931, Kurt G\"odel showed that in any consistent formal system containing basic arithmetic, there are true statements that cannot be proved within the system.
\end{historybox}

In mathematics, sentences are divided by this sharp taxonomic line.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{statement} (or \emph{proposition}) is a sentence that is unambiguously either true or false, but not both.
\end{definition}

In pure mathematics, statements are the central objects of deduction. A proof is a deductive demonstration that a given statement is true, rather than a numerical calculation. If a sentence has no truth value, asking for a proof is a category mistake.

Consider these examples:
\begin{itemize}
\item ``$7$ is a prime number'' is a true statement.
\item ``$9$ is a prime number'' is a false statement.
\item ``There are infinitely many prime numbers'' is a true statement.
\item ``Every continuous function on $\R$ is differentiable'' is a false statement (the absolute value function $f(x) = |x|$ fails to have a derivative at $x = 0$).
\end{itemize}

A sentence does not need to be easy to evaluate to qualify as a statement. For more than three centuries, Fermat's Last Theorem---the claim that $a^n + b^n = c^n$ has no positive integer solutions for integers $n > 2$---remained unproved. Its truth was unknown until Andrew Wiles and Richard Taylor completed a proof in 1995. Throughout those three centuries, the sentence was not a vague riddle; it was a well-defined statement whose truth value had not yet been established.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Test your intuition on these four sentences. Which ones are statements?
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item ``$2 + 3 = 5$.''
\item ``$x + 3 = 5$.''
\item ``$1 = 0$.''
\item ``Please solve for $x$.''
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Check:} Sentence (a) is a true statement. Sentence (c) is also a statement---it is completely false, but its falsity is unambiguous. Sentence (b) is \emph{not} a statement yet; without knowing what $x$ stands for, it cannot be judged true or false. Sentence (d) is an imperative command, not an assertion of fact.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Predicates: sentences with open variable slots}

An expression such as $x^2 - 1 = 0$ looks like a statement, but it is not. You cannot say whether it is true or false until you know what $x$ is. If $x = 1$, the sentence becomes $1^2 - 1 = 0$, which is true. If $x = 3$, the sentence becomes $3^2 - 1 = 0$, which is false.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{predicate} is a sentence containing one or more variables that becomes a statement whenever each variable is replaced by a specific element from a specified domain, or bound by a quantifier.
\end{definition}

In computer science, a predicate is a function that returns a boolean value:
\begin{center}
\texttt{isEven : Integer $\to$ \{True, False\}}
\end{center}
Until you pass an input into \texttt{isEven(n)}, the function produces no boolean output. A predicate functions like a template with open variable slots: until an input fills every slot, the expression does not evaluate to True or False. In mathematics, we write $P(x)$ to indicate that $P$ is an open condition waiting for an input $x$.

There are two primary ways to close an open slot and obtain a statement:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Instantiation (Substituting a Value):} We select a specific candidate $c$ from our domain and evaluate $P(c)$. For $P(x) : x^2 - 1 = 0$, setting $x = 1$ yields the true statement $1^2 - 1 = 0$; setting $x = 3$ yields the false statement $3^2 - 1 = 0$.
\item \textbf{Quantification (Binding the Variable):} We close the variable slot by asserting how widely the condition holds across the entire domain. The universal quantifier $\forall$ (read ``for all'') asserts that every candidate satisfies the predicate:
\[
\forall x \in \R,\; x^2 - 1 = 0 \quad (\text{False: witnessed by } x = 0).
\]
The existential quantifier $\exists$ (read ``there exists'') asserts that at least one candidate works:
\[
\exists x \in \R,\; x^2 - 1 = 0 \quad (\text{True: witnessed by } x = 1).
\]
Once bound by a quantifier, $x$ is no longer an open slot waiting for input; it is a bound dummy variable. The entire sentence is now a closed statement.
\end{enumerate}

Predicates can have more than one variable. For instance, the inequality $L(x, y) : x < y$ has two free variables: $L(-, -)$. If you substitute only one value, say $x = 3$, you obtain $L(3, y) : 3 < y$, which is still a predicate in $y$. Only when both variables are resolved does a statement emerge: $L(3, 5)$ is True, while $L(3, 2)$ is False. We will explore multiple variables and quantifier order in depth in Chapters~3 and~4.

\subsection{The domain changes everything}

When you work with a predicate, the mathematical universe from which variables may be chosen---the \emph{domain of discourse}---is not an optional footnote. It governs the truth of the sentence.

Recall our standard number systems:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\N = \{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$: the natural numbers (positive integers).
\item $\Z = \{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$: the integers.
\item $\Q = \{p/q : p, q \in \Z, q \neq 0\}$: the rational numbers (fractions).
\item $\R$: the real numbers (including rationals and irrationals like $\sqrt{2}$ and $\pi$).
\item $\C = \{a + bi : a, b \in \R, i^2 = -1\}$: the complex numbers.
\end{itemize}

Now examine the predicate $P(x) : x^2 < 2$. Its truth depends entirely on where $x$ is allowed to live:
\begin{itemize}
\item Over the natural numbers $\N$, the statement ``$\exists x \in \N, x^2 < 2$'' has only a single witness: $x = 1$.
\item Over the integers $\Z$, the solutions are $x \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$.
\item Over the rational numbers $\Q$, there are infinitely many solutions, yet the boundary points $-\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{2}$ do not exist inside $\Q$.
\item Over the real numbers $\R$, the solution set is the open interval $(-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2})$.
\item Over the complex numbers $\C$, the inequality $x^2 < 2$ is not even standardly defined, because the complex numbers do not possess an ordering compatible with arithmetic.
\end{itemize}

\begin{trapbox}
Always specify the domain of a variable. The assertion $x^2 \ge 0$ depends directly on where $x$ lives: over $\R$ it is true, while over $\C$ choosing $x = i$ gives $i^2 = -1 < 0$.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Consider the predicate $E(x) : 2x = 1$. Over which of the following domains does the statement ``$\exists x, E(x)$'' become True?
(a) The natural numbers $\N$; (b) The integers $\Z$; (c) The rational numbers $\Q$; (d) The real numbers $\R$.

\textbf{Answer:} Over $\N$ and $\Z$, the statement is False: no integer doubled equals $1$. Over $\Q$ and $\R$, it is True: the witness $x = 1/2$ exists in both domains. The choice of domain directly determines whether the statement is true or false.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Claims, theorems, and mathematical etiquette}

When mathematicians state assertions they intend to establish, they use conventional labels to communicate the structural role of the result:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Theorem}: A central, landmark result that anchors an entire theory (such as the Pythagorean Theorem or the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic).
\item \textbf{Proposition}: A solid, standalone result of intermediate significance, worth recording and reusing in its own right.
\item \textbf{Lemma}: A stepping-stone result. Lemmas often do the heavy algebraic lifting so that the main theorem can be stated and proved cleanly.
\item \textbf{Corollary}: A direct and immediate consequence of an established theorem, requiring minimal additional effort to verify.
\item \textbf{Conjecture}: A statement believed to be true based on strong evidence or heuristic patterns, but which currently lacks a deductive proof (such as the Twin Prime Conjecture or the Goldbach Conjecture).
\item \textbf{Claim}: A local sub-goal inside an ongoing proof. An author isolates an intermediate milestone by declaring ``Claim: \ldots'', establishes it, and then resumes the main argument.
\end{itemize}

These terms are stylistic and architectural rather than purely formal. To a computer checking formal logic, a lemma, a proposition, and a theorem are identical: they are all verified true statements. For a human reader, however, these labels organize the architecture of a theory, and one author's stepping-stone lemma might later become celebrated as a major theorem (such as Zorn's Lemma in set theory or Gauss's Lemma in number theory).

\subsection{What formal grammar gives us---and where it stops}

Frege's functional taxonomy brought order to mathematical sentences: it separated the nouns we study (terms) from the assertions we make about them (statements), and gave open conditions a precise home as predicates. It protects us from categorical type errors, such as treating a number as a subset, or testing whether an open formula is true without binding its variables.

Yet this grammatical taxonomy is static: it classifies expressions individually, but does not describe how variables behave across a multi-step proof. When an argument introduces a variable $x$, we must know how it is introduced: is $x$ a fixed parameter, an arbitrary element representing any member of a set, a specific witness chosen to satisfy a condition, or a bound variable local to a formula?

Without clear rules governing variable scope and hypotheses, even well-formed sentences can lead to invalid deductions. In Chapter~3 (\emph{Variables Have Roles: Fixed, Arbitrary, Chosen, Bound}), we examine the four standard roles of variables and the principles of variable scope in mathematical proofs.

\begin{walkbox}
When reading a mathematical argument, notice how often the word ``is'' appears. \end{walkbox}
