\section{Proof Debugging: Turning Fog Into Work}
\chapterline

\subsection{The hidden scratchpad: why polished proofs hide the work}

In the preceding chapters, we previewed abstract algebra, topology, differential geometry, and algebraic geometry. But whatever the topic, doing mathematics eventually comes down to sitting with pencil and paper to write a proof. And often, progress stalls.

In mathematical textbooks and research papers, proofs are usually presented in final, polished form. The author omits the exploratory scratch work, dead ends, and tentative revisions that led to the result.

This presentation can make mathematical proof seem like a single uninterrupted deduction. When a beginner writes several lines, encounters an obstacle, and is unsure how to proceed, they may conclude that proof writing requires immediate insight rather than methodical troubleshooting.

In 1945, the Hungarian-American mathematician George P\'olya published \emph{How to Solve It}, emphasizing that mathematical problem-solving relies on systematic \emph{heuristics}---practical strategies and rules of thumb that guide work when no algorithmic recipe exists. Effective mathematicians rely on concrete methods: decomposing problems, testing minimal examples, and diagnosing why initial attempts fail.

\subsection{Treating proofs as structured arguments}

When a computer program crashes or produces the wrong output, a programmer does not stare at the screen waiting for inspiration; they open a debugger, inspect the variables, and find the exact line where the state went wrong.

Getting stuck on a proof works the same way. Hitting a wall usually points to a concrete issue---a definition you have not yet written out in full, a missing hypothesis, a quantifier in the wrong order, or a false claim. Once you inspect a stalled draft the way you would debug code, getting stuck gives you something specific to check. Figure~\ref{fig:debug-triage} shows the order in which to check things.

\subsection{The five-stage diagnostic protocol}

When your pencil stops moving, work through these five steps in order:

\subsubsection{1. Test a minimal non-trivial example}
Before trying to prove a general theorem about all integers, all sets, or all functions, write down the smallest concrete instance you can think of. (A \emph{non-trivial} example is one where the claim has real content to check, not one where it holds automatically.) If a claim involves integers, test $n = 0, 1, 2, 3$. If it involves sets, test sets with $0$, $1$, and $2$ elements.
\begin{itemize}
\item If your claim fails on $n = 2$, you have found a counterexample, saving hours attempting to prove a false statement.
\item If your claim holds on small examples, observe \emph{why} it holds. The mechanism that makes $n = 2$ work often reveals the algebra needed for general $n$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose someone asserts: ``Every integer $n > 2$ can be written as the sum of two distinct prime numbers.'' Before trying an abstract proof, test minimal instances: $n = 3, 4, 5, 6$. For $n = 3$, the only positive integers smaller than $3$ are $1$ and $2$; since $1$ is not prime, $3$ cannot be written as a sum of two primes at all. For $n = 4$, the only prime smaller than $4$ is $2$, and $2 + 2 = 4$ repeats the same prime, so they are not distinct. The claim fails on the first two instances you check, so there is nothing to prove. Figure~\ref{fig:small-cases} records all four checks; $n = 6$ fails too, since $6 = 3 + 3$ repeats a prime and $6 = 1 + 5$ uses the non-prime $1$.

\subsubsection{2. Unpack every definition down to its quantifier core}
A word like ``continuous'' hides the logic inside it. Writing the definition out exposes its quantifiers, $\forall$ (``for all'') and $\exists$ (``there exists''), from Chapter~4. If the theorem says ``$f$ is continuous,'' do not leave the word ``continuous'' on your paper. Replace it with:
\[
\forall x_0 \in \R, \; \forall \eps > 0, \; \exists \delta > 0, \; \forall x \in \R, \; (|x - x_0| < \delta \implies |f(x) - f(x_0)| < \eps).
\]
Here $x_0$ is the point where continuity is being checked, $\eps > 0$ is an allowed error around the output $f(x_0)$, and $\delta > 0$ is a radius around $x_0$ such that every input within $\delta$ of $x_0$ gives an output within $\eps$ of $f(x_0)$ (Chapter~20 treats this in detail). Once a definition is written in explicit logical symbols, the quantifiers determine the structure of the proof: read from left to right, each quantifier becomes one sentence of the proof, as Figure~\ref{fig:quantifier-ladder} shows.

\subsubsection{3. Separate the Given shelf from the Goal shelf}
Take a blank sheet of paper and draw a horizontal line across the middle:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Top half (The Given Shelf)}: List every assumption and hypothesis, and every variable you have introduced together with what it stands for. These are the tools already in your hands.
\item \textbf{Bottom half (The Goal Shelf)}: State the exact target expression you must reach.
\end{itemize}
A common error is confusing an assumption with a target. Using the target as if it were already known is \emph{circular reasoning}: the argument assumes what it set out to prove. Keeping the two on separate halves of the paper guards against this. Figure~\ref{fig:two-shelf} shows the layout and the two directions of work.

\subsubsection{4. Type-check every mathematical symbol}
In a programming language, every value has a \emph{type} (integer, string, list, and so on), and the compiler rejects code that uses a value of one type where another is expected. Mathematical objects have types in the same sense, and checking them catches many errors:
\begin{itemize}
\item Is $x$ an element, a set, or a function?
\item Does the expression $f(A)$ mean the evaluation of a point or the image of a subset?
\item If $A$ and $B$ are sets, writing $A < B$ is a type error unless an order relation has been explicitly defined on the power set.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose $f: X \to Y$ is a function between sets, and $B \subseteq Y$. What is the type of $f^{-1}(B)$ versus $f^{-1}(y)$ for a point $y \in Y$? The preimage $f^{-1}(B) = \{x \in X : f(x) \in B\}$ is always a well-defined subset of $X$, regardless of whether $f$ has an inverse. But writing $f^{-1}(y)$ treats $f^{-1}$ as an inverse function mapping a point to a point, which is a type error unless $f$ is already known to be bijective (a one-to-one matching). Confusing a set with an element is one of the most common type errors in beginner proofs. Figure~\ref{fig:preimage-types} shows a small function where the preimage of a single point is a set of two elements, or no elements at all.

\subsubsection{5. Verify the connective architecture}
Recall from Chapter~5 that a \emph{logical connective} builds a statement out of smaller ones: ``and'' ($\land$), ``or'' ($\lor$), ``not'' ($\neg$), ``if \ldots\ then'' ($\implies$). Quantifiers $\forall$ and $\exists$ play a similar role. The \emph{outermost} one is the one that governs the whole statement; in $\forall x, \, (P(x) \implies Q(x))$ it is $\forall$. Look at the outermost connective or quantifier of your goal. For a direct proof:
\begin{itemize}
\item If the goal is $\forall x \in S, \, P(x)$, your proof begins: ``Let $x \in S$ be arbitrary.''
\item If the goal is $P \implies Q$, your proof begins: ``Assume $P$.'' Your temporary goal becomes $Q$. (The other options are the contrapositive, used in the case study below, and contradiction, from Chapter~8.)
\item If the goal is $A \subseteq B$, your proof begins: ``Let $x \in A$.'' Your target is to show $x \in B$.
\item If the goal is $\exists x \in S, \, P(x)$, your proof must name or construct a specific witness $x \in S$ and demonstrate that $P(x)$ holds.
\item If the goal is $P \land Q$, your proof must split into two independent sub-proofs: first deduce $P$, then deduce $Q$.
\item If the goal is $P \lor Q$, you may assume $\neg P$ and deduce $Q$ (since $P \lor Q$ is logically equivalent to $\neg P \implies Q$), or divide your argument into cases that together cover every possibility.
\end{itemize}
The grammar of the goal gives you the first line of your proof before you understand the hard mathematics inside. Figure~\ref{fig:goal-openers} collects the four most common openings.

\subsection{Catalog of proof gaps and repairs}

\begin{longtable}{p{0.18\linewidth}p{0.35\linewidth}p{0.39\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Gap Name} & \textbf{Symptom in Draft} & \textbf{Surgical Repair} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Quantifier Gap} & An object is manipulated without introducing where it came from or what role it plays. & Declare the variable explicitly: state whether it is arbitrary ($\forall$) or chosen ($\exists$), and specify its domain. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Definition Gap} & Words like ``continuous'' or ``divisible'' are used by feel, without their precise definitions. & Erase the English word and replace it with its exact definition. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Existence Gap} & A proof asserts ``choose an element $x$ with property $P$'' when no such element is guaranteed to exist. & Construct an explicit formula for $x$, or cite a theorem that guarantees existence (for example, the Intermediate Value Theorem: a continuous function on an interval that takes a negative value and a positive value is zero somewhere in between). \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Implication Gap} & A line jumps directly from step $A$ to step $B$ with the word ``clearly,'' hiding a non-trivial theorem. & Break the leap into two smaller steps; write down the intermediate lemma and verify it. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Case Gap} & An algebraic step divides by $x - 1$ without considering the possibility that $x = 1$. & Split into explicit cases that together cover every possibility: Case 1 ($x = 1$) and Case 2 ($x \neq 1$). \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Converse Error} & Attempting to prove $P \implies Q$ by assuming $Q$ and showing it leads to $P$. & Reorient the proof: start strictly with hypothesis $P$ and verify that each arrow points forward toward $Q$. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\subsection{Case study: debugging a broken parity proof}

Consider the following student proof attempt regarding \emph{parity} (whether an integer is even or odd):

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Claim}: If $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even. \\
\textbf{Draft Proof}: Since $n^2$ is even, $n^2 = 2k$ for some integer $k$. Taking square roots of both sides gives $n = \sqrt{2k}$. Therefore $n$ is even. \hfill $\square$
\end{quote}

\textbf{Debugging Analysis}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Line 1}: $n^2 = 2k$ for $k \in \Z$. Valid; correctly unpacks the definition of even.
\item \emph{Line 2}: $n = \sqrt{2k}$. Already flawed for general integers: the symbol $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ always means the non-negative square root, so this step silently drops every negative integer (for instance, $n = -2$ satisfies $n^2 = 4$, yet $\sqrt{4} = 2 \neq -2$). Even if we wrote $n = \pm\sqrt{2k}$, worse trouble waits on the next line.
\item \emph{Line 3}: ``Therefore $n$ is even.'' \textbf{Bug detected.} The definition of even requires $n = 2m$ for an integer $m$. Writing $|n| = \sqrt{2k} = \sqrt{2}\sqrt{k}$ places the factor of $2$ under a square-root sign, which does not show that $n$ is an integer multiple of $2$. Deducing from $\sqrt{2k}$ that $n$ is an even integer would require knowing that $k = 2m^2$---which assumes the very conclusion we are trying to prove. Taking square roots moves the argument out of integer arithmetic into the real numbers.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Surgical Repair via Contrapositive}:
Taking square roots is inconvenient because square roots do not preserve integer divisibility directly. Instead, consider the \emph{contrapositive} (proving ``if $P$ then $Q$'' by establishing the logically equivalent statement ``if not $Q$ then not $P$''):
\[
\text{If } n \text{ is odd, then } n^2 \text{ is odd.}
\]
Now the algebra stays inside the integers:
\begin{proof}
We prove the contrapositive. Assume $n$ is odd. By definition, there exists an integer $m$ such that $n = 2m + 1$. Squaring both sides yields
\begin{align*}
n^2 &= (2m + 1)^2 \\
    &= 4m^2 + 4m + 1 && \text{(expand the square)} \\
    &= 2(2m^2 + 2m) + 1. && \text{(factor $2$ out of the first two terms)}
\end{align*}
Since $m$ is an integer, $k = 2m^2 + 2m$ is an integer. Thus $n^2 = 2k + 1$, which means $n^2$ is odd. Having proved the contrapositive, we conclude that the original claim holds.
\end{proof}

\begin{historybox}
In his 1976 study \emph{Proofs and Refutations}, Imre Lakatos argued that mathematical knowledge often develops through an iterative process of conjectures, counterexamples, and conceptual refinements.

Lakatos reconstructed the historical development of Euler's formula for polyhedra---three-dimensional solid shapes bounded by flat polygonal faces, straight edges, and sharp vertices. In 1758, Leonhard Euler observed that for familiar solids like the cube and tetrahedron, the count of vertices $V$, edges $E$, and faces $F$ obeys $V - E + F = 2$. In 1813, Augustin-Louis Cauchy published an argument: remove one face, stretch the remaining network flat onto a plane, and cut each polygon into triangles. Yet over the following century, counterexamples emerged. Self-intersecting star polyhedra with interlocking vertices violated the count. A small cube nested hollowly inside a larger cube gave $V - E + F = 4$, and a picture-frame polyhedron with a central tunnel gave $V - E + F = 0$.

When confronted with these counterexamples, mathematicians initially debated whether to exclude non-standard polyhedra (an approach Lakatos called \emph{monster-barring}) or to modify definitions to accommodate them. The mathematical resolution came from inspecting where Cauchy's argument failed: Cauchy had assumed that the boundary of every polyhedron is a single connected surface that can be inflated into a sphere without holes (whereas the hollow cube has two separate spherical boundary shells, contributing $2 + 2 = 4$, and the picture frame has a tunnel). By identifying that topological assumption and stating it as an explicit hypothesis, mathematicians proved the general relation for every polyhedron whose surface is one connected piece:
\[
V - E + F = 2 - 2g,
\]
where $g$ is the genus from Chapter~9 (the number of doughnut-like tunnels passing through the solid; $g = 0$ for a cube, $g = 1$ for a picture frame). Figure~\ref{fig:euler-polyhedra} shows the three solids and their counts.
Today, proofs can also be checked by computer proof assistants such as Lean, Coq, and Isabelle (Chapter~3). These programs check every step of a fully formal proof. An active area of research is automated proof repair: given an informal, incomplete, or buggy proof draft, how can an algorithm isolate the missing lemma, find a counterexample, or distinguish between a notation error and a conceptual gap?
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The craft of proof debugging---and the practice ahead}

Getting stuck in the middle of a proof is normal; what matters is having concrete moves to make next. The five checks---small examples, written-out definitions, separate Given and Goal shelves, types, and the goal's outermost connective---turn a vague sense of confusion into specific questions. Even when a draft fails outright, the counterexample that breaks it shows which hypothesis was missing or how the claim must change.

These checks become habits only through practice. Chapter~26, \textbf{A Serious Velleman Readiness Plan}, prepares you for Daniel Velleman's textbook \emph{How to Prove It} with a four-week practice schedule and a set of ten benchmark proofs.

\subsection{Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Mental Check (Spot the Gap Type):} For each of the following flawed proof fragments, identify the primary gap from the catalog (Quantifier Gap, Definition Gap, Existence Gap, Implication Gap, Case Gap, or Converse Error):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item ``To prove that $f$ is continuous on $\R$, suppose $|x - y|$ is very small. Then $f(x)$ is close to $f(y)$, so $f$ is continuous.''
\item ``We wish to prove that if $n$ is a multiple of $6$, then $n$ is even. Assume $n$ is even, so $n = 2k$. Choosing $k = 3m$ gives $n = 6m$, so $n$ is a multiple of $6$.''
\item ``Let $x^2 - x = 0$. Dividing both sides by $x$ gives $x - 1 = 0$, so $x = 1$. Therefore $1$ is the unique solution.''
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Routine Practice (Debugging Parity and Divisibility):} A student attempts to prove the following claim:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Claim:} For all integers $n$, if $3n + 2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd.\\
\textbf{Student Draft:} Assume $3n + 2$ is odd. Then $3n + 2 = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$. Subtracting $2$ yields $3n = 2k - 1$. Dividing by $3$ gives $n = (2k - 1)/3$. Since $2k - 1$ is odd, $n$ must be odd. \hfill $\square$
\end{quote}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Diagnose the fatal bug in the student's draft. (Why does $n = (2k - 1)/3$ fail to prove that $n$ is an odd integer?)
\item Formulate the contrapositive of the claim.
\item Write a complete, correct proof using the contrapositive.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Routine Practice (Element Chasing and Case Splitting):} A student writes the following proof that $A \cap (B \cup C) \subseteq (A \cap B) \cup C$ for arbitrary sets $A, B, C$:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Student Draft:} Let $x \in A \cap (B \cup C)$. Then $x \in A$ and $x \in B \cup C$. Thus $x \in A$ and $x \in B$. Therefore $x \in A \cap B$, which implies $x \in (A \cap B) \cup C$. \hfill $\square$
\end{quote}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Point out the exact line where the draft makes an unjustified leap.
\item Provide a concrete counterexample showing that $x \in B \cup C$ does not imply $x \in B$.
\item Supply the missing repair by partitioning into cases ($x \in B$ versus $x \in C$) to complete a valid proof.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Deeper Inquiry (The All-Horses Fallacy):} Consider the famous false theorem: ``All horses have the same color.'' The induction attempt argues:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Base step ($n = 1$):} In any set consisting of one horse, all horses in the set trivially have the same color.
\item \emph{Induction step:} Assume any set of $k$ horses has the same color. Given a set of $k+1$ horses $\{h_1, h_2, \dots, h_{k+1}\}$, the first $k$ horses $\{h_1, \dots, h_k\}$ share the same color by the induction hypothesis. Similarly, the last $k$ horses $\{h_2, \dots, h_{k+1}\}$ share the same color. Since the two groups overlap at the middle horses, all $k+1$ horses must share the same color.
\end{itemize}
Test this argument on minimal examples ($k = 1, 2$). For which value of $k$ does the deduction bridge collapse, and what exact set-theoretic condition fails?

\item \textbf{Deeper Inquiry (Preserving Intersections Under Maps):} Consider the claim that for any function $f: X \to Y$ and any subsets $A, B \subseteq X$:
\[
f(A \cap B) = f(A) \cap f(B).
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Test a minimal finite example: let $X = \{1, 2\}$, $Y = \{0\}$, and let $f(1) = f(2) = 0$. Choose $A = \{1\}$ and $B = \{2\}$. Compute $f(A \cap B)$ and $f(A) \cap f(B)$. Is the claim true?
\item Set up the Two-Shelf Scratchpad to prove the containment $f(A \cap B) \subseteq f(A) \cap f(B)$. Write a complete proof of this inclusion.
\item What property must the function $f$ possess (e.g., injectivity or surjectivity) to guarantee the reverse inclusion $f(A) \cap f(B) \subseteq f(A \cap B)$? Prove your assertion.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
