\section{The Change in Game: From Getting Answers to Giving Reasons}
\chapterline

In the opening pages of this book (\emph{How This Book Should Be Read}), we distinguished between calculating numerical answers and providing deductive proofs. A \emph{deductive proof} is a chain of steps in which each step follows by logic alone from definitions, assumptions, or facts already proved, so that the conclusion cannot be false if the starting points are true. In most earlier mathematics courses---whether school algebra, calculus, or engineering---your training directs your attention toward methods for computing an answer. Given a quadratic equation, you apply the quadratic formula or factor; given a system of equations, you eliminate variables step by step; given a derivative or integral, you reach for a calculation rule.

That computational skill is valuable. Pure mathematics asks you to pause before reaching for a method. It shifts the central question from \emph{what is the answer?} to a different one:

\begin{quote}
\itshape What exact claim is being made here, and why is it logically impossible for it to fail under our assumptions?
\end{quote}

Answering that question requires a different kind of argument than computing a result. Figure~\ref{01:fig-answers-vs-reasons} sets the two kinds of work side by side.

\subsection{Checking numbers versus establishing truth}

Consider a numerical experiment. Add consecutive odd numbers and watch the running totals:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
1 &= 1 &&= 1^2, \\
1 + 3 &= 4 &&= 2^2, \\
1 + 3 + 5 &= 9 &&= 3^2, \\
1 + 3 + 5 + 7 &= 16 &&= 4^2, \\
1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 &= 25 &&= 5^2.
\end{alignat*}

Every total is a perfect square. It is natural to form a conjecture---a proposed statement believed to be true based on patterns, but not yet proved---that for any positive integer $n$ (written $n \in \N$, where $\N = \{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$ denotes the natural numbers):
\[
1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n - 1) = n^2.
\]

You could write a quick script to test this equation for $n = 10$, $n = 1{,}000$, or even the first ten billion natural numbers. Every test will return a match.

In engineering, ten billion successful trials is strong \emph{empirical} evidence (evidence gathered by observation and testing). In pure mathematics, those ten billion checks still leave infinitely many natural numbers untested. Checking specific numbers, no matter how many, cannot prove a claim for every natural number $n$.

Greek mathematicians, from Thales and Pythagoras in the sixth century BCE to Euclid around 300 BCE, are the first we know of to demand general arguments instead of checked examples. They saw that testing cases one at a time can never cover an infinite collection.

Several famous conjectures held for thousands or millions of test cases before failing at a single \emph{counterexample}---a concrete instance where a general claim fails:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fermat's Prime Conjecture (1640)}: Pierre de Fermat noticed that the numbers $F_n = 2^{2^n} + 1$ are prime (whole numbers greater than $1$ with no positive divisors other than $1$ and themselves) for $n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$:
\[
F_0 = 3, \quad F_1 = 5, \quad F_2 = 17, \quad F_3 = 257, \quad F_4 = 65{,}537.
\]
All five are prime. Fermat conjectured that $F_n$ is prime for \emph{every} integer $n \ge 0$. Almost a century later, in 1732, Leonhard Euler factored the next case:
\[
F_5 = 2^{32} + 1 = 4{,}294{,}967{,}297 = 641 \times 6{,}700{,}417.
\]
No prime of this form beyond $F_4$ has ever been found.
\item \textbf{Euler's Sum of Powers (1769)}: A \emph{$k$-th power} is a number of the form $m^k$, such as $2^4 = 16$. Euler conjectured that, for $k \ge 3$, you need at least $k$ positive $k$-th powers to add up to another $k$-th power. For fourth powers, this asserted that $a^4 + b^4 + c^4 = d^4$ has no solutions in positive integers $a, b, c, d$. For more than two centuries, mathematicians found no counterexample, and twentieth-century computer searches turned up nothing. Yet in 1986, using advanced geometric methods instead of a blind search, Noam Elkies found one:
\[
2{,}682{,}440^4 + 15{,}365{,}639^4 + 18{,}796{,}760^4 = 20{,}615{,}673^4.
\]
\item \textbf{The P\'olya Conjecture (1919)}: George P\'olya conjectured that for any integer $n > 1$, at least half of the integers up to $n$ have an odd number of prime factors (counted with repetition, so $12 = 2 \times 2 \times 3$ has three prime factors, which is odd, while $4 = 2 \times 2$ has two, which is even). The conjecture was verified for millions of integers. It holds for every $n$ up to $906{,}150{,}256$. The smallest counterexample appears only at $n = 906{,}150{,}257$.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{01:fig-counterexample-scale} places these three counterexamples on a scale where each step is ten times the one before. All three lie far beyond anything a person could check by hand.

To establish that our odd-number pattern holds across the entire infinite tail in Figure~\ref{01:fig-sampling-vs-proof}, we need a single argument that covers every $n$ at once.

\subsection{Seeing the reason geometrically}

Why \emph{must} the sum of the first $n$ odd numbers equal $n^2$? We do not need a computer to check cases. We can arrange the numbers visually into a square.

Start with a single $1 \times 1$ block. To expand this block into a $2 \times 2$ square, you must wrap around its top and right edges. How many new unit squares are required? Exactly $3$. 

To expand the $2 \times 2$ square into a $3 \times 3$ square, you again wrap an L-shaped band around the outer border. That band consists of two sides of length $2$, plus the single corner block where they meet: $2 + 2 + 1 = 5$ unit squares. Since ancient Greek times, such an L-shaped border has been called a \emph{gnomon}, after the Greek word for a carpenter's square. Figure~\ref{01:fig-gnomon} shows the squares built up this way to $n = 4$.

\caption{Building squares from odd numbers. Each coloured L-shape (gnomon) wraps around the square before it. Going from an $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ square to an $n \times n$ square takes two strips of length $n-1$ plus one corner tile: $2n-1$ tiles.}
\label{01:fig-gnomon}

In general, if you already have an $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ square containing $(n-1)^2$ tiles, completing the $n \times n$ square requires adding:
\begin{align*}
n^2 - (n-1)^2 &= n^2 - (n^2 - 2n + 1) && \text{(expand $(n-1)^2$)} \\
&= 2n - 1 && \text{(the $n^2$ terms cancel)}
\end{align*}
new tiles. Geometrically, this breaks into two arms of length $n-1$ along the top and right, plus $1$ single corner block where they meet:
\[
(n - 1) + (n - 1) + 1 = 2n - 1.
\]
Since $2n - 1$ is the $n$-th odd number ($1, 3, 5, \ldots$), this gnomon shows that adding the $n$-th odd number turns an $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ square into an $n \times n$ square. Starting from one tile and adding $3, 5, \ldots, 2n-1$ in turn therefore builds the $n \times n$ square, so the sum is $n^2$.

We did not need to test individual cases up to $n = 10^{12}$. The argument works for every natural number $n$ at once, because nothing in it depends on which $n$ we picked.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Look at the gnomon equation $n^2 - (n-1)^2 = 2n - 1$. What happens if you add consecutive \emph{even} numbers instead: $2 + 4 + 6 + \cdots + 2n$? Can you picture what shape is formed if you attach one extra column of unit tiles to the side of the square? (Can you see why the sum is $n(n+1)$?)
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{From formulas to structural invariants}

In engineering, formulas often serve as compact input-output converters: Newton's law (force $\vec F$ equals mass $m$ times acceleration $\vec a$), Euler's formula linking the exponential function to cosine and sine (here $i$ is the imaginary unit, with $i^2 = -1$, and $\theta$ is an angle), and a system of linear equations (a matrix $A$ times an unknown vector $\vec x$ gives a known vector $\vec b$):
\[
\vec{F} = m\vec{a}, \qquad e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta, \qquad A\vec{x} = \vec{b}.
\]
In pure mathematics, formulas are often consequences of broader structures. Instead of asking only what numerical output a formula produces, pure mathematics investigates the rules governing the systems where the formula lives:
\begin{itemize}
\item What is a \emph{set} (a well-defined collection of distinct objects), and how do we build collections without running into contradictions? (Some collections defined in terms of themselves turn out to be impossible.)
\item What is a \emph{function}? It is more than a formula like $f(x) = x^2$: it is a rule that assigns to each element of an input set (the \emph{domain}) exactly one output in a target set.
\item What does it mean for two mathematical systems to share the ``same structure''? This is an \emph{isomorphism} (from the Greek \emph{isos} ``equal'' and \emph{morphe} ``form''): a dictionary that matches every element and operation of one system with those of another, with nothing lost in either direction.
\item What properties remain \emph{invariant} (unchanged under transformations, such as stretching, twisting, or coordinate shifts)?
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{01:fig-function-isomorphism} pictures two of these ideas. On the left, the function $f(x) = x^2$ sends each input along exactly one arrow. On the right, hours on a clock with only four positions ($0$, $1$, $2$, $3$, where $3$ hours after $1$ o'clock is $0$ o'clock) are matched with quarter turns of a square. Adding hours on the clock behaves exactly like combining turns, so the two systems have the same structure.

Studying structure this way lets one result cover problems that look unrelated. Results about \emph{groups}---collections of actions, such as the rotations of a square, that can be combined and undone according to fixed rules---explain facts about modular arithmetic (``clock arithmetic'', where you keep only the remainder after dividing by a fixed number), the symmetries of crystals, the roots of polynomial equations, and conservation laws in physics.

\begin{historybox}
Early Greek mathematicians studied numbers by arranging pebbles (\emph{psephoi}) into geometric arrays: triangles, squares, and rectangles. (Our word \emph{calculate} comes from the Latin \emph{calculus}, ``small pebble''.) Laying an L-shaped row of pebbles, named after the carpenter's square, around a square array of pebbles turned it into the next larger square.

Pebble arrangements, like computer tests, only verify finitely many instances. The shift to deductive proof, best known from Euclid's \emph{Elements} (a Greek textbook from about 300 BCE that derives geometry and number facts from a short list of starting assumptions), aimed to cover all infinitely many cases at once by reasoning about the structure instead of counting stones.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{General proofs and the limits of visual intuition}

An argument like our gnomon tiling covers every positive integer $n$ at once, rather than checking values one by one.

Even so, visual and geometric reasoning has a real limitation: \textbf{our eyes can be deceived}. Drawings rely on physical ink and human visual perception, both of which have finite resolution. A slight tilt or a tiny gap can pass unnoticed, making false arguments appear plausible.

To see how visual intuition can mislead, consider the classic dissection paradox shown in Figure~\ref{01:fig-dissection}. An $8 \times 8$ square of area $64$ (the size of a chessboard) is sliced into four pieces: two right triangles (each with legs $3$ and $8$, area $12$) and two trapezoids (each of width $5$ with parallel sides $3$ and $5$, area $20$). When rearranged, the pieces appear to fit together into a $13 \times 5$ rectangle of area $65$.

Where did the extra square unit come from?

The eye sees a clean diagonal line running from $(0,0)$ to $(13,5)$. But calculate the slopes of the pieces that meet along it:
\begin{align*}
\text{slope of the triangle's slanted edge} &= \frac{3}{8} = 0.375, \\
\text{slope of the trapezoid's slanted edge} &= \frac{2}{5} = 0.400.
\end{align*}
Because $\frac{3}{8} \ne \frac{2}{5}$, the pieces do not meet along a straight line. Their edges bend slightly away from the diagonal, so the four pieces (areas $12 + 12 + 20 + 20 = 64$) leave a long, thin parallelogram gap inside the $13 \times 5 = 65$ frame. Subtracting the piece areas from the outer frame gives $65 - 64 = 1$. You can also compute the gap directly: a parallelogram with one corner at the origin and the two neighbouring corners at $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$ has area $\lvert ad - bc \rvert$. The gap has corners $(0,0)$, $(5,2)$, $(8,3)$ and $(13,5)$, so with $(a,b) = (5,2)$ and $(c,d) = (8,3)$ its area is
\[
\lvert 5 \cdot 3 - 8 \cdot 2 \rvert = \lvert 15 - 16 \rvert = 1.
\]
Because the slope difference is only $0.025$, our eyes smooth over the gap and see a solid rectangle. Figure~\ref{01:fig-gap-zoom} stretches the picture vertically to show how thin the gap is: it is never more than $\tfrac{1}{8}$ of a unit tall, and most of it is thinner still. If we accepted pictures alone as proofs, we would be forced to conclude that $64 = 65$.

Pictures also cannot show higher dimensions. We can draw two or three independent directions, but not the ten directions of a ten-dimensional space, let alone the infinitely many needed for some spaces mathematicians study.

When visual intuition reaches its practical boundary, mathematics turns to precise symbolic language. In Chapter~2 (\emph{Mathematical Sentences: Statements, Predicates, and Claims}), we begin building that grammar from the ground up, learning how to distinguish expressions from assertions, open conditions from true-or-false statements, and valid deductive arguments from empirical patterns.

\subsection{Exercises: cultivating three perspectives}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
\item \textbf{Mental Check: Patterns versus Invariants.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Consider the polynomial $P(n) = n^2 - n + 41$. Compute $P(1)$, $P(2)$, $P(3)$ and $P(4)$. Are all four values prime? (In fact, $P(n)$ produces prime numbers for every integer from $n = 1$ up to $n = 40$.) What happens at $n = 41$? Why does this reinforce the lesson of Figure~\ref{01:fig-sampling-vs-proof}?
\item Someone claims that $(n-1)(n-2)(n-3) = 0$ is a universal law for all natural numbers, citing $n = 1, 2, 3$ as evidence. At what value of $n$ does this claim break, and what went wrong with the tester's reasoning?
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Core Practice: Three Perspectives on Odd Numbers.}
For the identity
\[
1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n-1) = n^2,
\]
write down three distinct explanations:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item A concrete numerical verification for $n = 6$.
\item A paragraph in plain English explaining the gnomon diagram (Figure~\ref{01:fig-gnomon}) to someone who has never studied algebra.
\item An inductive step: assuming the identity holds for some positive integer $k$, so that $1 + 3 + \cdots + (2k-1) = k^2$, show algebraically how adding the next odd number, $2(k+1) - 1$, produces $(k+1)^2$. (This step is the main part of a proof method called \emph{induction}, developed later in the book.)
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Core Practice: Summing Consecutive Even Numbers.}
Consider the sum of the first $n$ consecutive even numbers: $2 + 4 + 6 + \cdots + 2n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Calculate the sum for $n = 1, 2, 3, 4$. What pattern emerges in relation to $n(n+1)$?
\item By pairing each odd number in $1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n-1)$ with $1$ extra unit, deduce geometrically why the even sum forms an $n \times (n+1)$ rectangle of area $n(n+1)$.
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Deeper Reflection: The Anatomy of a Visual Illusion.}
In the chessboard dissection paradox (Figure~\ref{01:fig-dissection}), the dimensions $3, 5, 8, 13$ are consecutive terms of the Fibonacci sequence, where each term is the sum of the two before it ($1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, \dots$). Write $F_k$ for the $k$-th Fibonacci number (not the Fermat numbers from earlier), so $F_4 = 3$, $F_5 = 5$, $F_6 = 8$ and $F_7 = 13$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Cassini's identity states that $F_{k-1} F_{k+1} - F_k^2 = (-1)^k$. Check it for $k = 6$ by calculating $F_5 \cdot F_7 - F_6^2 = 5 \cdot 13 - 8^2$. How does the answer relate to the extra unit of area?
\item The two slopes in the paradox are
\[
\frac{F_{k-2}}{F_k} \ \text{(triangle)} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{F_{k-3}}{F_{k-1}} \ \text{(trapezoid)};
\]
for $k = 6$ these are $\frac{3}{8}$ and $\frac{2}{5}$. What happens to these slopes as $k$ grows larger? Does the gap become easier or harder for the human eye to detect?
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{walkbox}
Take an equation or rule of thumb you rely on frequently in your own work. Do you know the precise boundary conditions where it ceases to be true? What hidden assumption prevents it from failing?
\end{walkbox}
