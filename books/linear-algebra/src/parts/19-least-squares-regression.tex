\chapter{Least Squares and Regression}

Take three measurements of two quantities that should follow a straight line, and plot them. They
almost never line up exactly. Hooke's law, for example, says the stretch of a spring is proportional
to the force pulling on it, but measurement errors and effects nobody controlled make real points
scatter around the ideal line. No line passes through all three points. Which line is the
\emph{best} one, and how would you compute it?

This chapter answers both questions. ``Best'' will mean: the line whose total squared miss is
smallest. Computing it will come down to one small square system of equations, the normal equations
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\,\hat{\vec{x}}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$, which Chapter~18 met as the recipe for
projecting onto a subspace. Here we reach the same equations from the data side, by two
independent roads (geometry and calculus), and then use them to fit lines, curves, and the linear
models of statistics and machine learning. Fitting a model to data this way is called
\textbf{linear regression}.

\section{Why \texorpdfstring{$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$}{Ax=b} usually has no solution}

Start with a small example. Suppose you believe two quantities are related by a
straight line $y = c + d\,t$, and you collect three data points:
\[
  (t_1,y_1)=(0,6),\qquad (t_2,y_2)=(1,0),\qquad (t_3,y_3)=(2,0).
\]
If a single line $y=c+dt$ passed through all three, the unknowns $c,d$ would have to satisfy
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    c + d\cdot 0 &= 6,\\
    c + d\cdot 1 &= 0,\\
    c + d\cdot 2 &= 0.
  \end{aligned}
\]
In matrix form $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ with
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 1 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \vec{x}=\colvec{c\\d},
  \qquad
  \vec{b}=\colvec{6\\0\\0}.
\]
There are three equations and only two unknowns. The first equation forces $c=6$. The second
then forces $d=-6$. But the third now reads $6 + 2(-6) = -6 \ne 0$, a contradiction. No line
passes through all three points. The system is \textbf{inconsistent} -- meaning there is no choice of parameters that can
satisfy every equation at once.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The columns of $\mat{A}$ span a two-dimensional subspace (a plane) in $\R^3$:
the span of $\colvec{1\\1\\1}$ and $\colvec{0\\1\\2}$. Every linear combination
$\mat{A}\vec{x}=c\colvec{1\\1\\1} + d\colvec{0\\1\\2}$ lies on this plane.
Because the measurement vector $\vec{b}=\colvec{6\\0\\0}$ does not lie in this
plane, no choice of coefficients $c,d$ can produce $\vec{b}$, and the system
$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ has no exact solution.
\end{intuitionbox}

Figure~\ref{ch19:plane-off} draws this: two columns spanning a sheet, and $\vec{b}$
hovering off it. This is the usual situation when fitting data. Whenever you have \emph{more
equations than unknowns} (more data points than parameters, which is what you want in science), the
matrix $\mat{A}$ is tall and skinny: $m$ rows (one per data point) and $n<m$ columns (one per
parameter). Its column space is a thin subspace of the big output space $\R^m$, and a measured
$\vec{b}$ almost never lands inside it. Demanding an exact solution means demanding that noisy data
fall exactly on the model, which almost never happens.

\begin{trapbox}
``No solution'' from elimination does not mean ``no answer.'' It means there is no \emph{exact}
answer, which for overdetermined data is the usual case. An exact fit to
many noisy points would usually mean your model has so many parameters that it is memorizing noise
(a hazard known as \emph{overfitting}). Inconsistent systems are resolved by orthogonal projection.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} Suppose you have $m$ data points and fit a polynomial of degree $m-1$
(for example, two points and a line $y=c_0+c_1 t$, or three points and a parabola
$y=c_0+c_1 t+c_2 t^2$). Does $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ have a solution?
Yes, always: when the number of parameters $n$ equals the number of distinct data points $m$, the
matrix $\mat{A}$ is square and invertible, so an exact polynomial passes through every point with zero
error. Inconsistency and the need for least squares only begin when $m > n$---when you collect more
measurements than your model has parameters, so the data overconstrains the equations.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{What ``closest'' should mean: the error vector}

We give up on $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ and instead ask: of all the vectors $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ we
\emph{can} produce (all of which lie in the column space), which one is nearest to $\vec{b}$?
Define the \textbf{residual} or \textbf{error vector}
\[
  \vec{e} \;=\; \vec{b}-\mat{A}\vec{x}.
\]
The residual is what the model leaves unexplained: each component $e_i = b_i - (\mat{A}\vec{x})_i$
measures how far the model's prediction $(\mat{A}\vec{x})_i$ (the $i$th entry of $\mat{A}\vec{x}$)
misses the measurement $b_i$. We want to make this whole vector small. But ``small'' in what sense?

The standard choice is to minimize the \textbf{squared length}
\[
  \norm{\vec{e}}^2 \;=\; \norm{\vec{b}-\mat{A}\vec{x}}^2
  \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{m} \bigl(b_i - (\mat{A}\vec{x})_i\bigr)^2 .
\]
This is the sum of the squared misses, and minimizing it is what gives the method its name:
\textbf{least squares}. For the line-fitting problem, $(\mat{A}\vec{x})_i = c+dt_i$ is the height
of the line above $t_i$, so $\norm{\vec{e}}^2 = \sum_i (y_i - c - d t_i)^2$ is the total squared
vertical distance from the data points to the line. We measure \emph{vertical}
distances, not perpendicular distances to the line: in scientific experiments, the input settings
$t_i$ (time, temperature, load) are treated as controlled and known, whereas the output
measurements $y_i$ carry all the random experimental noise.
Figure~\ref{ch19:squared-misses} draws each squared miss $e_i^2$ as the area of a square built on
the vertical miss; least squares makes the total area of these squares as small as possible.

\subsection*{Why squares?}

Why add up \emph{squared} misses, rather than the plain sizes $\abs{e_i}$ of the misses? Three
reasons, and the first two are what make this chapter work.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Geometry.} By Pythagoras, $e_1^2+e_2^2+\dots+e_m^2$ is the squared length of the
        vector $\vec{e}$, which is the squared distance from the point $\vec{b}$ to the point
        $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ in $\R^m$. Squaring does not change which $\vec{x}$ wins (a shorter
        length always has a smaller square), so ``least squares'' means exactly ``the point
        $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ closest to $\vec{b}$''. That turns data fitting into the closest-point
        problem that Chapter~18 solved.
  \item \textbf{Calculus.} A square $e^2$ is a smooth bowl with slope $2e$, so setting slopes to
        zero gives \emph{linear} equations. The size $\abs{e}$ has a sharp corner at $e=0$, where
        it has no slope at all (Figure~\ref{ch19:abs-vs-square}). Minimizing $\sum\abs{e_i}$ is
        possible, but it needs more elaborate methods and has no formula for its answer.
  \item \textbf{Honest cost.} Squaring punishes big misses heavily: a miss twice as large counts
        four times as much. So one wild measurement can drag the whole fit toward it. We return to
        this weakness in the history box below.
\end{itemize}

\section{The geometry: projection onto the column space}

All the achievable outputs $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ form the column space $\col(\mat{A})$, a plane through
the origin (in general, a subspace of $\R^m$). The data $\vec{b}$ is a point off it. Least squares
asks for the point of the plane closest to $\vec{b}$, and Chapter~18 found it: drop a perpendicular.
The foot of the perpendicular is the projection $\vec{p}$ of $\vec{b}$ onto $\col(\mat{A})$; it is
the closest point (Theorem~\ref{ch18:closest-thm}), and the $\hat{\vec{x}}$ we want is the one that
produces it, $\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}=\vec{p}$. Figure~\ref{ch19:projection-picture} shows this for our
three data points, drawn to scale.

Why must the best error be perpendicular to the plane? Suppose the error $\vec{e}=\vec{b}-\mat{A}\vec{x}$
leaned toward one of the columns, meaning its dot product with that column is not zero. Then
sliding $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ a little along that column would bring it closer to $\vec{b}$ (Chapter~18
works this out, Figure~\ref{ch18:lean-fig}). At the best $\hat{\vec{x}}$ no slide helps, so the
error has zero dot product with every column.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} What happens to this picture if $\vec{b}$ already lies \emph{inside} the
column space $\col(\mat{A})$?
Then $\vec{b}$ is already in the plane, so dropping a perpendicular moves it nowhere: the projection is
$\vec{p} = \vec{b}$, and the error vector is $\vec{e} = \vec{0}$. The normal equations become
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}} = \mat{A}\T\vec{b}$, which is automatically satisfied by the exact
solution of $\mat{A}\vec{x} = \vec{b}$. Least squares never distorts a problem that was already
solvable.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Deriving the normal equations}\label{ch19:sec-derive}

There are two roads to the best $\hat{\vec{x}}$: one through the picture, one through calculus.
They meet at the same equations. We take them for the line $y=c+dt$, where the matrix $\mat{A}$ has
two columns: the all-ones column $\mathbf{1}=(1,1,\dots,1)$, which multiplies $c$, and the column of
input values $\vec{t}=(t_1,\dots,t_m)$, which multiplies $d$.

\subsection*{Road 1: geometry (the error is perpendicular to every column)}

The best error $\vec{e}=\vec{b}-\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$ has zero dot product with each column:
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{1}\T\vec{e} &= e_1+e_2+\dots+e_m = 0
     && \text{(the misses cancel out)},\\
  \vec{t}\T\vec{e} &= t_1e_1+t_2e_2+\dots+t_me_m = 0
     && \text{(the misses show no trend in $t$)}.
\end{align*}
In words: the line is not too high or too low overall, and it is not too steep or too shallow.
Stacked, the two conditions say $\mat{A}\T\vec{e}=\vec{0}$. Write out $\vec{e}$:
\begin{align*}
  \mat{A}\T(\vec{b}-\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}) &= \vec{0}
     && \text{(error perpendicular to both columns)}\\
  \mat{A}\T\mat{A}\,\hat{\vec{x}} &= \mat{A}\T\vec{b}
     && \text{(multiply out and move one term across).}
\end{align*}
These are the \textbf{normal equations} of Chapter~18 (``normal'' means perpendicular). Nothing
depended on having two columns. For a model with $n$ parameters, $\mat{A}$ is $m\times n$, there is
one perpendicularity condition per column, and $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is a small square $n\times n$
matrix, however many data points there are.

\subsection*{Road 2: calculus (set the slopes to zero)}

Now forget the picture. The total squared miss of the line $y=c+dt$ is a function of the two numbers
$c$ and $d$:
\[
  E(c,d) = \sum_{i=1}^m \bigl(y_i - c - d\,t_i\bigr)^2 .
\]
At its lowest point, changing $c$ alone cannot lower $E$, and neither can changing $d$ alone. So
both \emph{partial derivatives} (the rate of change of $E$ when one variable moves and the other is
held fixed) are zero there. Differentiate each square by the chain rule (the derivative of $u^2$ is
$2u$ times the derivative of $u$), and recall that $y_i-c-dt_i$ is the miss $e_i$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial E}{\partial c}
    &= \sum_{i=1}^m 2\bigl(y_i-c-dt_i\bigr)\cdot(-1) = -2\sum_{i=1}^m e_i,\\
  \frac{\partial E}{\partial d}
    &= \sum_{i=1}^m 2\bigl(y_i-c-dt_i\bigr)\cdot(-t_i) = -2\sum_{i=1}^m t_i e_i .
\end{align*}
Setting both to zero gives $\sum e_i=0$ and $\sum t_ie_i=0$: word for word the two
perpendicularity conditions of Road~1. The roads do not just reach the same answer; they produce
the same equations.

The same happens for any model. The miss $e_i=b_i-(A_{i1}x_1+\dots+A_{in}x_n)$ changes at rate
$-A_{ij}$ when $x_j$ moves, so
\[
  \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\norm{\vec{b}-\mat{A}\vec{x}}^2
  = \sum_{i=1}^m 2e_i\cdot(-A_{ij})
  = -2\,\vec{a}_j\T\vec{e}.
\]
``The slope of the squared error in the $x_j$ direction is zero'' and ``the error is perpendicular
to column $j$'' are one statement.

Figure~\ref{ch19:error-bowl} draws $E(c,d)$ for our three data points. Each normal equation is a
straight line in the $(c,d)$-plane, the set of points where one partial derivative is zero, and
the best fit is where the two lines cross. Why is that crossing a lowest point, and not a highest
point or a \emph{saddle} (lowest in some directions, highest in others, like a mountain pass)?
Because $E$ is a sum of squares: it is never negative and grows without bound as $c$ or $d$ runs
off, so its graph is a bowl that opens upward. When the columns of $\mat{A}$ are independent the
bowl has exactly one lowest point.

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Least-squares solution.} Let $\mat{A}$ be $m\times n$ with linearly independent columns.
Then $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is invertible (Lemma~\ref{ch18:ata-invertible}), and the unique minimizer of
$\norm{\vec{b}-\mat{A}\vec{x}}^2$ is
\[
  \hat{\vec{x}} \;=\; (\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv \mat{A}\T\vec{b}.
\]
The fitted values are $\vec{p}=\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$, the projection of $\vec{b}$ onto
$\col(\mat{A})$.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Geometry (drop a perpendicular) and calculus (set the slopes of a quadratic to zero) reached the
same matrix equation. This is a general pattern in optimization. Minimizing a smooth function means
finding where its slopes are all zero, and for a \emph{quadratic} function the slopes are linear,
so ``all slopes $=0$'' is a linear system. For functions that are not quadratic, \emph{gradient
descent} repeatedly takes a small step downhill, and \emph{Newton's method} repeatedly replaces the
function by a matching quadratic bowl and jumps to its bottom. For least squares the bowl is exactly
quadratic, so one jump gives the answer.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection*{Reading the normal equations for a line}

Every entry of $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ and $\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$ is a dot product of the columns
$\mathbf{1}$ and $\vec{t}$ with each other and with the data $\vec{b}=(y_1,\dots,y_m)$:
\[
  \mat{A}\T\mat{A}
  = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1}\T\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{1}\T\vec{t}\\ \vec{t}\T\mathbf{1} & \vec{t}\T\vec{t}\end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} m & \sum t_i\\ \sum t_i & \sum t_i^2\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{A}\T\vec{b}
  = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1}\T\vec{b}\\ \vec{t}\T\vec{b}\end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} \sum y_i\\ \sum t_iy_i\end{bmatrix}.
\]
So the first normal equation reads $m\,c+\bigl(\sum t_i\bigr)d=\sum y_i$. Divide by $m$ and write
$\bar{t}$ and $\bar{y}$ for the averages of the $t_i$ and of the $y_i$ (the bar means ``average''):
\[
  c + d\,\bar{t} = \bar{y}.
\]
The best line always passes through the centre of the data, the point $(\bar{t},\bar{y})$. The second
equation, $\bigl(\sum t_i\bigr)c+\bigl(\sum t_i^2\bigr)d=\sum t_iy_i$, then fixes the slope.

\begin{historybox}
On 1 January 1801 Giuseppe Piazzi, at the observatory in Palermo, saw a faint point of light moving
against the stars: Ceres, the largest body in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. He followed
it for about six weeks, across roughly three degrees of sky, until it was lost in the glare of the
Sun. To find it again, astronomers needed its orbit. But six weeks of slightly inaccurate angle
measurements cover only a small arc of an orbit that takes 4.6 years, and the usual methods failed.

Carl Friedrich Gauss, then 24, took up the problem. He devised a new way to compute an orbit from a
few observations and fitted it to Piazzi's data; he later said that least squares, which he had been
using since 1795, was part of that work. At the end of 1801 Franz Xaver von Zach found Ceres within
about half a degree of Gauss's predicted position, and Heinrich Olbers confirmed the sighting
shortly after.

The first to publish the method was Adrien-Marie Legendre, in 1805, in an appendix to a book on the
orbits of comets. He named it the \emph{méthode des moindres quarrés} (method of least squares) and
wrote down the equations for the minimum, which are the normal equations. When Gauss published his
own account in 1809 and said he had used the method since 1795, Legendre objected, and the priority
dispute was never settled. Gauss added a reason from probability: if measurement errors follow the
bell-shaped curve now called the Gaussian (or normal) distribution, least squares gives the most
likely values of the unknowns. (This ``normal'' is a different use of the word from the one in
``normal equations''.)

Roger Boscovich (in 1757) and Pierre-Simon Laplace had proposed minimizing the sum of the
\emph{sizes} of the misses, $\sum\abs{e_i}$, instead. That needs no squaring but gives no linear
equations, and it was impractical before the computational methods of the twentieth century. Least
squares won because its equations are linear. Its weakness is the one noted earlier: squaring lets
a single bad measurement (an \emph{outlier}, a point far from the others) pull the whole fit toward
it. Methods that resist outliers are studied under the name \emph{robust statistics}.
\end{historybox}

\subsection*{When the columns are dependent}

The normal equations have exactly one solution when $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is invertible, which happens
exactly when the columns of $\mat{A}$ are independent (Chapter~18 proves both directions). For a
line fit the columns are $\mathbf{1}$ and $\vec{t}$. They are dependent only when $\vec{t}$ is a
multiple of $\mathbf{1}$, that is, when every measurement was taken at the same input $t_0$. Then
\[
  \mat{A}\T\mat{A}
  = \begin{bmatrix} m & m\,t_0\\ m\,t_0 & m\,t_0^2\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \det(\mat{A}\T\mat{A}) = m^2t_0^2 - m^2t_0^2 = 0 .
\]
The picture says why (Figure~\ref{ch19:dependent-cols}): the points stand in one vertical column,
and every line through $(t_0,\bar{y})$ misses them by exactly the same amounts. The data contain no
information about the slope, so no formula can produce one.

\begin{trapbox}
If the columns of $\mat{A}$ are \emph{dependent} -- say you accidentally fed in the same
\emph{feature} (measured input quantity, one column of $\mat{A}$) twice, or measured temperature in
both Celsius and Fahrenheit -- then $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is
singular and $(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv$ does not exist. The projection $\vec{p}$ is still
well defined (the closest point on the plane is unique), but $\hat{\vec{x}}$ is not unique: many
different parameter vectors produce the same best fit. In statistics this is called
\textbf{collinearity} (or multicollinearity): one feature column is a combination, or nearly a
combination, of the others, so the columns carry redundant information and the data cannot
separate the features' individual effects. The cure is either to drop redundant features or to
add a shrinkage penalty (ridge regression, later in this chapter), which restores invertibility.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Worked example: fitting a line}\label{ch19:sec-worked}

Let us finish the three-point problem from the start of the chapter. We had
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 1 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \vec{b}=\colvec{6\\0\\0},
  \qquad
  \hat{\vec{x}}=\colvec{c\\d}.
\]
Build the two pieces of the normal equations. First $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$:
\[
  \mat{A}\T\mat{A}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1&1&1\\ 0&1&2\end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ 1&1\\ 1&2\end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3\\ 3 & 5\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The top-left entry $3$ is the number of data points; the off-diagonal $3=\sum t_i$ and the
bottom-right $5=\sum t_i^2$. Now $\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$:
\[
  \mat{A}\T\vec{b}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1&1&1\\ 0&1&2\end{bmatrix}\colvec{6\\0\\0}
  = \colvec{6\\0}
  = \colvec{\sum y_i \\ \sum t_i y_i}.
\]
So the normal equations are the tidy $2\times 2$ system
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3\\ 3 & 5\end{bmatrix}\colvec{c\\d} = \colvec{6\\0}.
\]
Solve it. The first row says $3c+3d=6$, that is, $c+d=2$. The second says $3c+5d=0$. Subtract the
first row from the second:
\[
  (3c+5d)-(3c+3d) = 0-6
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  2d=-6,
\]
so $d=-3$, and then $c=2-d=5$. The best-fit line is
\[
  \boxed{\,y = 5 - 3t.\,}
\]
It passes through the centre of the data, $(\bar{t},\bar{y})=(1,2)$, as the first normal equation
promised: $5-3\cdot1=2$.
Let us check the fit. The line predicts $\hat{y}=(5,2,-1)$ at $t=(0,1,2)$, while the data were
$\vec{b}=(6,0,0)$. So the projection and error are
\[
  \vec{p}=\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}=\colvec{5\\2\\-1},
  \qquad
  \vec{e}=\vec{b}-\vec{p}=\colvec{6\\0\\0}-\colvec{5\\2\\-1}=\colvec{1\\-2\\1}.
\]
Two sanity checks confirm the geometry. First, the residual should be orthogonal to both columns
of $\mat{A}$. Against the all-ones column: $1-2+1=0$ (the misses cancel). Against the time column
$(0,1,2)$: $0-2+2=0$ (no trend). So $\vec{e}$ is perpendicular to the plane, as the picture
requires. Second, the squared error is $\norm{\vec{e}}^2 = 1+4+1=6$; no other line does better.
The same numbers appear twice in this chapter's figures: Figure~\ref{ch19:line-fit} shows the data,
the line and the vertical misses in the $(t,y)$-plane, and Figure~\ref{ch19:projection-picture}
shows $\vec{b}$, $\vec{p}$ and $\vec{e}$ as vectors in $\R^3$. Each miss $e_i$ in the first picture
is one coordinate of the perpendicular $\vec{e}$ in the second.

\begin{labbox}
Do the whole thing yourself with a fresh tiny data set: points $(t,y)=(-1,1),(0,1),(1,3)$. Build
$\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$, $\vec{b}=\colvec{1\\1\\3}$, form
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$ and $\mat{A}\T\vec{b}=\colvec{5\\2}$, and
read off $\hat{\vec{x}}=\colvec{5/3\\1}$. The best line is $y=\tfrac53 + t$. Verify the residual
$\vec{e}=\colvec{1\\1\\3}-\colvec{2/3\\5/3\\8/3}=\colvec{1/3\\-2/3\\1/3}$ is orthogonal to both
columns. (The times $t=-1,0,1$ add up to zero, so the two columns of $\mat{A}$ are orthogonal and
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ comes out \emph{diagonal}, which makes the system trivial to solve.
Exercise~\ref{ch19:ex-centering} shows how to arrange this for any data.)
\end{labbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
The entries of the line-fitting normal equations, $m$, $\sum t_i$, $\sum t_i^2$, $\sum y_i$ and
$\sum t_iy_i$, are \emph{sums} you compute in one pass through the data. However many data points
there are, the system you solve is $2\times2$: fitting a line to a billion points means adding up a
billion terms for each of these sums, then solving the same small system as for three points. The
number of data points $m$ affects only the sums; the size of the system is set by the number of
\emph{parameters} $n$. That is what makes regression practical on enormous data sets.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Fitting a parabola, and any other model linear in its parameters}

Nothing about the method needed the model to be a line. Suppose the data look curved and you want
the best parabola $y=c_0+c_1 t+c_2 t^2$. The unknowns are $c_0,c_1,c_2$, and the model
is still \emph{linear in those unknowns} even though it is quadratic in $t$: each unknown is only
multiplied by a known number ($1$, $t_i$ or $t_i^2$) and added, never squared or multiplied by
another unknown. Each data point gives
one equation $c_0+c_1 t_i + c_2 t_i^2 = y_i$, so
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}
    1 & t_1 & t_1^2\\
    1 & t_2 & t_2^2\\
    \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\
    1 & t_m & t_m^2
  \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \vec{x}=\colvec{c_0\\c_1\\c_2},
  \qquad
  \vec{b}=\colvec{y_1\\\vdots\\y_m}.
\]
This tall matrix (where each row contains increasing powers $1, t_i, t_i^2$ of an input sample,
a structure known as a \textbf{Vandermonde matrix}) translates polynomial evaluation into matrix
multiplication. It goes straight into the same normal equations
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\,\hat{\vec{x}}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$. The recipe is identical; only the number of
columns changed.

\begin{example}\label{ch19:parabola}
Fit $y=c_0+c_1t+c_2t^2$ to the four points $(t,y)=(-1,1),(0,1),(1,0),(2,4)$. Build
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 1&-1&1\\ 1&0&0\\ 1&1&1\\ 1&2&4\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \vec{b}=\colvec{1\\1\\0\\4}.
\]
Then
\[
  \mat{A}\T\mat{A}
  =\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 & 6\\ 2 & 6 & 8\\ 6 & 8 & 18\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{A}\T\vec{b}=\colvec{6\\7\\17}.
\]
(Each entry of $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is again a sum of powers of $t_i$: the top-left $4=\sum t_i^0=m$,
the next diagonal $6=\sum t_i^2$, the corner $18=\sum t_i^4$, and so on. The entries of
$\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$ are $\sum y_i=6$, $\sum t_i y_i=7$, and $\sum t_i^2 y_i=17$.) Solving the
$3\times3$ system by elimination gives
\[
  \hat{\vec{x}}=\colvec{c_0\\c_1\\c_2}=\colvec{1/10\\ -1/5 \\ 1}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  y = 0.1 - 0.2\,t + t^2 .
\]
The leading coefficient came out to $1$, so the fitted curve is close to $y=t^2$, with small
constant and linear terms that account for the scatter. The predictions and the residual are
\[
  \vec{p}=\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}
  =\colvec{13/10\\ 1/10\\ 9/10\\ 37/10},
  \qquad
  \vec{e}=\vec{b}-\vec{p}=\colvec{-3/10\\ 9/10\\ -9/10\\ 3/10},
\]
and you can confirm that $\vec{e}$ is orthogonal to all three columns of $\mat{A}$ (each dot product is $0$).
Figure~\ref{ch19:parabola-fit} plots the data, the curve, and these residuals.
The fit is a true parabola, not a line, yet the normal equations stayed linear throughout.
\end{example}

\begin{focusbox}
``Linear regression'' does not mean ``fit a straight line.'' It means \textbf{linear in the
parameters}. You may use any features of your inputs you like -- powers $t,t^2,t^3$, products of
two variables, $\sin t$, $\log t$, a hand-crafted indicator like ``is it a weekend'' -- and as long
as the unknown coefficients enter \emph{linearly}, the fit is a least-squares problem with the same
normal equations. The columns of $\mat{A}$ are your chosen features evaluated at each data point.
This single idea, build a feature matrix and then project, was most of practical machine learning
before neural networks.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Measuring the fit: residuals and \texorpdfstring{$R^2$}{R-squared}}

A best-fit line is only as good as the data allows. How good is it? The answer starts from
the residual vector $\vec{e}=\vec{b}-\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$. Its squared length
$\norm{\vec{e}}^2=\sum e_i^2$ is the \textbf{residual sum of squares}, often written $\mathrm{SS_{res}}$.
Smaller is better, but it has units (squared $y$-units) and scale, so on its own a number like
``$6$'' means nothing. We want a unitless score that says ``how much of the variation in $y$ did
the model explain?''

That score is $R^2$, the \textbf{coefficient of determination}, and it comes from the Pythagorean
theorem. Compare your model against the laziest possible model: predicting the constant
mean $\bar{y}=\tfrac1m\sum y_i$ for every point (the bar means ``average''). The lazy model's error
is the \textbf{total sum of squares}
\[
  \mathrm{SS_{tot}} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^m (y_i-\bar{y})^2,
\]
the raw variation in the data. Then define
\[
  \boxed{\;R^2 \;=\; 1 - \dfrac{\mathrm{SS_{res}}}{\mathrm{SS_{tot}}}
  \;=\; 1 - \dfrac{\norm{\vec{b}-\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}}^2}{\norm{\vec{b}-\bar{y}\,\mathbf{1}}^2},\;}
\]
where $\mathbf{1}=(1,1,\dots,1)$ is the vector of all ones, so $\bar{y}\,\mathbf{1}$ is the lazy
model's vector of predictions.
If the model fits perfectly, $\mathrm{SS_{res}}=0$ and $R^2=1$. If the model does no better than
predicting the mean, $\mathrm{SS_{res}}=\mathrm{SS_{tot}}$ and $R^2=0$. In between, $R^2$ is the
fraction of the variance (the spread of the data around its mean) that the model accounts for.

\begin{intuitionbox}
$R^2$ compares two sums of squared distances. Total variation $\mathrm{SS_{tot}}$
measures how far the data lie from the horizontal line at the mean
$\bar{y}$; residual variation $\mathrm{SS_{res}}$ measures how far the data lie
from the fitted line. Because the model contains the constant mean as a
special case (slope $d=0$), we always have $\mathrm{SS_{res}}\le\mathrm{SS_{tot}}$.
An $R^2$ of $0.9$ means that the model accounts for $90\%$ of the total
variation in $y$, leaving $10\%$ in the residual error.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} Can $R^2$ ever be negative?
Under the standard definition $R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{SS_{res}}/\mathrm{SS_{tot}}$, yes. If you fit a
model without an intercept (forcing the line through the origin) and the true data sit far above the
origin, the fitted line can make larger errors than the simple horizontal average $\bar{y}$. Then
$\mathrm{SS_{res}} > \mathrm{SS_{tot}}$, and $R^2$ drops below zero. A negative $R^2$ indicates
that the model fits the data worse than the simple sample mean $\bar{y}$.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{example}\label{ch19:rsquared}
For our line fit $y=5-3t$ we had $\vec{b}=(6,0,0)$ and residual $\vec{e}=(1,-2,1)$, so
$\mathrm{SS_{res}}=\norm{\vec{e}}^2=6$. The mean is $\bar{y}=(6+0+0)/3=2$, so the deviations from
the mean are $(6-2,\,0-2,\,0-2)=(4,-2,-2)$ and
\[
  \mathrm{SS_{tot}} = 4^2+(-2)^2+(-2)^2 = 16+4+4 = 24.
\]
Therefore
\[
  R^2 = 1-\frac{6}{24} = 1-\frac14 = 0.75 .
\]
The line explains $75\%$ of the variation in this (tiny, noisy) data set, leaving a quarter
unexplained. Figure~\ref{ch19:rsquared-panels} draws the two sums side by side.
\end{example}

distance of the predictions from the mean (the variation the model reproduces).
Figure~\ref{ch19:rsquared-triangle} shows how the three sums fit together, and the box after it
explains why the angle is a right angle.

\begin{connectionbox}
Because the residual $\vec{e}$ is orthogonal to
the column space and the fit $\vec{p}=\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$ lies in it, $\vec{b}=\vec{p}+\vec{e}$
splits into perpendicular pieces, and $\norm{\vec{b}}^2=\norm{\vec{p}}^2+\norm{\vec{e}}^2$.
The same works after subtracting the mean. The all-ones column is one of the columns of $\mat{A}$,
so $\bar{y}\,\mathbf{1}$ also lies in $\col(\mat{A})$, and so does $\vec{p}-\bar{y}\,\mathbf{1}$.
Hence $\vec{b}-\bar{y}\,\mathbf{1}=(\vec{p}-\bar{y}\,\mathbf{1})+\vec{e}$ splits into perpendicular
pieces too, and Pythagoras gives
\[
  \mathrm{SS_{tot}}=\mathrm{SS_{model}}+\mathrm{SS_{res}}.
\]
Statisticians call this splitting of the total variation into an explained part and an unexplained
part the \emph{analysis of variance} (ANOVA); Figure~\ref{ch19:rsquared-triangle} draws it. For a
straight-line fit, the \emph{correlation coefficient} $r$ (a number between $-1$ and $1$ that
measures how closely the points follow a line) is $\pm\sqrt{R^2}$, and it equals the cosine of the
angle between the centered data $\vec{b}-\bar{y}\,\mathbf{1}$ and the centered times
$\vec{t}-\bar{t}\,\mathbf{1}$ (``centered'' means the average has been subtracted).
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{trapbox}
A high $R^2$ does \emph{not} mean your model is correct or useful. You can always raise $R^2$ by
adding more features (more columns to $\mat{A}$): in the extreme, fit $m$ points with an
$(m{-}1)$-degree polynomial and you get $R^2=1$ exactly, since the curve passes through every point.
Yet that curve wiggles wildly between the points and predicts the next data point badly, as
Figure~\ref{ch19:overfit} shows. This is the \textbf{overfitting} mentioned at the start of the
chapter: the model has memorized the random noise instead of the underlying relationship. That is
why practitioners report \emph{adjusted} $R^2$ (a version of $R^2$ that subtracts a penalty for each
extra parameter), hold out test data (points kept aside, not used in the fit, to check the
predictions), or penalize large parameters. $R^2$ measures how well the model fits the data used to
fit it, not how well it predicts new data.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Least squares as the engine of statistics and machine learning}

Everything above was phrased as ``fit a curve,'' but the same algebra is the core of
\emph{supervised learning}: learning to predict a known target quantity from examples where the
answer is given. Here is the translation.

In machine learning you have $m$ training examples. Each example $i$ has a row of
\textbf{features} $\vec{a}_i\T = (a_{i1},\dots,a_{in})$ (the input attributes) and a numerical
\textbf{label} $b_i$ (the target value to predict). Stack the feature rows into the \textbf{design
matrix} $\mat{A}$ (the table of all inputs, with one row per example and one column per feature)
and the target labels into $\vec{b}$ (Figure~\ref{ch19:design-matrix}). A \textbf{linear model}
predicts $\hat{b}_i=\vec{a}_i\T\vec{x}$ using a weight vector $\vec{x}$, so the vector of all
predictions is exactly $\mat{A}\vec{x}$. Training the model to minimize
squared prediction error is \emph{precisely} least squares, and the optimal weights are
\[
  \hat{\vec{x}} = (\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T\vec{b}.
\]
The fitted weights tell you how much each feature pushes the prediction; the residuals tell you
where the model is wrong; $R^2$ tells you how much of the variation you captured. This is
\textbf{linear regression}, the first model in most machine-learning courses and still the model
behind a large share of deployed prediction systems, because it is fast, easy to interpret (each
weight has a plain meaning) and rarely fails in surprising ways.

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Why regression is everywhere.} A bank scoring loan applications stacks each applicant's
features (income, debt ratio, credit age) as a row of $\mat{A}$ and past repayment outcomes as
$\vec{b}$; the weights $\hat{\vec{x}}$ become the scorecard. A climate model fits temperature
against $\mathrm{CO}_2$, solar cycle, and aerosol features. A sports analyst predicts a player's
value from game statistics (points, assists, minutes played). In all of them the computation is the
same projection onto a column space.

Three engineering notes from real practice:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Never invert $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ directly} on a real problem. Forming
  $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ squares the matrix's \textbf{condition number} $\kappa(\mat{A})$ (kappa: the
  largest factor by which $\mat{A}$ stretches a unit vector, divided by the smallest). The
  condition number measures how much \emph{round-off errors} (the tiny errors a computer makes
  because it stores every number to about $16$ significant digits) get magnified in the answer.
  Instead, practical software solves least squares via the
  $\mat{Q}\mat{R}$ factorization (the subject of the next chapter) or the singular value
  decomposition (SVD, Part~VII), which work directly on $\mat{A}$ and are numerically stable
  (rounding errors stay small). The normal equations are how you \emph{understand} least
  squares, not how you compute it on a computer.
  \item \textbf{Collinear features} (nearly dependent columns) make $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ nearly
  singular, so tiny data changes swing the weights wildly. The standard fix is \textbf{ridge
  regression}: pick a positive number $\lambda$ (lambda) that sets how strongly large weights are
  penalized, and minimize $\norm{\vec{b}-\mat{A}\vec{x}}^2 + \lambda\norm{\vec{x}}^2$. Its normal
  equations are $(\mat{A}\T\mat{A}+\lambda\mat{I})\hat{\vec{x}}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$. The added
  $\lambda\mat{I}$ guarantees invertibility and shrinks the weights toward zero.
  \item \textbf{Gradient descent} solves the same problem one step at a time when $\mat{A}$ is
  too large to factor: it walks downhill on the bowl $f(\vec{x})=\norm{\vec{b}-\mat{A}\vec{x}}^2$
  (Figure~\ref{ch19:error-bowl}). The bowl is \emph{convex} (the straight segment between any two
  points on its surface never passes below the surface), so it has no other dips where the walk
  could get stuck. The least-squares bowl is the standard first test problem for the optimization
  methods used in machine learning.
\end{itemize}
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{strangbox}
Strang frames least squares as the meeting point of the whole first half of linear
algebra: it combines the column space, orthogonality (the right angle of the error),
and projections (the matrix $\mat{P}$) into the single normal system
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$. Before Strang, keep the geometric
picture in mind: data vector off the subspace, drop a perpendicular. After Strang,
watch for this same equation in later topics: as the Moore--Penrose
pseudoinverse $\mat{A}^{+}=(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$ (a stand-in for an inverse when
$\mat{A}$ is not square), as the minimum-norm solution via the SVD (the shortest $\vec{x}$
among all least-squares solutions, needed when the columns are dependent), and as the linear
layer of a regression model. Strang
calls $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ the fundamental matrix of applied mathematics, with least
squares providing its primary motivation.
\end{strangbox}

\section{The projection matrix, revisited}

The fitted values are $\vec{p}=\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}=\mat{P}\vec{b}$
where
\[
  \mat{P} \;=\; \mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T.
\]
This $\mat{P}$ is the projection matrix onto $\col(\mat{A})$. As shown in Chapter~18, it
has two defining properties that you can verify directly from its formula:
\[
  \mat{P}\T=\mat{P}\quad(\text{symmetric}),
  \qquad
  \mat{P}^2=\mat{P}\quad(\text{idempotent: projecting twice changes nothing}).
\]
The symmetry check uses $(\mat{X}\mat{Y}\mat{Z})\T=\mat{Z}\T\mat{Y}\T\mat{X}\T$ and the fact that
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is symmetric, so its inverse is too:
\begin{align*}
  \mat{P}\T &= \bigl(\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T\bigr)\T\\
  &= \mat{A}\bigl((\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\bigr)\T\mat{A}\T\\
  &= \mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T = \mat{P}.
\end{align*}
For idempotence, the middle factors cancel:
\[
  \mat{P}^2 = \mat{A}\underbrace{(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T\mat{A}}_{=\;\mat{I}}
  (\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T
  = \mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T = \mat{P}.
\]
Geometrically:
once you have landed on the plane, projecting again does nothing, because you are already there.

\begin{example}\label{ch19:Pmatrix}
For our line-fitting matrix $\mat{A}$ we found
\[
  \mat{A}\T\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad\text{whose inverse is}\qquad
  (\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv=\frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix}5&-3\\-3&3\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Multiplying out
$\mat{P}=\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$ gives
\[
  \mat{P}=\frac16\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 & -1\\ 2 & 2 & 2\\ -1 & 2 & 5\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Check it against the data:
\[
  \mat{P}\vec{b}=\frac16\begin{bmatrix}5&2&-1\\2&2&2\\-1&2&5\end{bmatrix}\colvec{6\\0\\0}
  =\frac16\colvec{30\\12\\-6}=\colvec{5\\2\\-1}=\vec{p},
\]
matching the projection we
computed by solving the normal equations. The rows of $\mat{P}$ each sum to $1$ (it leaves the
all-ones vector unchanged, since that vector lies in the column space), while $\tr\mat{P}
=\tfrac{5+2+5}{6}=2$ equals the rank, the dimension of the plane we projected onto.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The complementary matrix $\mat{I}-\mat{P}$ projects onto the orthogonal complement of the column
space $(\col(\mat{A}))^\perp = \nullsp(\mat{A}\T)$ (the subspace of all vectors perpendicular to
every column of $\mat{A}$, known as the left nullspace), and it sends $\vec{b}$ directly to the
residual: $(\mat{I}-\mat{P})\vec{b}=\vec{b}-\vec{p}=\vec{e}$. So $\mat{P}$ splits every data vector
into ``signal that the model can explain'' ($\mat{P}\vec{b}$, in the column space) plus ``noise it
cannot'' ($\vec{e}$, perpendicular to it). That decomposition $\vec{b}=\mat{P}\vec{b}
+(\mat{I}-\mat{P})\vec{b}$ is least squares in one line.
\end{remark}

\section{Summary}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Summary of core concepts.} When $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ has no solution because
$\vec{b}$ lies outside the column space, project: the best approximate solution $\hat{\vec{x}}$
makes the error $\vec{e}=\vec{b}-\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$ perpendicular to $\col(\mat{A})$,
which gives the normal equations
\[
  \mat{A}\T\mat{A}\,\hat{\vec{x}}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b},
  \qquad
  \hat{\vec{x}}=(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T\vec{b}
  \ \text{ when columns are independent.}
\]
The fit is $\vec{p}=\mat{P}\vec{b}$ with $\mat{P}=\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$
symmetric and idempotent. To fit a line, parabola, or any model linear in its parameters, put your
features in the columns of $\mat{A}$ and solve the normal equations. The coefficient of determination
is $R^2=1-\mathrm{SS_{res}}/\mathrm{SS_{tot}}$. Linear regression is the direct application of
orthogonal projections to observational data.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Solving the normal equations $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$ directly carries
a hidden numerical hazard. As the engineering notes above explained, forming $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$
squares the condition number:
\[
  \kappa(\mat{A}\T\mat{A}) \;=\; \bigl(\kappa(\mat{A})\bigr)^2.
\]
If two features in $\mat{A}$ are nearly collinear, $\kappa(\mat{A})$ might be $10^4$. Squaring it
gives $10^8$, so you can lose about eight of the roughly sixteen significant digits a computer
carries. With worse conditioning, the computed weights can be pure rounding noise.

Can we compute the least-squares projection \emph{without} ever forming $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$? In
Chapter~20, we will see that the answer is yes: the \textbf{Gram--Schmidt process} replaces the
columns of $\mat{A}$ by perpendicular unit vectors (an orthonormal basis for the same column space)
and factors $\mat{A} = \mat{Q}\mat{R}$. Because $\mat{Q}$ has orthonormal columns,
$\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q} = \mat{I}$, and least squares can be solved without $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ and
without the loss of precision.
\end{connectionbox}

\section*{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-mental-twopoints}
\textbf{(Mental check: exact fit vs approximation.)} Suppose you fit a straight line $y=c+dt$ to
only two distinct points $(t_1,y_1)=(1,3)$ and $(t_2,y_2)=(3,7)$. Before writing down any matrix:
what is the residual vector $\vec{e}$? What is $R^2$? Explain why fitting $n$ independent parameters
to $n$ data points never requires least squares.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-mental-colspace}
\textbf{(Mental check: data already in the column space.)} Suppose a vector $\vec{b}$ already lies
in the column space of $\mat{A}$, so that $\mat{A}\vec{x}_0 = \vec{b}$ has an exact solution. What
is the projection $\vec{p}=\mat{P}\vec{b}$? What does the residual vector $\vec{e}$ equal? Verify
that the normal equations $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$ are satisfied by
$\hat{\vec{x}}=\vec{x}_0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-line}
Fit a line $y=c+dt$ by least squares to the four points $(0,1),(1,3),(2,4),(3,4)$. Form
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ and $\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$, solve the $2\times2$ normal equations, and report the
best-fit line. Then compute the residual vector and verify it is orthogonal to both columns of
$\mat{A}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-rsquared}
For your fit in Exercise~\ref{ch19:ex-line}, compute $\mathrm{SS_{res}}$, $\mathrm{SS_{tot}}$
(using the mean $\bar{y}$), and $R^2$. In one sentence, say what the resulting $R^2$ means about
the data.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-horizontal}
Show that fitting the constant model $y=c$ (a horizontal line, so $\mat{A}$ is the single all-ones
column $\mathbf{1}$) by least squares gives $\hat{c}=\bar{y}$, the mean of the data. Conclude that
the mean is the least-squares ``best single number'' summarizing a data set, and explain why this
makes $R^2=0$ for the constant model.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-parabola}
Fit a parabola $y=c_0+c_1 t+c_2 t^2$ to the three points $(-1,1),(0,0),(1,1)$. (With three points
and three parameters the fit should be \emph{exact}; predict before computing that the residual
will be zero, then confirm it by solving and checking $R^2=1$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-centering}
Suppose you shift all the times so they sum to zero (replace $t_i$ by $t_i-\bar{t}$). Show that
then $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} m & 0\\ 0 & \sum t_i^2\end{bmatrix}$ is diagonal, and hence
the best slope and intercept decouple into $\hat{d}=\dfrac{\sum t_i y_i}{\sum t_i^2}$ and
$\hat{c}=\bar{y}$. Explain geometrically why centering the data makes the two columns of $\mat{A}$
orthogonal.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-P-properties}
Let $\mat{P}=\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$ for an $m\times n$ matrix $\mat{A}$ with
independent columns. Prove directly from the formula that (a) $\mat{P}\vec{p}=\vec{p}$ for any
$\vec{p}$ in the column space, and (b) $\mat{P}\vec{v}=\vec{0}$ for any $\vec{v}$ orthogonal to
every column of $\mat{A}$. Conclude that $\mat{P}$ acts as the identity on $\col(\mat{A})$ and as
zero on its orthogonal complement.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-trace-rank}
Using the cyclic property $\tr(\mat{X}\mat{Y})=\tr(\mat{Y}\mat{X})$, show that $\tr\mat{P}=n$ for
the projection matrix of an $m\times n$ matrix with independent columns. Interpret this number
geometrically (what is the dimension of the space $\mat{P}$ projects onto?).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-ridge}
\textbf{(Ridge regression.)} For scalar data, suppose $\mat{A}=\mathbf{1}$ (the all-ones column,
$m$ entries) and we minimize $\norm{\vec{b}-c\mathbf{1}}^2+\lambda c^2$. Derive the modified normal
equation $(m+\lambda)\hat{c}=\sum b_i$, so $\hat{c}=\dfrac{m\,\bar{y}}{m+\lambda}$, where $\bar{y}$ is
the mean of the $b_i$. Show that as
$\lambda\to 0$ you recover the mean, and as $\lambda\to\infty$ the estimate shrinks to $0$.
Explain in words why a penalty $\lambda\norm{\vec{x}}^2$ ``shrinks'' the fit.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-two-features}
\textbf{(Multiple regression.)} You want to predict $b$ from two features $u$ and $v$ with the
model $b\approx x_1 u + x_2 v$ (no intercept). The data are
$(u,v,b)=(1,0,1),(0,1,1),(1,1,3)$. Set up $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$,
$\vec{b}=\colvec{1\\1\\3}$, solve the normal equations for $\hat{\vec{x}}$, and interpret each
weight as the effect of one feature holding the other fixed.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch19:ex-why-squares}
\textbf{(A proof about minimizing.)} Let $\vec{p}$ be the projection of $\vec{b}$ onto a subspace
$S=\col(\mat{A})$, so $\vec{b}-\vec{p}\perp S$. Take \emph{any} other vector $\vec{q}\in S$ and
prove $\norm{\vec{b}-\vec{q}}^2=\norm{\vec{b}-\vec{p}}^2+\norm{\vec{p}-\vec{q}}^2$ (Pythagoras),
hence $\norm{\vec{b}-\vec{q}}\ge\norm{\vec{b}-\vec{p}}$ with equality only when $\vec{q}=\vec{p}$.
This is the rigorous statement that the projection is the unique closest point -- the foundation the
whole chapter rests on.
\end{exercise}
