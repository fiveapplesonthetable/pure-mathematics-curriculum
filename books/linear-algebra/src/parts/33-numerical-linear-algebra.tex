\chapter{Numerical Linear Algebra}

In Chapter~32 we met unitary transforms like the Fourier matrix, where exact algebra
led to fast algorithms. Every formula in this book so far has assumed one thing:
that arithmetic is exact.

We wrote $\inv{\mat{A}}$, $\sqrt{2}$ and $1/3$, and trusted those symbols to mean
precisely what they say. A real computer cannot do that. It stores each number in a
fixed number of bits, so $1/3$ becomes $0.3333333\ldots$ cut off somewhere, $\sqrt{2}$
becomes a nearby fraction, and every arithmetic step introduces a tiny rounding error.
Most of the time those errors stay harmless. Sometimes they accumulate and grow until
the computed answer has no correct digits left.

Numerical linear algebra is the study of how to do linear algebra on a machine
\emph{and trust the result}. It splits into two questions that we will keep apart:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Conditioning} is a property of the \emph{problem}. Some problems are
    delicate by nature: tiny changes to the input force large changes in the true
    answer. Such a problem is called \textbf{ill-conditioned}. No algorithm can rescue
    it, because the input itself is already uncertain (it was measured, or rounded
    when it was stored).
  \item \textbf{Stability} is a property of the \emph{algorithm} (the step-by-step
    method the computer follows). A \textbf{stable} algorithm adds little error of its
    own; an unstable one can wreck even a well-conditioned problem through its own
    rounding.
\end{itemize}

The condition number, defined below, measures the problem. Stability describes the
method. You want both: a well-conditioned problem solved by a stable algorithm.



\section{Floating point: numbers with a budget}

A computer stores a real number the way scientific notation does, but in binary and
with a fixed budget of digits. Such numbers are called \textbf{floating-point numbers}
(the binary point ``floats'' to wherever the exponent puts it), or \emph{floats} for
short. The standard format today is \textbf{double precision}, called
\texttt{float64} in most programming languages because it uses $64$ bits. A
double-precision number looks like
\[
  \pm\, 1.\underbrace{b_1 b_2 \cdots b_{52}}_{\text{52 bits}} \times 2^{e}.
\]
It has three parts: a sign, a $52$-bit fraction $b_1b_2\cdots b_{52}$ (each $b_i$ is a
binary digit, $0$ or $1$), and an exponent $e$, a whole number. The fraction is called
the \textbf{mantissa}. Every nonzero number is first scaled by a power of $2$ so that it
starts with a $1$ before the binary point (just as $0.0042$ is written $4.2\times 10^{-3}$
in scientific notation); that leading $1$ is not stored, and the mantissa holds the
$52$ binary digits after it. Because the mantissa has only $52$ bits, the gap between one
representable number and the next is relative, not absolute: near $1$ the spacing is
$2^{-52}\approx 2.2\times 10^{-16}$, and near $1000$ it is about a thousand times larger
(Figure~\ref{ch33:ruler}). The most important constant in the subject is this spacing
at $1$, called \textbf{machine epsilon}:
\[
  \eps_{\text{mach}} \;=\; 2^{-52} \;\approx\; 2.22\times 10^{-16}.
\]
Machine epsilon is the gap between $1.0$ and the next number the computer can
represent. Adding to $1.0$ any number smaller than half of $\eps_{\text{mach}}$
gives a result that rounds back to $1.0$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of the real line as a ruler whose tick marks get \emph{farther apart} as you
move away from zero, but always stay the same distance apart \emph{relative} to where
you are: about $16$ significant decimal digits everywhere. (The \emph{significant
digits} of a number are its digits starting from the first nonzero one; $0.004217$ has
four.) When you store a number, you snap it to the nearest tick. The snap costs at most
half a tick, so the relative error of storing any number $x$ is bounded:
\[
  \frac{\abs{\text{fl}(x) - x}}{\abs{x}} \;\le\; \tfrac12\,\eps_{\text{mach}}.
\]
Here $\text{fl}(x)$ means ``$x$ rounded to the nearest float.'' Sixteen digits sounds
like plenty. The danger is never one rounding; it is what happens when millions of
them interact.
\end{intuitionbox}

The IEEE~754 standard (the international rulebook for floating-point arithmetic, which
almost every computer follows; IEEE is the Institute of Electrical and Electronics
Engineers) guarantees that the four basic operations are \emph{correctly rounded}: the
computed sum, written $x \oplus y$, equals the true $x+y$ rounded to the nearest float.
In symbols, every operation introduces a tiny relative slip:
\[
  \text{fl}(x \circ y) \;=\; (x \circ y)(1+\delta), \qquad \abs{\delta}\le \tfrac12\eps_{\text{mach}},
\]
where $\circ$ stands for any one of $+,-,\times,\div$ and $\delta$ is the (unknown,
tiny) relative error of that one operation. That factor $(1+\delta)$ is the basic unit
of all rounding analysis. A long computation stacks billions of them, and you want them
to stay small and \emph{not} reinforce one another.

\subsection{Catastrophic cancellation}

Multiplication and division are gentle: each adds one more factor $(1+\delta)$ to the
relative error and nothing worse. Addition is gentle too, \emph{unless you subtract two
nearly equal numbers}. That is where significant digits are lost.

\begin{example}[Subtracting close numbers]
\label{ch33:cancel}
Suppose a machine keeps only $6$ significant decimal digits, and you compute the
difference between two quantities whose true values are
\[
  a = 1.234567, \qquad b = 1.234561.
\]
Before subtracting, the machine must round each number to its $6$-digit budget.
Assuming standard round-to-nearest, $a$ rounds up to $\text{fl}(a) = 1.23457$, while
$b$ rounds down to $\text{fl}(b) = 1.23456$. The machine then subtracts the two stored
numbers:
\[
  \text{fl}(a) - \text{fl}(b) = 1.23457 - 1.23456 = 0.000010 = 1.00000 \times 10^{-5}.
\]
The machine confidently displays six digits. But what is the true difference?
\[
  a - b = 1.234567 - 1.234561 = 0.000006 = 6.00000 \times 10^{-6}.
\]
The computed result is off by nearly $70\%$. The subtraction itself was performed with
zero arithmetic error: $1.23457 - 1.23456$ is exactly $0.00001$. The problem is that the
five leading digits $1.2345$ were identical and subtracted to zero. That cancellation
wiped out the real information and promoted the rounding error in the sixth digit into
the single leading digit of the final answer (Figure~\ref{ch33:cancelfig}).
\end{example}

This is \textbf{catastrophic cancellation}: subtracting two nearly equal numbers wipes
out their shared leading digits and leaves only the rounding error behind. It causes
most severe losses of precision. The subtraction itself is computed correctly; it
exposes the rounding errors that were already sitting in $a$ and $b$. The lesson, which
applies equally to elimination and least squares: \emph{avoid subtracting quantities
that are nearly equal whenever an algebraically equivalent route avoids it.}

\begin{labbox}
The quadratic formula
\[
  x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}
\]
hides this trap. Take $a=1$, $b=-200$, $c=1$. Then
$\sqrt{b^2-4ac}=\sqrt{40000-4}=\sqrt{39996}\approx 199.98999\ldots$. The ``$+$'' root,
$\tfrac{200+199.98999}{2}\approx 199.995$, is fine. But the ``$-$'' root computes
$\tfrac{200-199.98999}{2}$, a subtraction of two nearly equal numbers, and loses most of
its digits. The cure is to compute the safe root $x_1$ first, then get the other from
the product of the roots, $x_1 x_2 = c/a$; here $x_2 = 1/x_1$. Try both ways on a
calculator that shows few digits and compare.
\end{labbox}

\begin{trapbox}
``Double precision has $16$ digits, so I have $16$ good digits.'' No. You \emph{start}
with $16$ digits. Each catastrophic cancellation can erase a clump of them at once. A
computation that subtracts two numbers agreeing in their first $12$ digits keeps about
$4$. The subtraction does not destroy those digits; it reveals that the trailing digits
were already wrong.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose you evaluate $f(x) = \sqrt{x+1} - \sqrt{x}$ when
$x = 10^8$. In $16$-digit floating point, $\sqrt{10^8+1} \approx 10000.00005$ and
$\sqrt{10^8} = 10000$. Subtracting them directly throws away half your significant
digits. How can high-school algebra save the computation?
\emph{Hint:} Multiply and divide by the conjugate $\sqrt{x+1}+\sqrt{x}$ to rewrite
\[
  f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+1} + \sqrt{x}}.
\]
Adding two positive numbers loses nothing; the subtraction was the culprit.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Conditioning: how delicate is the problem?}

Forget computers for a moment. Suppose someone hands you a linear system
$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ and warns you that $\vec{b}$ is only known to within a small
error $\Delta\vec{b}$ (a measurement error, say; $\Delta$ is read ``change in''). How
much can the solution $\vec{x}$ change in response? If a small $\Delta\vec{b}$ can force
a large change $\Delta\vec{x}$, the problem is ill-conditioned, and \emph{no} algorithm
can save you: the answer is uncertain.

Geometrically, solving $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ in $\R^2$ means intersecting two
lines (the two equations). If the lines cross at a wide angle, nudging one
line barely moves the crossing point. But if the two lines are \emph{nearly parallel},
a tiny nudge to either line slides the crossing point a long way
(Figure~\ref{ch33:nearparallel}). That near-parallel picture is what ill-conditioning
looks like.

\subsection{The condition number, made precise}

To put a number on that delicacy, start from $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ and change
the right side to $\vec{b}+\Delta\vec{b}$, giving a new solution
$\vec{x}+\Delta\vec{x}$. Subtracting the two systems gives $\mat{A}\,\Delta\vec{x} =
\Delta\vec{b}$, so $\Delta\vec{x} = \inv{\mat{A}}\,\Delta\vec{b}$.

We need one tool: the \textbf{norm} of a matrix. For a vector, $\norm{\vec{v}}$ is its
length. For a matrix $\mat{M}$, the norm $\norm{\mat{M}}$ is the largest factor by which
$\mat{M}$ stretches any vector:
\[
  \norm{\mat{M}} = \max_{\vec{v}\ne\vec{0}} \frac{\norm{\mat{M}\vec{v}}}{\norm{\vec{v}}},
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  \norm{\mat{M}\vec{v}}\le\norm{\mat{M}}\,\norm{\vec{v}} \ \text{ for every } \vec{v}.
\]
(This is the spectral norm $\norm{\mat{M}}_2$ from the exercises of Chapter~29.)
Applying the inequality twice:
\begin{align*}
  \norm{\Delta\vec{x}} &= \norm{\inv{\mat{A}}\,\Delta\vec{b}}
     \le \norm{\inv{\mat{A}}}\,\norm{\Delta\vec{b}},\\
  \norm{\vec{b}} &= \norm{\mat{A}\vec{x}} \le \norm{\mat{A}}\,\norm{\vec{x}},
  \quad\text{so}\quad
  \frac{1}{\norm{\vec{x}}} \le \frac{\norm{\mat{A}}}{\norm{\vec{b}}}
  \qquad(\vec{b}\ne\vec{0}).
\end{align*}
Multiplying these two inequalities between nonnegative numbers gives a bound on the
relative error (the size of the error divided by the size of the quantity):
\[
  \boxed{\;\frac{\norm{\Delta\vec{x}}}{\norm{\vec{x}}}
   \;\le\; \underbrace{\norm{\mat{A}}\,\norm{\inv{\mat{A}}}}_{\displaystyle \kappa(\mat{A})}
   \;\cdot\; \frac{\norm{\Delta\vec{b}}}{\norm{\vec{b}}}\;.}
\]
The factor in the middle is the \textbf{condition number}
$\kappa(\mat{A}) = \norm{\mat{A}}\,\norm{\inv{\mat{A}}}$ ($\kappa$ is the Greek letter
kappa). It is the largest factor by which a relative error in the input $\vec{b}$ can be
multiplied into a relative error in the output $\vec{x}$.

\begin{formalbox}
For the $2$-norm (the standard Euclidean length), the matrix norm $\norm{\mat{A}}$ equals
the largest singular value $\sigma_{\max}$, and $\norm{\inv{\mat{A}}} = 1/\sigma_{\min}$,
where $\sigma_{\min}$ is the smallest singular value. So
\[
  \kappa_2(\mat{A}) \;=\; \frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}},
\]
the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value of $\mat{A}$ (the subscript $2$
records which norm is used). A condition number near $1$ is ideal; orthogonal matrices
have $\kappa_2 = 1$ exactly, because they preserve all lengths. A condition number of
$10^{k}$ means you can expect to lose about $k$ decimal digits of accuracy: if
$\kappa_2(\mat{A})\approx 10^{8}$ and your machine begins with $16$ digits, you can trust
only about $16 - 8 = 8$ digits of the computed solution.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Recall from Chapter~29 that $\mat{A}$ maps the unit sphere onto an ellipsoid whose
semi-axes (half-axis lengths) are the singular values $\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_n$.
The condition number $\kappa_2(\mat{A}) = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ is the
\emph{aspect ratio} of that ellipsoid, its longest axis divided by its shortest
(Figure~\ref{ch33:condellipse}). A nearly round ellipsoid means $\mat{A}$ stretches
every direction about equally, and undoing it is safe. When
$\sigma_{\max} \gg \sigma_{\min}$ ($\gg$ means ``much larger than''), $\mat{A}$
flattens the unit sphere into a long thin needle. To solve $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$, the
inverse $\inv{\mat{A}}$ must stretch that needle back into a sphere, so any small
error in the needle's thin direction is magnified by the large factor
$1/\sigma_{\min}$. Conditioning is the shape of the SVD ellipsoid.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{example}[A mildly ill-conditioned $2\times 2$]
\label{ch33:cond2x2}
Take
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.001 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The two rows are \emph{almost} the same -- two nearly parallel lines. Its determinant
is $1\cdot 1.001 - 1\cdot 1 = 0.001$, tiny, so the inverse is large:
\[
  \inv{\mat{A}} = \frac{1}{0.001}\begin{bmatrix} 1.001 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
   = \begin{bmatrix} 1001 & -1000 \\ -1000 & 1000 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Here is the amplification in numbers. With $\vec{b}=\colvec{2\\2.001}$ the exact
solution is $\vec{x}=\colvec{1\\1}$. Change the second entry of $\vec{b}$ by
$0.001$ to $\vec{b}'=\colvec{2\\2.002}$:
\begin{align*}
  \vec{x}' &= \inv{\mat{A}}\vec{b}' \\
           &= \colvec{1001\cdot 2 - 1000\cdot 2.002 \\ -1000\cdot 2 + 1000\cdot 2.002} \\
           &= \colvec{0\\2}.
\end{align*}
Measured by length, $\vec{b}$ changed by about $0.035\%$
($\norm{\Delta\vec{b}}/\norm{\vec{b}} = 0.001/2.83$), yet the answer moved from $(1,1)$ to
$(0,2)$, a $100\%$ change ($\norm{\Delta\vec{x}}/\norm{\vec{x}} = \sqrt2/\sqrt2$). The
relative error was multiplied by about $2800$. The condition number here is
$\kappa_2(\mat{A})\approx 4000$, so this perturbation came close to the worst case the
bound allows.
\end{example}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose you model how heat spreads through a metal bar by
tracking the temperature at many evenly spaced points, which turns the physics into a
linear system $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$, and that $\mat{A}$ has singular values $\sigma_{\max} = 100$ and
$\sigma_{\min} = 10^{-4}$. What is $\kappa_2(\mat{A})$? If you solve $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$
using standard double-precision arithmetic ($16$ decimal digits), how many significant
digits can you trust in the solution vector $\vec{x}$?
\emph{Answer:} $\kappa_2(\mat{A}) = 100 / 10^{-4} = 10^6$. Since $\kappa_2 \approx 10^6$,
input uncertainties or internal roundings can be amplified by up to a factor of one
million, wiping out $6$ decimal digits of precision. Out of your initial $16$ digits,
you should expect to retain about $16 - 6 = 10$ trustworthy digits.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{trapbox}
A small determinant does \emph{not} mean ill-conditioned, and a large one does not
mean well-conditioned: the determinant measures volume, not shape. Scale $\mat{A}$ by
$\tfrac{1}{10}$ and its determinant drops by $10^{n}$, yet $\kappa(\mat{A})$ does not
change at all, because both $\norm{\mat{A}}$ and $\norm{\inv{\mat{A}}}$ rescale and the
factors cancel. Conditioning is about the \emph{ratio} of stretches
$\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$, not the overall volume $\det$. Judge sensitivity by
$\kappa$, never by $\det$.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{walkbox}
The Hilbert matrix has entries $H_{ij}=1/(i+j-1)$ in row $i$, column $j$: harmless-looking
fractions $1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13,\ldots$, each constant along a line running from lower
left to upper right. Its condition number grows explosively: about $500$ for
$3\times 3$, roughly half a million for $5\times 5$, about $1.6\times 10^{13}$ by
$10\times 10$, and past $10^{16}$ (where double precision has no correct digits left)
at $12\times 12$ (Figure~\ref{ch33:hilbert}). On a walk, ask yourself why smooth, slowly
varying matrices like this, whose rows look nearly proportional, should be the dangerous
ones, while a matrix of random $\pm 1$ entries is usually well-conditioned. (Hint: think
about how parallel the rows are.)
\end{walkbox}

\section{Stability: elimination versus QR}

The condition number is about the problem. Now we ask about the \emph{algorithm}. Even
a perfectly well-conditioned system can be wrecked by a careless method. The classic
example is Gaussian elimination without pivoting.

\begin{historybox}
In 1947, when the first electronic computers such as the room-sized ENIAC were new, John
von Neumann and Herman Goldstine published the paper \emph{Numerical Inverting of
Matrices of High Order}. Before electronic computers, linear systems were solved by teams
of people (then called ``computers'') working mechanical desk calculators; even a
$10 \times 10$ system took weeks of error-prone hand work. Electronic computers promised
to solve $100 \times 100$ systems in minutes.

Many mathematicians doubted that large systems could be solved accurately at all.
Norbert Wiener, among others, predicted that computers would be useless for large linear
systems. The fear was that the rounding error made at each elimination step would double
from row to row, growing like $2^n$ and erasing all significant digits after twenty or
thirty equations. Von Neumann and Goldstine introduced the condition number
$\kappa(\mat{A}) = \norm{\mat{A}}\,\norm{\inv{\mat{A}}}$ to bound the error growth, but
their estimates were pessimistic. Alan Turing, working on the Pilot ACE, an early British
computer, suspected that keeping the pivots large would keep the errors under control.

The breakthrough came around 1960 from James H. Wilkinson, Turing's former colleague at
the National Physical Laboratory. Wilkinson invented \textbf{backward error analysis}:
instead of asking how far the computed solution $\hat{\vec{x}}$ (the hat marks a computed
value) is from the true solution $\vec{x}$, he asked \emph{which slightly changed problem
the machine had solved exactly}. He proved that Gaussian elimination with partial
pivoting (swapping rows to put the largest available entry in the pivot position) gives
the exact solution of $(\mat{A} + \mat{E})\hat{\vec{x}} = \vec{b}$, where the error matrix
$\mat{E}$ is about $\eps_{\text{mach}}$ times the size of $\mat{A}$, multiplied by the
\emph{growth factor} (how much larger the entries become during elimination than they
were in $\mat{A}$). As long as that growth factor stays moderate, elimination does not
create large errors of its own; it only exposes the sensitivity already present in the
matrix. This analysis turned matrix computation into a reliable science and shaped
LINPACK and LAPACK, the libraries of linear-algebra routines that most scientific
software still calls today.

A puzzle remains open. With partial pivoting, the growth factor can in theory reach
$2^{n-1}$ (for specially built matrices with a pattern of $\pm1$ entries), which for a
$100 \times 100$ system would destroy all accuracy. Yet in over seventy years of real
computation this has almost never happened; in practice the growth factor typically
grows only like about $n^{2/3}$. Researchers still study why partial pivoting is so
reliable on typical matrices, using \textbf{smoothed analysis} (studying how an algorithm
behaves when its input is changed by small random amounts) and random matrix theory (the
study of matrices whose entries are chosen at random).
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Why elimination needs pivoting}

Recall elimination subtracts a multiple of one row from another to create zeros. The
multiplier is $m = (\text{entry to eliminate})/(\text{pivot})$. If the pivot is tiny,
$m$ is enormous, and multiplying a row by an enormous number before subtracting it
swamps the original entries, which then disappear in the rounding (often called
\emph{roundoff}).

\begin{example}[A tiny pivot ruins a benign system]
\label{ch33:pivot}
Solve, on a machine carrying only $3$ significant digits,
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 0.0001 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} =
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
This system is perfectly well-conditioned; the true answer is about
$x_1 = 1.0001\ldots,\ x_2 = 0.9999\ldots$, which rounds to $(1.00, 1.00)$ to three
digits.

\textbf{Without pivoting}, the pivot is $0.0001$ and the multiplier is
$m = 1/0.0001 = 10000$. Eliminating the second row:
\[
  \begin{gathered}
  \text{row}_2 \leftarrow \text{row}_2 - 10000\cdot\text{row}_1:\\
  (1 - 10000\cdot 0.0001)\,x_1 + (1 - 10000\cdot 1)\,x_2 = 2 - 10000\cdot 1.
  \end{gathered}
\]
The new $x_2$ coefficient is $1 - 10000 = -9999$, and the right side is $2-10000=-9998$.
On a machine with only $3$ significant digits, $-9999$ rounds to $-1.00 \times 10^4 =
-10000$, and $-9998$ rounds to $-1.00 \times 10^4 = -10000$. The original ``$1$'' and
``$2$'' are lost in the rounding. The machine then computes:
\[
  x_2 = \frac{-10000}{-10000} = 1.00,
\]
and back-substitution gives
\[
  x_1 = \frac{1 - 1\cdot 1.00}{0.0001} = \frac{0}{0.0001} = 0.
\]
The computed answer is $(0, 1.00)$: the first coordinate is off by $100\%$, on a
problem that was never delicate.

\textbf{With partial pivoting}, you first \emph{swap rows} so that the largest-magnitude
entry in the first column, the $1$, sits in the pivot position:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0.0001 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} =
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Now the multiplier is $m = 0.0001/1 = 0.0001$, which is small. Eliminating:
the $x_2$ coefficient becomes $1 - 0.0001 = 0.9999$, which rounds to $1.00$ in $3$
digits, and the right side becomes $1 - 0.0001\cdot 2 = 0.9998$, which also rounds to
$1.00$. Thus $x_2 = 1.00/1.00 = 1.00$, and back-substitution yields
$x_1 = 2 - 1.00 = 1.00$. The computed solution $(1.00, 1.00)$ is correct to every digit
of available precision. Same problem, same $3$-digit machine: reordering the
equations saved the answer.
\end{example}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Partial pivoting:} before eliminating in a column, swap rows so the
largest-magnitude available entry becomes the pivot. This keeps every multiplier
$\abs{m}\le 1$, so no row is multiplied by a large number before being subtracted. It
is cheap (a row swap), and it is why \texttt{A\textbackslash b} in MATLAB and
\texttt{numpy.linalg.solve} are reliable in practice. Plain LU \emph{without} pivoting
is unsafe in floating point; LU \emph{with} partial pivoting,
$\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ with $\mat{P}$ the permutation matrix that records the
row swaps (Chapter~9), is the standard.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{Backward stability, in one sentence}

Pivoted Gaussian elimination is \textbf{backward stable}: the computed answer
$\hat{\vec{x}}$ it returns is the \emph{exact} solution of a slightly changed system
$(\mat{A}+\mat{E})\hat{\vec{x}} = \vec{b}$, where $\mat{E}$ is tiny relative to $\mat{A}$.
That is the best one can ask of an algorithm in floating point. You did not solve your
problem exactly; you solved, exactly, a problem within rounding distance of yours.
Whether the \emph{answer} is then accurate depends on the conditioning: a stable
algorithm on an ill-conditioned problem still gives a large error, but that error comes
from the problem, not the method.

Two names make this precise (Figure~\ref{ch33:backward}). The \textbf{forward error} is
how wrong the answer is, $\norm{\hat{\vec{x}}-\vec{x}}/\norm{\vec{x}}$. The
\textbf{backward error} is how much the problem had to be changed for $\hat{\vec{x}}$ to
be exactly right, roughly $\norm{\mat{E}}/\norm{\mat{A}}$. They are linked by the rule
of thumb every numerical analyst carries:
\[
  \text{forward error} \;\lesssim\; \kappa(\mat{A}) \times \text{backward error},
\]
where $\lesssim$ means ``is at most about''. A backward-stable algorithm makes the
backward error about $\eps_{\text{mach}}$, so the forward error is about
$\kappa(\mat{A})\,\eps_{\text{mach}}$.

\subsection{Why QR provides numerical stability}

There is a class of matrices with condition number exactly $1$: orthogonal matrices
$\mat{Q}$, with $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$. They are rotations and reflections; they
preserve length, $\norm{\mat{Q}\vec{x}}=\norm{\vec{x}}$, so they cannot amplify any
error. That is why orthogonal transformations are the safest tools in numerical
computing: \emph{they never make a vector's relative error worse.}

The QR factorization $\mat{A}=\mat{Q}\mat{R}$ uses this. In practice it is computed not
by Gram--Schmidt but by more stable methods built entirely from orthogonal steps:
Householder reflections (Chapter~20) or Givens rotations, each of which rotates in just
two coordinates at a time to zero out one entry. To solve
$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ you write $\mat{Q}\mat{R}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$, multiply by
$\mat{Q}\T$ (safe, because $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$ and $\mat{Q}\T$ stretches
nothing) to get
$\mat{R}\vec{x}=\mat{Q}\T\vec{b}$, then back-substitute through the triangular $\mat{R}$.
The orthogonal step transports the data without inflating any error
(Figure~\ref{ch33:orthogonal}).

\begin{strangbox}
Strang teaches Gaussian elimination first because it is how you compute by hand and how
$\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ is born. He then introduces $\mat{A}=\mat{Q}\mat{R}$ for
least squares. The numerical lesson, which a first pass through Strang can leave
implicit: for solving square systems, pivoted LU is fast and stable enough; but for
\emph{least squares} $\min\norm{\mat{A}\vec{x}-\vec{b}}$, never form the normal
equations $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$ on a computer, because
$\kappa(\mat{A}\T\mat{A}) = \kappa(\mat{A})^2$ -- you square the condition number and
can lose twice as many digits. Solve via QR (or the SVD) instead.
\end{strangbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Why your machine-learning library uses QR/SVD.} A linear
regression with $p$ features fits $\min_{\vec\beta}\norm{\mat{X}\vec\beta - \vec{y}}$.
The textbook ``closed form'' $\vec\beta = (\mat{X}\T\mat{X})\inv\mat{X}\T\vec{y}$ is
mathematically correct and numerically risky: when features are correlated (as real
features almost always are), $\mat{X}$ is ill-conditioned, and $\mat{X}\T\mat{X}$ squares
its condition number. That is why \texttt{LinearRegression} in \texttt{scikit-learn} (a
widely used Python machine-learning library) calls LAPACK's SVD-based least-squares
routine, not the normal-equation formula. It is also why \emph{ridge regression}
(Chapter~27), which adds $\lambda\mat{I}$ to $\mat{X}\T\mat{X}$ for a small positive
number $\lambda$, helps: it raises every eigenvalue of $\mat{X}\T\mat{X}$ by $\lambda$,
lifting the smallest ones away from zero, which cuts the condition number and steadies
the fit. Regularization is, in part, a conditioning fix.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Iterative methods: solving without inverting}

Everything so far has been \emph{direct}: a finite recipe (elimination, QR) that
produces the answer after a fixed number of steps, roughly $\tfrac23 n^3$ for an
$n\times n$ system. For $n=10^6$ (a million unknowns, routine in scientific computing
and graphics) that is about $10^{18}$ operations, and storing every entry of the matrix
means $10^{12}$ numbers, more than fits in a computer's memory. Direct methods cannot
cope at that size.

But the giant matrices of practice are almost always \textbf{sparse}: mostly zeros, a
handful of nonzeros per row, because each unknown is linked only to its few neighbors (a
pixel to adjacent pixels, a node of a network to adjacent nodes). For these we use
\textbf{iterative methods}, which start from a guess and improve it over and over. They
never modify $\mat{A}$ and never form $\inv{\mat{A}}$; they only multiply $\mat{A}$ by
vectors, which is cheap when $\mat{A}$ is sparse (only the nonzero entries cost work).
They generate a sequence of guesses $\vec{x}^{(0)},
\vec{x}^{(1)},\vec{x}^{(2)},\ldots$ that gets closer to $\vec{x}$ at every step. The
superscript $(k)$ is a step counter, not a power: $\vec{x}^{(k)}$ is the guess after $k$
steps.

\subsection{Jacobi: everyone updates from last round}

Split $\mat{A}$ into its diagonal part $\mat{D}$ (the diagonal entries of $\mat{A}$,
zeros elsewhere) and the rest $\mat{R}=\mat{A}-\mat{D}$.
The system $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ becomes $\mat{D}\vec{x} = \vec{b}-\mat{R}\vec{x}$,
and if we already had $\vec{x}$ on the right we could read off $\vec{x}$ on the left.
We do not, so we iterate: plug in the current guess on the right to get the next guess
on the left,
\[
  \vec{x}^{(k+1)} = \inv{\mat{D}}\bigl(\vec{b} - \mat{R}\,\vec{x}^{(k)}\bigr).
\]
Component by component the recipe is: solve the $i$-th equation for $x_i$, treating
every \emph{other} unknown as known from the previous round. With $a_{ij}$ the entries
of $\mat{A}$ and $b_i$ those of $\vec{b}$,
\[
  x_i^{(k+1)} = \frac{1}{a_{ii}}\Bigl(b_i - \sum_{j\ne i} a_{ij}\,x_j^{(k)}\Bigr).
\]

\begin{example}[Three Jacobi sweeps by hand]
\label{ch33:jacobi}
Solve
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} = \colvec{1\\7},
  \qquad\text{true answer } \vec{x}\approx\colvec{-0.1111 \\ 1.4444}.
\]
The Jacobi updates are $x_1 \leftarrow (1 - x_2)/4$ and $x_2 \leftarrow (7-2x_1)/5$,
where $\leftarrow$ means ``replace by''. One \emph{sweep} applies both updates once.
Start at $\vec{x}^{(0)}=(0,0)$:
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  x_1^{(1)} &= (1-0)/4 = 0.25, & x_2^{(1)} &= (7-0)/5 = 1.4,\\
  x_1^{(2)} &= (1-1.4)/4 = -0.1, & x_2^{(2)} &= (7-2\cdot 0.25)/5 = 1.3,\\
  x_1^{(3)} &= (1-1.3)/4 = -0.075, & x_2^{(3)} &= (7-2\cdot(-0.1))/5 = 1.44.
  \end{aligned}
\]
After three sweeps we are at $(-0.075,\,1.44)$, approaching $(-0.11,\,1.44)$. Each
sweep used only the previous sweep's numbers, so all the updates in a sweep can be
computed at the same time, on separate processors.
\end{example}

\subsection{Gauss-Seidel: use the news as soon as you hear it}

Jacobi wastes information: while computing the new $x_2$ it still uses the old
$x_1$, even though a new $x_1$ was computed earlier in the same sweep. Gauss--Seidel
uses each new component as soon as it is available:
\[
  x_i^{(k+1)} = \frac{1}{a_{ii}}\Bigl(b_i - \sum_{j<i} a_{ij}\,x_j^{(k+1)}
     - \sum_{j>i} a_{ij}\,x_j^{(k)}\Bigr).
\]
The only change from Jacobi is the superscript on the first sum: $j<i$ uses the
already-updated values $\vec{x}^{(k+1)}$. Redo the example: $x_1^{(1)}=(1-0)/4=0.25$ as
before, but now $x_2^{(1)}=(7-2\cdot 0.25)/5 = 1.3$ immediately uses the fresh $0.25$.
Next sweep $x_1^{(2)}=(1-1.3)/4=-0.075$, then $x_2^{(2)}=(7-2\cdot(-0.075))/5=1.43$.
After two sweeps Gauss--Seidel sits at $(-0.075,1.43)$, where Jacobi needed three. It
typically converges about twice as fast, but the updates must be done one after
another (each component waits for the one before it).

\begin{intuitionbox}
Both methods are called \emph{relaxation} methods: each sweep nudges every unknown so
that its own equation is satisfied, given everyone else's current value. Like a roomful
of people each adjusting to their neighbors, the whole configuration settles toward the
state that satisfies all equations at once. Gauss--Seidel lets each person hear the
latest news before adjusting, so the room settles faster.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{formalbox}
These iterations converge for \emph{any} starting guess exactly when the
\textbf{spectral radius} of the iteration matrix (its largest eigenvalue in absolute
value, Chapter~28) is below $1$. For Jacobi the iteration matrix is
$\mat{M}=-\inv{\mat{D}}\mat{R}$, and
\[
  \vec{x}^{(k+1)}-\vec{x} = \mat{M}\bigl(\vec{x}^{(k)}-\vec{x}\bigr),
\]
so the error is multiplied by $\mat{M}$ each step and shrinks to zero exactly when every
eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $\mat{M}$ has $\abs{\lambda}<1$. A simple sufficient condition
you can check by eye is \textbf{strict diagonal dominance},
$\abs{a_{ii}} > \sum_{j\ne i}\abs{a_{ij}}$ for every row -- each diagonal entry bigger
than the rest of its row combined -- guarantees both Jacobi and Gauss--Seidel converge.
Our $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\2&5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ qualifies: $4>1$ and
$5>2$.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Iterative methods are not guaranteed to converge. If $\mat{A}$ is not diagonally
dominant (or, more generally, if the iteration matrix has spectral radius $\ge 1$),
Jacobi and Gauss--Seidel can stall or \emph{diverge to infinity}. Always check
diagonal dominance, or a related property such as positive definiteness, before
trusting them, and watch the residual $\norm{\mat{A}\vec{x}^{(k)}-\vec{b}}$ to see that
it shrinks.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Look at the symmetric matrix
$\mat{A} = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Can you
guarantee that Jacobi or Gauss--Seidel iteration will converge for
$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$?
\emph{Answer:} Check strict diagonal dominance row by row. Row 1 has
$\abs{a_{11}} = 3 > 2 = \abs{a_{12}}$, which passes. But row 2 has
$\abs{a_{22}} = 1 < 2 = \abs{a_{21}}$, which fails. In fact, the Jacobi iteration matrix
\begin{align*}
  \mat{M} &= -\inv{\mat{D}}\mat{R} \\
          &= -\begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \\
          &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2/3 \\ -2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
has eigenvalues $\lambda = \pm\sqrt{4/3} \approx \pm 1.155$. Because its spectral radius
exceeds $1$, the error grows each step and the iteration diverges.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Conjugate gradient}

For the most important case, $\mat{A}$ symmetric positive definite, there is a method
far faster than Jacobi and Gauss--Seidel: the \textbf{conjugate gradient} (CG) method.
It is easiest to understand through geometry.

When $\mat{A}$ is symmetric positive definite, solving $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ is
\emph{exactly} the same as minimizing the bowl-shaped quadratic
\[
  \phi(\vec{x}) = \tfrac12\,\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x} - \vec{b}\T\vec{x}.
\]
Here $\phi$ (the Greek letter phi) takes a vector and returns a number. Its gradient
(the vector of partial derivatives, Chapter~27) is $\nabla\phi = \mat{A}\vec{x}-\vec{b}$,
which is zero exactly at the solution. So solving the linear system \emph{is} finding the
bottom of a bowl. Because $\mat{A}$ is positive definite, the bowl curves up in every
direction. Its \emph{level sets} (the sets of points where $\phi$ has one fixed value,
like contour lines on a map) are nested ellipsoids, stretched into a long valley when
$\mat{A}$ is ill-conditioned.

The obvious way down is \emph{steepest descent}: always step in the direction of
steepest decrease (the negative gradient). On a round bowl that goes straight to the
bottom, but in a long ellipsoidal valley it zig-zags, each step partly undoing the
progress of the last (Figure~\ref{ch33:cg}, red). Conjugate gradient instead picks
search directions $\vec{p}_1,\vec{p}_2,\ldots$ that are \textbf{$\mat{A}$-orthogonal}
(also called \emph{conjugate}, which gives the method its name):
\[
  \vec{p}_i\T\mat{A}\,\vec{p}_j=0 \qquad\text{for } i\ne j.
\]
Geometrically, change coordinates to $\vec{y}=\mat{A}^{1/2}\vec{x}$, where
$\mat{A}^{1/2}$ is the positive definite square root of $\mat{A}$ from Chapter~27. In
these coordinates the stretched bowl becomes perfectly round, and
\[
  \vec{p}_i\T\mat{A}\vec{p}_j=(\mat{A}^{1/2}\vec{p}_i)\cdot(\mat{A}^{1/2}\vec{p}_j)=0
\]
says the search directions are ordinary perpendicular directions in the round bowl.
With such directions, a step that finishes the job along one direction never spoils
another, so there is no zig-zag and no backtracking. In exact arithmetic CG reaches the
exact solution of an $n\times n$ system in at most $n$ steps, and usually gets close in
far fewer.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Steepest descent is like walking downhill in a narrow canyon by always facing the
steepest local slope: you bounce from wall to wall and creep forward. Conjugate gradient
is like realizing the canyon has a few main directions and dealing with each one
\emph{once and for all}, so you reach the bottom in a handful of moves. The number of
steps needed for a given accuracy grows like $\sqrt{\kappa(\mat{A})}$. That is why
\emph{preconditioning} (multiplying the system by a cheap approximate inverse of
$\mat{A}$ to shrink $\kappa$) is what makes CG practical on real problems.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Conjugate gradient and its relatives do much of the work in large-scale computation.
When a graphics engine simulates cloth or smoke, when engineers test an aircraft part by
finite-element analysis (cutting the part into many small pieces and writing one
equation per piece), or when a recommendation system solves a sparse linear system in
millions of unknowns, there is almost always one of these solvers inside the loop. They
are called \emph{Krylov-subspace methods} because each step searches within the span of
$\vec{b}, \mat{A}\vec{b}, \mat{A}^2\vec{b}, \ldots$ (that span is a Krylov subspace). CG
is used for symmetric positive definite problems and GMRES (generalized minimal residual)
for general ones. They win because they use $\mat{A}$ only through matrix-vector
products, which skip the zero entries and can be split across thousands of processors;
a dense $\inv{\mat{A}}$ allows neither.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{The power method: finding the top eigenvalue}

Iteration also works for eigenvalues. The simplest eigenvalue algorithm, the
\textbf{power method}, computes the eigenvalue of largest absolute value (the
\emph{dominant} eigenvalue) and its eigenvector, using only repeated matrix-vector
multiplication.

The idea: start with almost any vector $\vec{v}^{(0)}$ and multiply it by $\mat{A}$ over
and over. Suppose $\mat{A}$ has an eigenbasis $\vec{x}_1,\ldots,\vec{x}_n$ with
eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$, numbered so that
$\abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2}\ge\cdots\ge\abs{\lambda_n}$. Write
$\vec{v}^{(0)} = c_1\vec{x}_1 + c_2\vec{x}_2 + \cdots + c_n\vec{x}_n$ (with $c_1\ne 0$;
that is what ``almost any'' means). Then
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \mat{A}^k\vec{v}^{(0)} &= c_1\lambda_1^k\vec{x}_1 + c_2\lambda_2^k\vec{x}_2 + \cdots
     + c_n\lambda_n^k\vec{x}_n\\
   &= \lambda_1^k\Bigl(c_1\vec{x}_1 + c_2\Bigl(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\Bigr)^{k}\vec{x}_2
     + \cdots + c_n\Bigl(\frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_1}\Bigr)^{k}\vec{x}_n\Bigr).
  \end{aligned}
\]
Every ratio $\lambda_j/\lambda_1$ with $j\ge 2$ has absolute value below $1$, so its
$k$-th power decays to zero. The dominant direction $\vec{x}_1$ survives and everything
else fades. After enough multiplications the vector points almost exactly along
$\vec{x}_1$. The entries of $\mat{A}^k\vec{v}^{(0)}$ grow or shrink like $\lambda_1^k$
and would soon \emph{overflow} (exceed the largest number a float can hold) or
\emph{underflow} (become too small to represent, and be stored as $0$). So rescale the
vector to length $1$ after each step:
\[
  \vec{w} = \mat{A}\,\vec{v}^{(k)}, \qquad
  \vec{v}^{(k+1)} = \frac{\vec{w}}{\norm{\vec{w}}}.
\]
The eigenvalue itself comes from the \emph{Rayleigh quotient}
$\vec{v}\T\mat{A}\vec{v}/\vec{v}\T\vec{v}$ (Chapter~30); for the unit vector
$\vec{v}^{(k)}$ this is $\lambda_1 \approx (\vec{v}^{(k)})\T\mat{A}\,\vec{v}^{(k)}$. Or
read it off as the factor by which the vector grew before rescaling.

\begin{example}[Power method by hand]
\label{ch33:power}
Take
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix},
\]
whose true eigenvalues are $3$ (eigenvector $(1,1)$) and $1$ (eigenvector $(1,-1)$).
Start with the lopsided $\vec{v}^{(0)}=\colvec{1\\0}$ and track the vector
$\mat{A}^k\vec{v}^{(0)}$ without rescaling:
\[
  \mat{A}\colvec{1\\0}=\colvec{2\\1},\quad
  \mat{A}\colvec{2\\1}=\colvec{5\\4},\quad
  \mat{A}\colvec{5\\4}=\colvec{14\\13},\quad
  \mat{A}\colvec{14\\13}=\colvec{41\\40}.
\]
The ratio of the two components heads for $1$ (the direction of $(1,1)$):
$2/1$, then $5/4$, then $14/13$, then $41/40$. And the growth factor per step heads
for $3$: from $\colvec{14\\13}$ to $\colvec{41\\40}$ the first component went
$14\to 41$, a factor $2.93$, closing on the true dominant eigenvalue $\lambda_1=3$.
Four multiplications and we already have two good digits
(Figure~\ref{ch33:powerfig}).
\end{example}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{PageRank is the power method.} Google's original ranking treated the web as a
giant matrix $\mat{A}$ where $A_{ij}$ is the probability that a ``random surfer'' (an
imaginary reader who keeps clicking links at random) moves from page $j$ to page $i$.
(Chapter~35 uses the transposed convention, with row vectors.) The importance scores of
all pages form the dominant eigenvector of that billion-by-billion matrix, and you find
it by the iteration above: start with a vector whose entries are all equal, multiply by
$\mat{A}$, rescale, repeat. Because $\mat{A}$ is extremely sparse (each page links to
only a handful of others), each multiplication is cheap. The dominant eigenvalue is $1$,
and the next eigenvalue is well below it in size, so the power method converges in a few
dozen passes over the entire web.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{remark}
The power method only finds the \emph{top} eigenvalue, and it stalls if the top two are
equal in absolute value (then neither direction wins). To get other eigenvalues you can
\emph{shift and invert}: apply the power method to $(\mat{A}-\mu\mat{I})\inv$, whose
dominant eigenvalue comes from the eigenvalue of $\mat{A}$ nearest a chosen number
$\mu$. Or you can \emph{deflate}: remove the eigenvalue already found from the matrix
and run the power method again. For all eigenvalues at once, the standard method is the
\textbf{QR algorithm}: repeatedly factor $\mat{A}_k=\mat{Q}_k\mat{R}_k$ and multiply the
factors in the other order, $\mat{A}_{k+1}=\mat{R}_k\mat{Q}_k$. Since
$\mat{A}_{k+1}=\mat{Q}_k\T\mat{A}_k\mat{Q}_k$, every $\mat{A}_k$ is similar to $\mat{A}$
and has the same eigenvalues (Chapter~28). The iteration drives the matrix toward
upper-triangular form, and the eigenvalues can then be read off the diagonal. It is a
refined relative of the power method, and it is what the \texttt{eig} function in
MATLAB and NumPy runs.
\end{remark}

\section{Practical warnings to carry with you}

Here are the main practical rules of the chapter, collected as a checklist.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Rules for computing with matrices.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Never invert a matrix to solve a system.} To solve $\mat{A}\vec{x}=
    \vec{b}$, factor and back-substitute (\texttt{solve}, \texttt{A\textbackslash b}) --
    do not compute $\inv{\mat{A}}$ and multiply. Forming $\inv{\mat{A}}$ is slower, less
    accurate, and destroys sparsity.
  \item \textbf{Never solve least squares via the normal equations.} Use QR or SVD;
    $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ squares the condition number.
  \item \textbf{Check the condition number} (\texttt{cond(A)}) before trusting a
    result. If $\kappa\approx 10^{k}$, distrust the last $k$ digits.
  \item \textbf{Avoid subtracting nearly equal numbers.} Reformulate to dodge
    catastrophic cancellation when you can.
  \item \textbf{Never test floating-point numbers for exact equality.} Write
    $\abs{a-b} < \text{tol}$ for a small chosen tolerance $\text{tol}$, not
    $a == b$; after rounding, two quantities that should be equal rarely are.
  \item \textbf{Use structure.} Sparse, symmetric, positive definite, banded (nonzeros
    only in a narrow band around the diagonal), and triangular matrices each have
    faster, more stable specialized solvers. Tell your library what you know.
  \item \textbf{Watch the residual, and remember conditioning.} A small residual
    $\norm{\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}-\vec{b}}$ does not by itself guarantee a small error
    $\norm{\hat{\vec{x}}-\vec{x}}$: the relative error can be up to $\kappa(\mat{A})$
    times the relative residual.
\end{enumerate}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Pure linear algebra gives us exact objects: $\inv{\mat{A}}$, exact eigenvalues,
subspaces. Numerical linear algebra asks \emph{which of these can be computed reliably
on a machine that rounds every number}. Orthogonal matrices are safe because they
preserve length. Sparse problems stay manageable because we never form the dense
inverse. Ill-conditioned problems are flagged by a large $\kappa$, and backward-stable
algorithms add almost no error of their own. Together these ideas tell you how far a
computed answer can be trusted.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
Before the exercises, review the two key concepts. \emph{Conditioning} is about the problem:
$\kappa(\mat{A})=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ measures how much a relative error in the
data can be amplified into the answer; nothing can fix an ill-conditioned problem.
\emph{Stability} is about the algorithm: a backward-stable method (pivoted LU, QR)
solves a nearby problem exactly and adds little of its own. The master inequality
$\text{forward error}\lesssim\kappa\times\text{backward error}$ links them.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{The computational horizon: where numerical precision meets learning}

Direct factorizations like pivoted LU, QR, and the SVD are reliable at moderate
sizes, but at large scale numerical solvers face four limits:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The cubic scaling wall:} Direct factorizations like LU, QR, and the SVD
    scale cubically ($\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ operations) and require quadratic memory
    ($\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ entries). If a problem has $n = 10^7$ variables (routine when
    analysing large networks, training machine-learning models, or ranking web pages), a
    direct factorization of the full matrix
    would need about $800$ terabytes to store the matrix ($10^{14}$ entries of $8$
    bytes) and around $10^{21}$ operations to factor it.
  \item \textbf{Sparse fill-in:} Even when a matrix begins with $99.9\%$ zeros, Gaussian
    elimination turns zeros into nonzeros as rows are subtracted. This
    \emph{fill-in} can quickly use up the computer's memory unless the rows and columns
    are first reordered by specialized algorithms that keep the new nonzeros few.
  \item \textbf{The wall of severe ill-conditioning:} Backward stability guarantees that the
    computed solution is exact for a nearby system $(\mat{A}+\mat{E})\hat{\vec{x}}=\vec{b}$.
    It cannot rescue a problem where $\kappa(\mat{A}) > 1/\eps_{\text{mach}} \approx 10^{16}$.
    Then all sixteen decimal digits of double precision are lost to the problem's own
    sensitivity, and the computed answer is meaningless.
  \item \textbf{Small eigenvalue gaps:} Iterative methods depend on a \emph{spectral
    gap}, a clear difference in size between the largest eigenvalue and the next. When
    the second eigenvalue is close to the dominant one ($\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}
    \approx 1$), the power method and the relaxation methods stall, needing thousands of
    sweeps to gain a single digit of precision.
\end{itemize}

These limits change the practical goals. For more than half a
century, the main goal of numerical linear algebra was to compute the exact solution
of a fixed linear system to sixteen decimal digits of precision. But what happens when the
data is noisy, the parameters number in the billions, and computing sixteen digits is
neither possible nor useful?

In Chapter~34, we step into machine learning. We will use the geometric tools built
here---projections, singular vectors, condition numbers, and downhill steps on
quadratic bowls---as the language to learn patterns from data, rather than demanding exact answers.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Machine epsilon]
Double precision has a $52$-bit mantissa, so $\eps_{\text{mach}}=2^{-52}$. (a) Estimate
$\eps_{\text{mach}}$ in decimal and confirm it gives roughly $16$ significant digits.
(b) Explain in one sentence why the smallest number you can add to $1.0$ and get a
result different from $1.0$ is about $\eps_{\text{mach}}$, while near $1000$ that
smallest difference is about $1000\,\eps_{\text{mach}}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Catastrophic cancellation]
Working to $4$ significant decimal digits throughout, compute $f(x)=\dfrac{1-\cos x}
{x^2}$ at $x=0.001$ directly. The true value is close to $1/2$. (a) What do you
get, and why is it badly wrong? (b) Using $1-\cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$, recompute and
compare. (c) State which step in the direct route caused the loss.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Condition number of a $2\times 2$]
Let $\mat{A}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & \delta\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ for a
small $\delta>0$. (a) Find both singular values and write down $\kappa_2(\mat{A})$. (b)
For $\delta=10^{-6}$, how many decimal digits of accuracy do you expect to lose when
solving $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$? (c) Sketch the image of the unit circle under
$\mat{A}$ and relate its shape to $\kappa$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Determinant is not conditioning]
Let $\mat{A}=10^{-3}\mat{I}_3$ (the $3\times 3$ identity times $0.001$). (a) Compute
$\det\mat{A}$ -- it is tiny. (b) Compute $\kappa_2(\mat{A})$. (c) Explain in one or two
sentences why the small determinant is no cause for alarm here, tying it back to the
trap that conditioning is about a ratio of stretches, not a volume.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Pivoting saves the day]
Consider $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}10^{-4} & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\vec{x}=
\colvec{1\\2}$, solved on a machine with $3$ significant digits. (a) Solve by Gaussian
elimination \emph{without} pivoting and report the (wrong) answer. (b) Swap rows
(partial pivoting) and solve again. (c) State the multiplier in each case and explain
which one caused the trouble.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Diagonal dominance and convergence]
For $\mat{A}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}5 & 2\\ 1 & 4\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and
$\vec{b}=\colvec{7\\6}$: (a) Verify strict diagonal dominance. (b) Carry out three
Jacobi sweeps from $\vec{x}^{(0)}=(0,0)$. (c) Carry out three Gauss--Seidel sweeps from
the same start, and confirm it is closer to the true solution $(\tfrac{8}{9},
\tfrac{23}{18})\approx(0.889,1.278)$ after the same number of sweeps.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why not the normal equations]
Suppose $\mat{A}$ (tall, full column rank) has $\kappa_2(\mat{A})=10^{6}$. (a) What is
$\kappa_2(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})$? (b) Roughly how many decimal digits would you expect to
lose solving the least-squares problem through the normal equations versus through QR,
starting from double precision? (c) In one sentence, state the practical rule this
implies.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Power method by hand]
Let $\mat{A}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 1\\ 0 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, whose
eigenvalues are $3$ and $2$. Starting from $\vec{v}^{(0)}=\colvec{1\\1}$, perform three
power-method steps (you may skip normalization and track the vector and the ratio
of components). (a) Toward which eigenvector does the iterate swing? (b) Estimate the
dominant eigenvalue from the growth factor. (c) The subdominant component dies by what
factor each step, and what does that predict about the speed of convergence?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conjugate gradient geometry]
Take the symmetric positive definite $\mat{A}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 &
100\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and $\vec{b}=\colvec{1\\1}$. (a) Write the quadratic
$\phi(\vec{x})$ and describe the shape of its level sets. (b) Explain why steepest
descent from a generic start zig-zags badly, referencing the condition number
$\kappa_2(\mat{A})=100$. (c) Argue that conjugate gradient finishes in at most $2$
steps here, and say what general statement this illustrates.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A proof: orthogonal matrices are perfectly conditioned]
Let $\mat{Q}$ be a real $n\times n$ orthogonal matrix, $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$.
(a) Show $\norm{\mat{Q}\vec{x}}_2=\norm{\vec{x}}_2$ for every $\vec{x}$, hence
$\norm{\mat{Q}}_2=1$. (b) Show $\inv{\mat{Q}}=\mat{Q}\T$ is also orthogonal, so
$\norm{\inv{\mat{Q}}}_2=1$. (c) Conclude $\kappa_2(\mat{Q})=1$, and explain in words why
this makes orthogonal transformations the safest building blocks in numerical linear
algebra.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Residual versus error -- a challenge]
Using $\mat{A}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & 1.0001\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and
the true solution $\vec{x}=\colvec{1\\1}$ (so $\vec{b}=\colvec{2\\2.0001}$), consider
the approximate solution $\hat{\vec{x}}=\colvec{2\\0}$. (a) Compute the residual
$\vec{r}=\vec{b}-\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$ and its norm. (b) Compute the error
$\hat{\vec{x}}-\vec{x}$ and its norm. (c) The residual is small but the error is large.
Compute $\kappa_2(\mat{A})$ approximately and explain how the master inequality
$\text{forward error}\lesssim\kappa\times\text{backward error}$ accounts for the gap.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The conditioning of the Fourier matrix]
In Chapter~32 we encountered the Discrete Fourier Transform matrix $\mat{F}_n$, whose
entries are powers of the root of unity $\omega = e^{2\pi i / n}$. Consider the
normalized Fourier matrix $\mat{U} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mat{F}_n$.
(a) Show that $\mat{U}$ is unitary, satisfying $\mat{U}^*\mat{U} = \mat{I}_n$.
(b) Determine all singular values of $\mat{U}$.
(c) Compute the $2$-norm condition number $\kappa_2(\mat{U})$.
(d) Using your result, explain why the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) in practice does
not suffer from catastrophic roundoff error accumulation, tying this back to the geometric
stability of orthogonal and unitary transformations.
\end{exercise}
