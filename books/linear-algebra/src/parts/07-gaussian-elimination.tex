\chapter{Gaussian Elimination}

In the previous chapter, we saw linear systems $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ through
two geometric lenses: intersecting hyperplanes in the row picture, and combining
column arrows in the column picture. Both tell us what a solution means, but neither
gives a practical way to compute it when a system has ten or a thousand equations.

This chapter gives that method, \emph{Gaussian elimination}. Add multiples of
one equation to another to remove (``eliminate'') variables, until the last
equation contains only one variable, the one above it only two, and so on. Then
solve from the bottom equation upward, substituting each value you find into the
equation above; this upward pass is called \emph{back-substitution}.

\section{The picture: collapsing planes onto a corner}

Recall the two geometric faces of a linear system. In the row picture, each
equation is a constraint that cuts down the space of candidates. In two dimensions,
two non-parallel lines cross at a single point; in three dimensions, three tilted
planes meet at a point. Solving the system means \emph{pinning down that shared point}.

Gaussian elimination finds that point by
\textbf{replacing the tilted equations with simpler equations
that have exactly the same solution, until the coordinates can be read off one by one.}

In the row picture, each step swings a tilted plane until it no longer depends on
one of the variables, without moving the intersection point. In the column
picture, the same step puts zeros into the column vectors. At the end the last
column is the only one with a nonzero bottom entry, so the bottom coordinate of
$\vec{b}$ fixes the last weight on its own, and the other weights follow in
reverse order.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Imagine three tilted planes in space crossing at a single hidden point. You
cannot see where they meet because the planes are at awkward angles. Gaussian
elimination tilts the equations, without moving the
crossing point, until one plane becomes horizontal (it fixes $z$ outright),
the next pins down $y$ once $z$ is known, and the last pins down $x$ once $y$ and
$z$ are known. Only our \emph{description} of the point changes, from three
vague clues into three sharp ones.
\end{intuitionbox}

Everything we do will be a \emph{solution-preserving} operation: it changes the
equations but not their common solution. The rest of the chapter depends on that
claim, so we check it below.

Figure~\ref{ch07:two-lines} shows the simplest case, two lines in the plane,
before and after one elimination step. The step swings the second
line into a horizontal one (it now constrains only $y$), and the crossing point
stays where it was.

\subsection{The three legal moves}

We are allowed exactly three operations on the equations of a system. Each one
can be undone, and each one leaves the set of solutions unchanged.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The three elementary row operations.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Scaling:} multiply an equation by a nonzero number.
  \item \textbf{Replacement:} add a multiple of one equation to another equation.
  \item \textbf{Exchange:} swap the positions of two equations.
\end{enumerate}
Every one of these is reversible, so none of them can lose or invent solutions.
\end{focusbox}

(They are called \emph{row} operations because, once the system is written as a
grid of numbers below, each equation becomes one row of the grid.)

Why do these preserve the solution set? Consider row replacement. Call the two
equations $E_1$ and $E_2$, and let $c$ be any number; ``$E_2 + cE_1$'' means the
equation whose left side is (left side of $E_2$) $+\,c\,\cdot$ (left side of
$E_1$) and whose right side is built the same way. Suppose
a point $(x,y,z)$ satisfies $E_1$ and $E_2$. Then it
satisfies $E_2 + cE_1$, because both sides of $E_1$ are equal
at that point, so adding $c$ times a true equation to another true equation
keeps it true. Conversely, if a point satisfies $E_1$ and $E_2 + cE_1$, we can
\emph{recover} $E_2$ as $(E_2 + cE_1) - cE_1$, so the point satisfies $E_2$ too.
The two systems have identical solution sets. Scaling by a nonzero number $c$ is
undone by scaling by $1/c$, and a swap is undone by swapping back. (Scaling by
zero is forbidden because it \emph{cannot} be undone: it would erase an equation.)

\begin{trapbox}
The forbidden move is multiplying an equation by \textbf{zero}. ``$0 = 0$'' is
true everywhere, so it throws away the information that equation carried, and you
can never get it back. Every legal move must be undoable. Keep that test in your
head: if you could not reverse it, you may not do it.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{From equations to a grid of numbers}

Writing $x$, $y$, $z$ and plus signs over and over wastes ink. The
variables only hold places; what matters are the coefficients. So we strip the
system down to its numbers and stack them in a rectangular grid. The system
\[
\begin{aligned}
2x + 1y + 1z &= 5\\
4x + 1y + 0z &= 6\\
-2x + 2y + 1z &= 1
\end{aligned}
\qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
2 & 1 & 1 & 5\\
4 & 1 & 0 & 6\\
-2 & 2 & 1 & 1
\end{array}\right].
\]
The vertical bar separates the coefficient matrix $\mat{A}$ from the right-hand
side $\vec{b}$. The whole grid is the \emph{augmented matrix}, written
$[\,\mat{A}\mid\vec{b}\,]$. Each row is one equation; each of the first three
columns belongs to one variable. The three row operations now become operations
on rows of numbers, which is the form a computer can work with.

We name entries by position: $a_{ij}$ is the entry of $\mat{A}$ in row $i$ and
column $j$ (row first, then column). In the grid above, $a_{21}=4$ and
$a_{32}=2$.

\section{Elimination to upper-triangular form}

The goal of the elimination phase is to make the coefficient matrix
\emph{upper triangular}: every entry below the \emph{main diagonal} becomes zero.
(The main diagonal is the line of entries $a_{11}, a_{22}, a_{33}, \dots$ running
from the top-left corner to the bottom-right.) Why
that shape? Because an upper-triangular system is a staircase you can climb from
the bottom. The last equation involves only the last variable, so you solve it
immediately. The second-to-last involves only the last two, and you already know
one of them. And so on up to the top. This climb is the back-substitution
described at the start of the chapter; once the triangle is built, it needs no guesswork.
Figure~\ref{ch07:triangle} shows the shape we are aiming for: zeros below the
diagonal, and nonzero \emph{pivots} (defined in the next subsection) on it.

\subsection{Pivots and multipliers}

We work column by column, left to right. In each
column we pick a \emph{pivot}: a nonzero entry on the diagonal that we use to
turn every entry below it into zero. To clear an entry below the pivot, we subtract the right
multiple of the pivot's row.

\begin{focusbox}
To eliminate the entry in row $i$, column $j$, using the pivot in row $j$,
column $j$, the \textbf{multiplier} is
\[
\ell_{ij} \;=\; \frac{\text{entry to kill}}{\text{pivot}}
\;=\; \frac{a_{ij}}{a_{jj}},
\]
and the operation is: \emph{new row $i$} $=$ \emph{old row $i$}
$-\;\ell_{ij}\times(\text{pivot row } j)$. Choosing $\ell_{ij}$ this way makes
the target entry become exactly $a_{ij} - \ell_{ij}\,a_{jj} = a_{ij} - a_{ij} =
0$. Keep a record of these multipliers: they encode the entire elimination and
reappear in Chapter~9 (Section~\ref{ch09:triangular-picture}) as the entries of
a matrix called $\mat{L}$.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose you reach the second row and find a zero in its pivot position. Could you fix it by adding a multiple of row 1? What would happen to column 1 if you did?
\end{recoverybox}

The answer is no: row 1 has its nonzero pivot in column 1, so adding a multiple of it would put a nonzero entry back into column 1 of row 2, undoing the zero just created. Zeros already made must stay zero. That is why the algorithm always looks \emph{below} for a replacement pivot, never above.

\subsection{A $3\times 3$ example traced fully}

We now run the example above all the way through, recording every multiplier.
Figure~\ref{ch07:trace} summarizes the three steps.
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
2 & 1 & 1 & 5\\
4 & 1 & 0 & 6\\
-2 & 2 & 1 & 1
\end{array}\right].
\]
The first pivot is the $2$ in the top-left corner. We use it to clear the rest of
column 1.

\textbf{Step 1.} Kill the $4$ in row 2. Multiplier $\ell_{21} = 4/2 = 2$.
Replace row 2 with (row 2) $- 2\cdot$(row 1):
\[
[\,4,\;1,\;0\mid 6\,] - 2[\,2,\;1,\;1\mid 5\,]
= [\,0,\;-1,\;-2\mid -4\,].
\]
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
2 & 1 & 1 & 5\\
0 & -1 & -2 & -4\\
-2 & 2 & 1 & 1
\end{array}\right].
\]

\textbf{Step 2.} Kill the $-2$ in row 3. Multiplier $\ell_{31} = -2/2 = -1$.
Replace row 3 with (row 3) $-(-1)\cdot$(row 1) $=$ (row 3) $+$ (row 1):
\[
[\,-2,\;2,\;1\mid 1\,] + [\,2,\;1,\;1\mid 5\,] = [\,0,\;3,\;2\mid 6\,].
\]
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
2 & 1 & 1 & 5\\
0 & -1 & -2 & -4\\
0 & 3 & 2 & 6
\end{array}\right].
\]
Column 1 is clean. Now we move to the second pivot, the $-1$ now sitting in row
2, column 2, and use it to clear column 2 below.

\textbf{Step 3.} Kill the $3$ in row 3. Multiplier $\ell_{32} = 3/(-1) = -3$.
Replace row 3 with (row 3) $-(-3)\cdot$(row 2) $=$ (row 3) $+ 3\cdot$(row 2):
\[
[\,0,\;3,\;2\mid 6\,] + 3[\,0,\;-1,\;-2\mid -4\,] = [\,0,\;0,\;-4\mid -6\,].
\]
\[
\mat{U}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
2 & 1 & 1 & 5\\
0 & -1 & -2 & -4\\
0 & 0 & -4 & -6
\end{array}\right].
\]
We have reached upper-triangular form. The three pivots are $2,\,-1,\,-4$, all
sitting on the diagonal. The forward sweep is done.

\subsection{Back-substitution: climbing the staircase}

Now read the triangle from the bottom up. The last row says
\[
-4z = -6 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = \tfrac{3}{2}.
\]
The middle row says $-y - 2z = -4$. Plug in $z = 3/2$:
\[
-y - 2\cdot\tfrac{3}{2} = -4 \;\Longrightarrow\; -y - 3 = -4 \;\Longrightarrow\;
-y = -1 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 1.
\]
The top row says $2x + y + z = 5$. Plug in $y = 1$ and $z = 3/2$:
\[
2x + 1 + \tfrac{3}{2} = 5 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x = \tfrac{5}{2}
\;\Longrightarrow\; x = \tfrac{5}{4}.
\]
So the three tilted planes meet at the single point
$\bigl(\tfrac54,\,1,\,\tfrac32\bigr)$. Always check the answer in the
original equations:
\begin{align*}
2\cdot\tfrac54 + 1 + \tfrac32 &= \tfrac52 + 1 + \tfrac32 = 5, \\
4\cdot\tfrac54 + 1 + 0 &= 5 + 1 = 6, \\
-2\cdot\tfrac54 + 2\cdot 1 + \tfrac32 &= -\tfrac52 + 2 + \tfrac32 = 1.
\end{align*}
All three hold.

Figure~\ref{ch07:back-sub} shows this climb. Once the zeros
are in place below the diagonal, the bottom equation gives the last
variable immediately. Substituting that value upward determines the next variable, and so
on up the triangle until every unknown is known.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The two phases.} \emph{Forward elimination} reduces the matrix to an
upper triangle $\mat{U}$, recording multipliers as it goes. \emph{Back-substitution}
then climbs the triangle from the bottom, solving one variable at
each step. Together they solve the system. Forward elimination is the expensive
part; back-substitution needs far fewer operations (counted in the next section).
\end{focusbox}

\begin{strangbox}
Strang's course opens with exactly this: ``the geometry of linear equations''
followed immediately by ``elimination with matrices.'' If you are reading this
\emph{before} Strang, you now have the geometric view (planes pinned down one
coordinate at a time) behind each algebraic step.
If you are reading \emph{after} Strang, we are being deliberate about
the multipliers $\ell_{ij}$: they are recorded rather than discarded.
They are the basis of the $\mat{A}=\mat{LU}$ factorization that Chapter~9 (Section~\ref{ch09:triangular-picture})
makes precise, and they encode the entire elimination as a single matrix.
\end{strangbox}

\begin{labbox}
Solve this $2\times 2$ system by hand, tracing both phases:
\[
\begin{aligned}
3x + 2y &= 7\\
6x + 5y &= 16.
\end{aligned}
\]
Pivot on the $3$, find the multiplier $6/3 = 2$, eliminate, then back-substitute.
You should get $y = 2$, $x = 1$. Check it against both original equations before
moving on -- the checking habit is half the skill.
\end{labbox}

\section{Counting the work, and why triangles are cheap}

Why is this the algorithm everyone uses? Cost. For an $n\times n$ system, forward
elimination does about $\tfrac13 n^3$ multiply-and-subtract operations. The
reason is shown in Figure~\ref{ch07:shrinking}: clearing column~1 updates every
entry of the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ block below and to the right of the first pivot,
about $n^2$ operations; clearing column~2 updates an $(n-2)\times(n-2)$ block;
and so on. Adding up these shrinking squares gives
\[
(n-1)^2 + (n-2)^2 + \cdots + 1^2 \;\approx\; \tfrac13 n^3 .
\]
Back-substitution does only about $\tfrac12 n^2$: the bottom row costs one
operation, the next two, and so on up to $n$, and $1+2+\cdots+n\approx\tfrac12 n^2$.
The forward sweep dominates, as Figure~\ref{ch07:cost} shows.

In the big-O notation of Chapter~4, which keeps only how fast the work grows and
ignores the constant in front, forward elimination is $O(n^3)$ and
back-substitution is $O(n^2)$.

\begin{aestheticbox}
Doubling the size of your system makes elimination eight times slower, not twice
as slow. That is why large \emph{sparse} systems (where most coefficients are
zero, as in the equations for a fine mesh in an engineering simulation) need
special algorithms that avoid ``fill-in'', the turning of those zeros into
nonzeros during elimination. For \emph{dense} systems (most coefficients
nonzero), standard elimination remains the method of choice.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The $n^3$ growth is the same one you meet when multiplying two $n\times n$
matrices by the row-times-column rule, and this is no coincidence. Inverting
$\mat{A}$ and multiplying matrices are known to be equally hard: any speed-up for
one gives the same growth rate for the other, and solving
$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ can be done at that same rate. A calculus comparison: the cost of a numerical triple integral also grows
with each added dimension, because each new dimension multiplies the work.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Row exchanges: when the pivot is in the wrong place}

So far every pivot we needed was nonzero and sitting on the
diagonal. Sometimes, though, a zero shows up exactly where
we wanted a pivot. Consider
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
0 & 2 & 1 & 4\\
1 & 1 & 1 & 6\\
2 & 1 & 3 & 11
\end{array}\right].
\]
The top-left entry is $0$. We cannot use it as a pivot: the multiplier would
need a division by zero. The fix is easy. The equations happen to be
listed in an order that puts a zero on the diagonal, so we exchange
two rows to bring a nonzero number into the pivot position. Exchange
row 1 and row 2:
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & 1 & 1 & 6\\
0 & 2 & 1 & 4\\
2 & 1 & 3 & 11
\end{array}\right].
\]
Now the pivot is $1$, and elimination proceeds. Clear column 1: row 3
has a $2$ to remove, multiplier $\ell_{31}=2/1=2$, so row 3 becomes (row 3)
$-2\cdot$(row 1) $= [\,0,\,-1,\,1\mid -1\,]$. Row 2 already starts with $0$, so
there is nothing to do there.
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & 1 & 1 & 6\\
0 & 2 & 1 & 4\\
0 & -1 & 1 & -1
\end{array}\right].
\]
Second pivot is $2$ in row 2. Clear below: multiplier $\ell_{32}=-1/2$, so row 3
becomes (row 3) $-(-\tfrac12)\cdot$(row 2) $=$ (row 3) $+\tfrac12\cdot$(row 2)
$= [\,0,\,0,\,\tfrac32\mid 1\,]$.
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & 1 & 1 & 6\\
0 & 2 & 1 & 4\\
0 & 0 & \tfrac32 & 1
\end{array}\right].
\]
Back-substitution:
\begin{align*}
\tfrac32 z &= 1 &&\Longrightarrow\quad z = \tfrac23,\\
2y + \tfrac23 &= 4 &&\Longrightarrow\quad y = \tfrac53,\\
x + \tfrac53 + \tfrac23 &= 6 &&\Longrightarrow\quad x = \tfrac{11}{3}.
\end{align*}

\begin{focusbox}
A zero in the pivot position is \textbf{not} necessarily a sign of trouble. If
there is a nonzero entry \emph{below} it in the same column, swap rows to
bring that entry up, and carry on. The swap costs nothing and changes no
solution: you are only reordering the equations.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Computers exchange rows even when they do not strictly have to, and the reason is
\emph{numerical stability}: keeping the tiny rounding errors of computer
arithmetic from growing into wrong answers. A computer stores each number with a
fixed number of digits (this is called \emph{floating-point arithmetic}) and
rounds after every operation. Dividing by a pivot that
is tiny, say $0.0001$, multiplies any rounding error in that row by ten thousand,
and that magnified error can swamp the true answer.

So practical solvers use \emph{partial pivoting}: before clearing a column, look
at all the entries on or below the diagonal in that column, and swap the row with
the largest absolute value into the pivot position. Then every multiplier satisfies
$|\ell_{ij}| \le 1$, so a row subtraction never multiplies the errors already in
the pivot row by more than $1$. Every time
the Python routine \texttt{numpy.linalg.solve}, MATLAB's backslash operator
(\texttt{A\textbackslash b}), or the standard Fortran library LAPACK (Linear
Algebra PACKage) solves a system, partial pivoting runs automatically.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{What goes wrong: zero pivots that will not move}

Row exchange rescues us when a pivot position holds a zero but the column below
has something nonzero to swap in. What if the \emph{entire} column below (and
including) the pivot position is zero? Then no swap can produce a pivot. This
tells us something about the system itself: its equations no longer pin down a
single point.

\subsection{Case one: no solution (an inconsistent system)}

Consider
\[
\begin{aligned}
x + 2y &= 3\\
2x + 4y &= 7.
\end{aligned}
\qquad
\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}
1 & 2 & 3\\
2 & 4 & 7
\end{array}\right].
\]
Pivot on the $1$. Multiplier $\ell_{21}=2$. Row 2 becomes (row 2) $-2\cdot$(row
1) $= [\,0,\,0\mid 1\,]$.
\[
\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}
1 & 2 & 3\\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{array}\right].
\]
The second row now reads $0x + 0y = 1$, that is, $0 = 1$. There is no point in
the plane where $0$ equals $1$. The system has \textbf{no solution}.
Geometrically the two lines $x+2y=3$ and $2x+4y=7$ are \emph{parallel}: same
slope, different intercepts, never crossing. Elimination finds this
automatically: a row of zeros on the left with a nonzero on the right is the
algebraic sign of parallel, non-intersecting geometry. The left panel of
Figure~\ref{ch07:singular-lines} shows the two parallel lines.

\subsection{Case two: infinitely many solutions}

Now change only the right-hand side:
\[
\begin{aligned}
x + 2y &= 3\\
2x + 4y &= 6.
\end{aligned}
\qquad
\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}
1 & 2 & 3\\
2 & 4 & 6
\end{array}\right]
\;\xrightarrow{\,R_2 - 2R_1\,}\;
\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}
1 & 2 & 3\\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{array}\right].
\]
This time the last row is $0 = 0$, which is always true and adds no constraint.
The two equations defined the same line (the second is twice the first),
so every point on $x + 2y = 3$ is a solution (the right panel of
Figure~\ref{ch07:singular-lines}). There are \textbf{infinitely
many}. We say $y$ is a \emph{free variable}: it can take any value, and then $x =
3 - 2y$ follows. The solution set is a whole line of points.

\begin{focusbox}
When a column has no possible pivot, look at the row of zeros it leaves at the
bottom, and read its right-hand side:
\begin{itemize}
  \item left side all zero, right side \textbf{nonzero} $\Rightarrow$ ``$0 =
  c$'' with $c\neq 0$: \textbf{no solution} (the system is called \emph{inconsistent}).
  \item left side all zero, right side \textbf{zero} $\Rightarrow$ ``$0 = 0$'':
  a \textbf{free variable} and \textbf{infinitely many} solutions.
\end{itemize}
A square $n\times n$ matrix that produces fewer than $n$ pivots is
\textbf{singular} (the word from Chapter~6):
it has no inverse, and the system has either no solution or a whole family of them.
A full set of $n$ nonzero pivots means $\mat{A}$ is \textbf{invertible} (there is
a matrix that undoes it, as in Chapter~6) and the
solution is unique.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Do not confuse the two failure modes. \emph{Both} produce a row whose left side
is all zeros. The difference is the entry in the augmented (right-hand) column: a
contradictory row like ``$0=5$'' means \emph{no solution}; a row like
``$0=0$'' means a \emph{free variable}. Always look at the augmented
column before classifying the system.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{walkbox}
Take a walk and picture three planes in space. List, in your head, all the ways
they can fail to meet at one point: all three parallel; two parallel and one
crossing; three planes meeting pairwise in three parallel lines (forming a
triangular prism); all three sharing a whole line (like pages meeting along a
book's spine); all three being the
same plane. Which of these give \emph{no} solution and which give
\emph{infinitely many}? Elimination sorts every one of these cases into the
two categories above.
\end{walkbox}

\section{Reduced row echelon form: the cleanest possible answer}

Upper-triangular form plus back-substitution is enough to \emph{solve} a system.
There is a tidier endpoint, though: one from which the answer can be read off with
no further work, and which later chapters use to describe the structure of a matrix.
Instead of stopping when the matrix is triangular, we continue until it is as
simple as row operations allow. The result is the \emph{reduced row echelon
form}, almost always abbreviated \textbf{RREF}. (``Echelon'' means a
staircase-like formation.)

First, the looser notion. A matrix is in \emph{row echelon form} when:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item all-zero rows (if any) sit at the bottom, and
  \item each row's leading nonzero entry (its first nonzero entry from the left,
  again called its \emph{pivot}) is strictly to the
  right of the pivot in the row above, so the pivots step down and to the
  right in a staircase.
\end{enumerate}
This is upper-triangular form extended to matrices that are not square or that
have fewer pivots than rows. To reach the \emph{reduced} form we ask for two more things:
\begin{enumerate}
  \setcounter{enumi}{2}
  \item every pivot equals $1$ (scale each pivot row), and
  \item each pivot is the \emph{only} nonzero entry in its column -- we clear
  \emph{upward} as well as downward.
\end{enumerate}
Figure~\ref{ch07:rref-staircase} shows the resulting staircase, with leading
$1$s stepping down and to the right. A column with no pivot in it belongs to a
free variable, as in the $0=0$ case above; we call it a \emph{free column}.

\begin{formalbox}
A matrix $\mat{R}$ is in \textbf{reduced row echelon form} when it satisfies four
conditions: (i) every zero row sits below every nonzero row, and (ii) the leading
entry of each nonzero row is $1$ (a \emph{pivot} or leading $1$). In addition,
(iii) each leading $1$ lies strictly to the right of the leading $1$ above it, and
(iv) each leading $1$ is the only nonzero entry in its column. For a given matrix
this form is \textbf{unique} -- you reach the same $\mat{R}$ no matter which legal
moves you choose along the way.
\end{formalbox}

Because $\mat{R}$ depends only on $\mat{A}$ and not on the order of the row
operations, the number of pivots and the pattern of free variables we read off
$\mat{R}$ are properties of $\mat{A}$ itself.

\subsection{From triangle to RREF, traced}

Take our first result and finish the job. We had
\[
\mat{U}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
2 & 1 & 1 & 5\\
0 & -1 & -2 & -4\\
0 & 0 & -4 & -6
\end{array}\right].
\]
\textbf{Scale each pivot to $1$.} Divide row 1 by $2$, row 2 by $-1$, row 3 by
$-4$:
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac52\\[2pt]
0 & 1 & 2 & 4\\[2pt]
0 & 0 & 1 & \tfrac32
\end{array}\right].
\]
\textbf{Clear upward in column 3.} Row 2 becomes (row 2) $-2\cdot$(row 3); row 1
becomes (row 1) $-\tfrac12\cdot$(row 3):
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & \tfrac12 & 0 & \tfrac74\\[2pt]
0 & 1 & 0 & 1\\[2pt]
0 & 0 & 1 & \tfrac32
\end{array}\right].
\]
\textbf{Clear upward in column 2.} Row 1 becomes (row 1) $-\tfrac12\cdot$(row 2):
\[
\mat{R}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & 0 & 0 & \tfrac54\\[2pt]
0 & 1 & 0 & 1\\[2pt]
0 & 0 & 1 & \tfrac32
\end{array}\right].
\]
In this final form, the left block is the identity matrix (ones on the diagonal,
zeros elsewhere), and the
solution appears directly in the augmented column: $x=\tfrac54$,
$y=1$, $z=\tfrac32$, matching the values found by back-substitution.
Clearing upward does the work of back-substitution inside the row operations.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Upper-triangular form produces a system ready for back-substitution. RREF
completes those substitutions during elimination, leaving the solution directly
visible in the augmented column. The cost is extra arithmetic up front
(clearing upward) in exchange for an answer you can read off. For solving a single
system by hand, stopping at triangular form is usually faster. For studying the
matrix itself (how many pivots it has, which variables are free, and, in later
chapters, which vectors it sends to zero), RREF is the standard form used
throughout the book.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{labbox}
Reduce this matrix all the way to RREF by hand. It has a free variable, so the
identity will \emph{not} appear; watch where the staircase breaks.
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & 2 & 1 & 2\\
2 & 4 & 0 & 6\\
1 & 2 & 3 & 0
\end{array}\right].
\]
(Hint: column 2 will never get a pivot, so $y$ is free. You should find that
$z$ is pinned to a single value while $x$ depends on $y$. You will get
$z=-1$ and $x=3-2y$.)
\end{labbox}

\section{Elimination is matrix multiplication}

This section turns elimination from a procedure into a statement about matrices,
and the next two chapters build on it. \textbf{Every
row operation is the same as multiplying on the left by a special
matrix.} These special matrices are called \emph{elementary matrices}. With them,
the whole algorithm becomes a product of matrices (matrix products, and the
row-times-column rule for computing them, are from Chapter~5).

To build an elementary matrix, \emph{do the row
operation to the identity matrix}. The result is the matrix that performs
that operation on \emph{any} matrix when you multiply it on the left
(``left-multiply'', meaning $\mat{E}$ is written to the left: $\mat{E}\mat{A}$).

\subsection{The replacement matrix}

Recall our first step: row 2 $\leftarrow$ row 2 $- 2\cdot$row 1, on a
$3$-row matrix. (The arrow $\leftarrow$ means ``is replaced by''.) Apply that
operation to $\mat{I}_3$, the $3\times 3$ identity matrix. Start from
\[
\mat{I}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix},
\quad\text{do row 2}\leftarrow\text{row 2}-2\cdot\text{row 1}, \quad
\mat{E}_{21}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\-2&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The only change from the identity is the $-2$ in position $(2,1)$: the
multiplier, with a minus sign, placed where the entry it removes used to
be. We name this matrix $\mat{E}_{21}$ after that position. Now multiply
$\mat{E}_{21}$ against the original augmented matrix:
\[
\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\-2&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
2 & 1 & 1 & 5\\
4 & 1 & 0 & 6\\
-2 & 2 & 1 & 1
\end{array}\right]
=
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
2 & 1 & 1 & 5\\
0 & -1 & -2 & -4\\
-2 & 2 & 1 & 1
\end{array}\right].
\]
Row 1 and row 3 pass through untouched (the identity rows copy them); row 2 comes
out as old row 2 minus twice row 1, which is Step 1 from before. To check
the new row 2 by hand, multiply row 2 of $\mat{E}_{21}$, which is $(-2,\,1,\,0)$,
against each column: the first entry is $(-2)(2) + (1)(4) + (0)(-2) = -4 + 4 = 0$;
the second is $(-2)(1)+(1)(1) = -1$; the third $(-2)(1)+(1)(0)=-2$; the augmented
entry $(-2)(5)+(1)(6) = -4$. The $-2$ in $\mat{E}_{21}$ \emph{is} the multiplier,
with its sign flipped.

\begin{focusbox}
The elementary matrix for ``row $i$ $\leftarrow$ row $i$ $-\ell\cdot$ row $j$''
is the identity with the single extra entry $-\ell$ placed at position
$(i,j)$. Left-multiplying by it performs the operation. To \emph{undo} it, flip
the sign: the inverse $\mat{E}\inv$ (the matrix that undoes $\mat{E}$) is the
identity with $+\ell$ at $(i,j)$. (Subtracting
$\ell\cdot$row $j$ and then adding it back leaves the matrix unchanged.)
\end{focusbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If an elementary matrix $\mat{E}$ subtracts $3\times(\text{row } 1)$ from row $2$, what does its inverse $\mat{E}\inv$ do? You do not need a formula: how would you undo that operation, in plain words?
\end{recoverybox}

Add $3\times(\text{row } 1)$ back to row $2$. Inverting an elementary row replacement changes only the sign of the off-diagonal entry: $-3$ becomes $+3$.

\subsection{The other two as matrices}

A \textbf{scaling} of row $i$ by $c$ is the identity with $c$ in place of the
$1$ at position $(i,i)$. A \textbf{row exchange} of rows $i$ and $j$ is the
identity with rows $i$ and $j$ swapped; such a matrix is called a
\emph{permutation matrix} (a permutation is a reordering), written $\mat{P}$. For example, swapping rows 1 and 2
of a $3$-row matrix is done by
\[
\mat{P}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mat{P}\begin{bmatrix}a\\b\\c\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}b\\a\\c\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Each of these is invertible (scaling by $c$ undoes with $1/c$; a swap undoes with
the same swap), which is the matrix version of ``every legal move is
reversible.''

\subsection{The whole elimination as one product}

Now string the steps together. Our full forward sweep was three replacement
operations: $\mat{E}_{21}$, then $\mat{E}_{31}$, then $\mat{E}_{32}$. Applying
them in order to $\mat{A}$ means left-multiplying one after another:
\[
\mat{E}_{32}\,\mat{E}_{31}\,\mat{E}_{21}\,\mat{A} = \mat{U}.
\]
Read it right to left, the order the operations happened. A single
matrix $\mat{E}=\mat{E}_{32}\mat{E}_{31}\mat{E}_{21}$ captures the entire forward
elimination. Because each elementary matrix is invertible, we can move them to the
other side (multiply both sides on the left by $\mat{E}_{32}\inv$, then
$\mat{E}_{31}\inv$, then $\mat{E}_{21}\inv$):
\[
\mat{A} = \mat{E}_{21}\inv\,\mat{E}_{31}\inv\,\mat{E}_{32}\inv\,\mat{U}
= \mat{L}\,\mat{U}.
\]
That product of inverse elementary matrices is \emph{lower triangular} (all
entries above the main diagonal are zero), and it is the
matrix $\mat{L}$. Its entries below the diagonal are the multipliers
$\ell_{ij}$ themselves, with their original sign. For our example,
\[
\mat{L}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\ \ell_{21}&1&0\\ \ell_{31}&\ell_{32}&1\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\ 2&1&0\\ -1&-3&1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
This is the
$\mat{A}=\mat{LU}$ factorization. Chapter~9 (Section~\ref{ch09:triangular-picture}) develops it in full.

\begin{aestheticbox}
Here elimination stops being only arithmetic and becomes \emph{algebra}.
A sequence of row subtractions is written as a matrix equation
$\mat{EA}=\mat{U}$, and matrices can be inverted, multiplied and factored. The
same viewpoint recurs throughout the book: rotations are matrices,
projections are matrices, and differentiation of polynomials can be written as a
matrix.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{strangbox}
``Elimination $=$ left-multiplication by elementary matrices, and undoing it
gives $\mat{A}=\mat{LU}$'' is the main idea of the first weeks of Strang's course.
If you are heading into his course, you have already met
$\mat{E}$, $\mat{P}$, and $\mat{L}$ as concrete grids of numbers. If you are
revisiting after Strang: we got $\mat{L}$ with no extra work, because
the multipliers we were already computing \emph{are} its entries.
\end{strangbox}

\subsection{Where permutations land: $\mat{PA}=\mat{LU}$}

When the elimination needs a row swap, a permutation matrix $\mat{P}$ joins the
product. If all the needed swaps are collected into a single $\mat{P}$ and applied
first, one can show that every square matrix satisfies $\mat{PA}=\mat{LU}$ for
some permutation matrix $\mat{P}$, lower-triangular $\mat{L}$, and upper-triangular
$\mat{U}$. That is the general statement of what Gaussian elimination
produces, and it is what a real solver computes and stores. Chapter~9
(Section~\ref{ch09:pivoting}) proves it for invertible matrices, the case we
need, and uses it. Row exchanges
fit into the ``elimination is matrix multiplication'' description: swaps
are more elementary matrices.

\section{The full algorithm, in one place}

Here is the whole procedure as one recipe you can run on any system.
Figure~\ref{ch07:flowchart} draws the decisions it makes at each column.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Gaussian elimination, end to end.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Form the augmented matrix $[\,\mat{A}\mid\vec{b}\,]$.
  \item \emph{Forward sweep.} For each column $j$ from left to right:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If the pivot position is zero, swap in a nonzero entry from
    below. If the whole column below is zero, move on to the next column but stay
    in the same row: this column has no
    pivot (it belongs to a free variable).
    \item For each row $i$ below the pivot, compute $\ell_{ij}=a_{ij}/a_{jj}$ and
    replace row $i$ by row $i$ $-\ell_{ij}\cdot$ (pivot row).
  \end{itemize}
  \item Inspect the result. A row ``$0\;\cdots\;0\mid c$'' with $c\neq 0$ means
  \emph{no solution}. Otherwise, a column without a pivot means a \emph{free
  variable} and infinitely many solutions.
  \item \emph{Solve.} Back-substitute up the triangle; or continue to RREF and
  read the answer off directly.
\end{enumerate}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\subsection{One more $3\times 3$ example, with a swap in the middle}

Here is a system where a swap is needed partway through, not at the start.
This is the case students most often get wrong, so trace it slowly.
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & 1 & 1 & 6\\
2 & 2 & 5 & 12\\
1 & 3 & 0 & 8
\end{array}\right].
\]
Pivot on the top-left $1$. Clear column 1. Row 2 $\leftarrow$ row 2 $-2\cdot$row
1 $= [\,0,\,0,\,3\mid 0\,]$; row 3 $\leftarrow$ row 3 $-1\cdot$row 1 $=
[\,0,\,2,\,-1\mid 2\,]$.
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & 1 & 1 & 6\\
0 & 0 & 3 & 0\\
0 & 2 & -1 & 2
\end{array}\right].
\]
Now the second pivot position $(2,2)$ holds a $0$. But the column below it has a
$2$ in row 3, so we swap rows 2 and 3:
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & 1 & 1 & 6\\
0 & 2 & -1 & 2\\
0 & 0 & 3 & 0
\end{array}\right].
\]
The matrix is already upper triangular: the swap left nothing more to clear.
Back-substitute: $3z = 0$ so $z = 0$; then $2y - 0 = 2$ so $y = 1$; then
$x + 1 + 0 = 6$ so $x = 5$. Check the original middle equation:
$2\cdot 5 + 2\cdot 1 + 5\cdot 0 = 12$. The pivots are
$1,2,3$, three of them and all nonzero, so this matrix is invertible and the
solution is the unique point $(5,1,0)$.

\begin{trapbox}
When a pivot position is zero, do \emph{not} divide by it and do \emph{not} give
up. First look down the column. Nonzero entry below? Swap. Nothing below? That
column has no pivot; move to the next column while
staying in the same row, and keep going. Dividing by zero is the most
common fatal error: it produces nonsense and corrupts every later step.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Gaussian elimination is not confined to the real numbers. The algorithm only
ever adds, subtracts, multiplies, and divides by nonzero pivots,
so it runs unchanged in any number system where those four operations behave as
usual. Mathematicians call such a number system a \emph{field}. Examples are the
rational numbers (fractions), the complex numbers,
and the integers modulo a prime $p$, where you keep only the remainder after
dividing by $p$. The smallest case is
$\F_2$: only the numbers $0$ and $1$, with arithmetic mod $2$, so $1+1=0$.
Solving linear systems over $\F_2$ is how many \emph{error-correcting codes}
(schemes that add check bits to data so that corrupted bits can be found and
repaired) are decoded, and it is also used to break weak ciphers whose
scrambling is linear. The same algorithm, run over a two-element
number system, is at work in storage devices and in digital radio links.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{What elimination tells you}

Beyond solving for a single right-hand side, the number of pivots a matrix produces,
counted across the whole forward sweep including swaps, is one of the most important
numbers attached to it. It is called the \emph{rank}, and it answers questions
that the original grid of numbers does not show:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{How many independent equations are there?} Exactly the
  number of pivots. Rows that elimination turns into all zeros were redundant: they
  carried no new information.
  \item \textbf{Is the square system uniquely solvable?} Yes if and only if
  every diagonal position gets a pivot: $n$ pivots for an $n\times n$ matrix.
  That is the same as $\mat{A}$ being invertible.
  \item \textbf{How many free variables are there?} The number of columns minus
  the number of pivots. When the system has solutions, each free variable is a
  direction you can move along without leaving the solution set, so this number
  is the dimension of the family of solutions.
\end{itemize}

Each of these ideas (rank, invertibility, free variables, the dimension of
the solution set) gets its own chapter later.

The next chapter, \emph{Matrix Multiplication and Inverses}, looks at matrix
products and inverses more broadly. Why does the row-times-column rule work the
way it does? And when a square matrix has a
full set of $n$ pivots, how do we find a single inverse matrix $\mat{A}\inv$ that
undoes $\mat{A}$ directly, turning $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ into
$\vec{x}=\mat{A}\inv\vec{b}$?

\begin{historybox}
Although named after Carl Friedrich Gauss, systematic elimination began much earlier.
Around 150~BCE, Chinese scholars compiling \emph{The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art}
solved tax and grain-yield problems involving several simultaneous conditions.
Working with bamboo counting rods arranged in rectangular arrays on a counting
board (the method is called \emph{fangcheng}, ``rectangular arrays''),
they repeatedly subtracted multiples of one column from another to clear entries,
performing the forward sweep of elimination more than eighteen centuries before Europe codified it.

The modern story starts with an astronomy problem in 1801. On New Year's Day,
the Italian astronomer Giuseppe Piazzi spotted a dim, moving body in the night sky, the dwarf
planet Ceres. Piazzi tracked it for only 41 days across a small arc before it was lost
in the glare of the Sun. Astronomers had
only a handful of imprecise observations, and Newton's laws of gravitation gave
nonlinear orbit equations that could not be solved directly.

The 24-year-old Gauss took up the challenge. Gauss later said he had used his \emph{method of least
squares}, a technique for finding the curve that best fits a set of
imperfect observations, which reduces the problem to systems of linear equations for the
unknown orbital quantities. In December 1801, guided by Gauss's prediction,
astronomers found Ceres within half a degree of where he said it would be.
Gauss described least squares in print in 1809, in \emph{Theoria Motus Corporum
Coelestium}, and in an 1810 paper on the orbit of the asteroid Pallas he set out the
elimination procedure in a systematic notation.

In 1888 the German geodesist Wilhelm Jordan was working in \emph{geodesy}, the science
of measuring the shape of the Earth and mapping networks of surveying triangles. Facing
large systems of equations, Jordan used each pivot to eliminate entries
\emph{upward} as well as downward. This drove the matrix straight to reduced row
echelon form, so surveyors could read off coordinates without a separate back-substitution.
The combined algorithm has been known as \emph{Gauss--Jordan elimination} ever since.

With exact fractions, elimination succeeds whenever the pivots are nonzero. In
floating-point arithmetic (numbers stored with a fixed number of digits and rounded
after every operation), naive elimination is dangerous. If a pivot is
tiny (say $10^{-5}$), dividing by it creates enormous multipliers (about $10^5$),
and later row subtractions can magnify small rounding errors until they swamp the true solution.

In 1943 the statistician Harold Hotelling published an analysis suggesting that
rounding errors in elimination could grow exponentially with the number of
equations, which cast doubt on solving large systems by machine. In 1947 John von
Neumann and Herman Goldstine gave the first careful error analysis of
elimination on a digital computer, and Alan Turing's 1948 paper
\emph{Rounding-Off Errors in Matrix Processes} also took up the question. James H.
Wilkinson settled it around 1961 using \emph{backward error analysis}. Rather than
asking how far the computed answer is from the true solution, backward error
analysis asks how slightly the problem would have to change for the computed answer
to be \emph{exactly} right. Wilkinson showed that with \textbf{partial pivoting}
(swapping rows at each step to put the largest available entry in the pivot position,
so that every $|\ell_{ij}| \le 1$), the computed answer is the exact answer to a
nearby problem for almost all matrices met in practice.

Two major questions about elimination remain open today. Here $\omega$ (omega)
denotes the smallest exponent such that $n\times n$ systems can be solved in about
$n^{\omega}$ operations.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The best exponent:} Is solving an $n\times n$ system an
  $O(n^3)$ problem, or can it be done in about $O(n^2)$ time, as fast as reading the $n^2$
  entries of the matrix? In 1969, Volker Strassen showed that matrix multiplication,
  and with it elimination, can be carried out in $O(n^{2.81})$ operations. Later work
  has pushed the exponent down to about $2.371$, but the true value of $\omega$ is
  one of the best-known open problems in computer science.
  \item \textbf{Worst-case stability versus speed:} Partial pivoting adds only
  $O(n^2)$ extra work (the comparisons) and almost never fails in practice, but
  Wilkinson found matrices for which its rounding errors can grow like $2^{n}$.
  \emph{Complete pivoting} (searching the whole remaining block and swapping both
  rows and columns) has a much better worst-case guarantee, but the searching costs
  $O(n^3)$ extra comparisons. Finding a pivoting strategy that is as cheap as partial
  pivoting with stronger guarantees is an active research topic in numerical linear algebra.
\end{itemize}

There is also a practical cost: if you solve $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ and then change only the right-hand side $\vec{b}$ (such
as testing several wind loads on the same bridge), running elimination from scratch repeats
$O(n^3)$ work on the same matrix $\mat{A}$. Chapter~8 (Section~\ref{ch08:origin}) examines matrix
multiplication and inverses, and Chapter~9 (Section~\ref{ch09:triangular-picture}) introduces
LU factorization ($\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{U}$), which stores the multipliers so that
each new right-hand side costs only $O(n^2)$.
\end{historybox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental checks: true or false?]
Decide whether each statement is true or false, and give a one-sentence geometric or algebraic reason:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Multiplying an entire equation by a nonzero constant changes the line or plane it represents in space.
  \item In an upper-triangular system $\mat{U}\vec{x} = \vec{c}$, back-substitution solves the system without computing any new multipliers $\ell_{ij}$.
  \item If an $n \times n$ system produces fewer than $n$ nonzero pivots during elimination, it is guaranteed to have no solution.
  \item Swapping two rows of an augmented matrix changes the coordinates $(x, y, z)$ of the intersection point.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Warm-up, fully traced]
Solve by Gaussian elimination, showing the multiplier at every step and then
back-substituting:
\[
\begin{aligned}
x + 2y + z &= 4\\
2x + 5y + 3z &= 11\\
2x + 3y + 2z &= 8.
\end{aligned}
\]
Verify your answer in all three original equations.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A forced swap]
Run elimination on
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
0 & 1 & 2 & 3\\
1 & 2 & 1 & 4\\
3 & 1 & 0 & 5
\end{array}\right].
\]
You must exchange rows before you can begin. Identify which swap, reach
upper-triangular form, and back-substitute. State the three pivots.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Diagnose the failure]
For each system, run elimination far enough to decide whether it has no solution,
exactly one, or infinitely many. If infinitely many, describe the solution set
(name the free variable and write the others in terms of it).
\[
\text{(a)}\;\;
\begin{aligned}
x + y &= 2\\
3x + 3y &= 5,
\end{aligned}
\qquad
\text{(b)}\;\;
\begin{aligned}
x - y &= 1\\
-2x + 2y &= -2.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[All the way to RREF]
Take the upper-triangular matrix
\[
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
3 & 6 & 1 & 0\\
0 & 2 & 4 & 8\\
0 & 0 & 5 & 10
\end{array}\right]
\]
and reduce it to RREF: scale each pivot to $1$ and clear upward. Read off
$x,y,z$ from the final augmented column.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Elementary matrices by hand]
Write down the $3\times 3$ elementary matrix $\mat{E}$ that performs ``row 3
$\leftarrow$ row 3 $-4\cdot$ row 1.'' Multiply it against
$\mat{A}=\colvec{1&0&2\\0&1&1\\4&2&0}$ and confirm by direct multiplication that
the product has a zero in position $(3,1)$. Then write $\mat{E}\inv$ and verify
$\mat{E}\inv\mat{E}=\mat{I}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A free variable in 3D]
Solve
\[
\begin{aligned}
x + y + z &= 3\\
2x + 2y + 3z &= 8\\
x + y + 2z &= 5,
\end{aligned}
\]
and notice that column 2 never produces a pivot. Identify the free variable and
write the full family of solutions as a point plus a multiple of a direction
vector. (Geometrically, the three planes share a whole line.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Tiny-pivot instability]
Consider, with exact arithmetic,
\[
\begin{aligned}
0.001\,x + y &= 1\\
x + y &= 2.
\end{aligned}
\]
(a) Solve exactly. (b) Now pretend your calculator keeps only $3$ significant
digits (it rounds every result to its first three nonzero digits, so $998.7$
becomes $999$ and $1001$ becomes $1000$) and eliminate \emph{without} swapping,
using $0.001$ as the pivot.
Watch the answer for $x$ degrade. (c) Swap the two rows first (partial pivoting)
and redo with $3$ digits. Explain why the swapped version is far more accurate.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Counting operations]
Argue that clearing column $1$ of an $n\times n$ matrix costs about $n^2$
multiply-add operations (you touch each of the $n-1$ rows below the pivot, and
each touched row has about $n$ entries). Summing the shrinking columns, explain
informally why the whole forward sweep costs on the order of $\tfrac13 n^3$.
(Hint: $\sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2 \approx \tfrac13 n^3$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Uniqueness of RREF -- a proof]
Suppose two different sequences of legal row operations turn the same matrix
$\mat{A}$ into row echelon forms $\mat{R}_1$ and $\mat{R}_2$. Argue that
$\mat{R}_1$ and $\mat{R}_2$ have their pivots in exactly the same columns.
(Hint: a column is a pivot column precisely when it is \emph{not} a linear
combination of the columns to its left, and row operations do not change which
columns are combinations of which earlier columns. You may take this last fact
as given for now -- we prove it carefully in the chapter on the column space.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Elimination over a tiny field]
Work over $\F_2$, where the only numbers are $0$ and $1$ and $1+1=0$.
Solve
\[
\begin{aligned}
x + y \phantom{{}+ z} &= 1\\
x \phantom{{}+ y} + z &= 0\\
y + z &= 1,
\end{aligned}
\]
using exactly the same elimination, but with mod-$2$ arithmetic (so subtracting
equals adding). Find all solutions. This is a small example of the kind of
computation used to decode an error-correcting code.
\end{exercise}
