\chapter{Transposes and Permutations}

In Chapter~9, we watched Gaussian elimination run into an awkward obstacle: whenever
a zero landed in the pivot position on the diagonal, elimination could not divide by zero
and ground to a halt. The rescue was simple: swap two rows and keep going. We collected
those row exchanges into a single shuffle matrix $\mat{P}$, producing the complete factorization
$\mat{P}\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{U}$.

At the time, $\mat{P}$ looked like emergency housekeeping. This chapter studies it properly,
together with a second operation that turns out to be closely tied to it. The first operation
is the \emph{transpose}: flip a matrix across its main diagonal so that rows become columns.
The second is a \emph{permutation}: shuffle the rows (or columns) of a matrix without changing
any of the numbers, only their positions.

The transpose is what connects a matrix to the dot product. We will see that you can move a
matrix from one side of a dot product to the other if you transpose it on the way, and that
this one rule explains a great deal: why some matrices are mirror images of themselves, why
the product $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ appears whenever data is fitted, and how a matrix can be ``run
backward''. Permutation matrices will give us our first examples of matrices that move space
without stretching it: every length and every angle stays the same.

\section{The transpose: flipping across the diagonal}

\subsection{The definition, with a picture}

Take a matrix and reflect it across the \emph{main diagonal}, the diagonal that
runs from the top-left corner down to the bottom-right. Whatever was in row $i$, column $j$ lands in
row $j$, column $i$.

\begin{definition}[Transpose]\label{ch10:def-transpose}
Let $\mat{A}$ be an $m\times n$ matrix with entries $a_{ij}$ (row $i$, column $j$). Its
\emph{transpose} $\mat{A}\T$ is the $n\times m$ matrix whose $(i,j)$ entry is $a_{ji}$:
\[
  (\mat{A}\T)_{ij} = a_{ji}.
\]
Rows of $\mat{A}$ become columns of $\mat{A}\T$, and columns of $\mat{A}$ become rows of $\mat{A}\T$.
\end{definition}

Here is a $2\times 3$ matrix and its $3\times 2$ transpose:
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{A}\T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Look at where each number went. The first \emph{row} of $\mat{A}$, namely $(1,2,3)$, became the first
\emph{column} of $\mat{A}\T$. The first column of $\mat{A}$, namely $\colvec{1\\4}$, became the first
row of $\mat{A}\T$. The diagonal entries that sit on the fold (here only $a_{11}=1$ and $a_{22}=5$)
do not move. Everything else swaps across the fold.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Picture the matrix as a grid of tiles. Lay a mirror along the main diagonal (top-left to
bottom-right) and the transpose is what you see in the mirror. A tall thin matrix becomes a short
wide one. A row vector becomes a column vector. Nothing is added, scaled, or destroyed; the same
numbers trade places across the fold.
\end{intuitionbox}

Three quick checks. If $\mat{A}$ is $m\times n$ then $\mat{A}\T$ is $n\times m$: the
shape flips. Transposing twice brings you home: $(\mat{A}\T)\T = \mat{A}$, because reflecting across
the diagonal and then reflecting again undoes itself. And a single column vector $\vec{x}$ (which we
think of as $n\times 1$) transposes into a single \emph{row}, $\vec{x}\T$, of shape $1\times n$. That
last fact matters most, because a row times a column is a dot product.

\begin{focusbox}
Hold onto three reflexes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $(\mat{A}\T)_{ij} = a_{ji}$: swap the indices.
  \item Shape flips: $m\times n \to n\times m$.
  \item $(\mat{A}\T)\T = \mat{A}$: transpose is its own undo.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If a matrix $\mat{M}$ has shape $4\times 7$, what is the shape of $(\mat{M}\T)\T$?
Which entries of $\mat{M}$ never change position when you transpose?
\emph{Answer:} Transposing once produces $7\times 4$; transposing again returns the original $4\times 7$.
Only the diagonal entries $m_{11}, m_{22}, m_{33}, m_{44}$ stay glued in place, because swapping row index $i$
with column index $i$ leaves the address unchanged.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{The transpose turns a column into a dot-product machine}

Why flip a grid at all? Here is the first reason. Recall the dot product
of two column vectors $\vec{u},\vec{v}\in\R^n$:
\[
  \inner{\vec{u}}{\vec{v}} \;=\; u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + \cdots + u_n v_n.
\]
Now write $\vec{u}$ as a column and transpose it into a row. Matrix-multiply that row by the column
$\vec{v}$:
\[
  \vec{u}\T \vec{v}
  = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & u_2 & \cdots & u_n \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix}
  = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + \cdots + u_nv_n
  = \inner{\vec{u}}{\vec{v}}.
\]
So the dot product \emph{is} a transposed vector times a vector. The product ``$\vec{u}\T\vec{v}$''
is matrix multiplication of a $1\times n$ row by an $n\times 1$ column, producing a $1\times 1$
matrix, which we treat as a number. This lets every dot-product fact be
written as a matrix product and back again, and we will use it throughout the chapter.

\begin{example}[A dot product as $\vec{u}\T\vec{v}$]\label{ch10:ex-dot}
Let $\vec{u}=\colvec{2\\-1\\3}$ and $\vec{v}=\colvec{1\\4\\2}$. Then
\[
  \vec{u}\T\vec{v}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\colvec{1\\4\\2}
  = (2)(1)+(-1)(4)+(3)(2) = 2-4+6 = 4.
\]
And $\norm{\vec{u}}^2 = \vec{u}\T\vec{u} = 4+1+9 = 14$. A vector dotted with itself, written
$\vec{u}\T\vec{u}$, is the squared length. Remember this: $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ later in the chapter is
the same idea applied to every pair of columns at once.

The order of multiplication matters. Row times column, $(1\times n)(n\times 1)$, produces
a $1\times 1$ result: the dot product, also called the \emph{inner product}. Reverse the order to
column times row, $(n\times 1)(1\times n)$, and you get an $n\times n$ grid of numbers: the
\emph{outer product} $\vec{u}\vec{v}\T$ from Chapter~8. Every row of an outer product is a scaled
copy of $\vec{v}\T$, and every column is a scaled copy of $\vec{u}$, which is what makes it a
rank-one matrix. Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-inner-outer} puts the two side by side. Keep
row-times-column (a number) distinct from column-times-row (a rank-one matrix).
\end{example}

\section{The algebra of transposes}

Before going further we need the rules for computing with transposes. There are four; three are
easy and one reverses the order of a product. We derive each, because a formula you can
derive in three lines is a formula you will not misremember.

\begin{theorem}[Rules of the transpose]\label{ch10:thm-rules}
For matrices of compatible sizes and a scalar $c$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $(\mat{A}+\mat{B})\T = \mat{A}\T + \mat{B}\T$.
  \item $(c\,\mat{A})\T = c\,\mat{A}\T$.
  \item $(\mat{A}\T)\T = \mat{A}$.
  \item $(\mat{A}\mat{B})\T = \mat{B}\T\mat{A}\T$ \quad(the order reverses).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Rules 1--3 follow by comparing entries. For (1), the $(i,j)$ entry of $(\mat{A}+\mat{B})\T$ is by definition the
$(j,i)$ entry of $\mat{A}+\mat{B}$, which is $a_{ji}+b_{ji}$, which is the $(i,j)$ entry of
$\mat{A}\T+\mat{B}\T$. Rule (2) is the same with a scalar pulled along. Rule (3) we already saw:
reflecting twice is the identity.

Rule (4) needs more care. Let $\mat{A}$ be $m\times n$ and $\mat{B}$ be $n\times p$,
so $\mat{A}\mat{B}$ is $m\times p$ and $(\mat{A}\mat{B})\T$ is $p\times m$. The $(i,j)$ entry of
$(\mat{A}\mat{B})\T$ is the $(j,i)$ entry of $\mat{A}\mat{B}$:
\[
  \big((\mat{A}\mat{B})\T\big)_{ij}
  = (\mat{A}\mat{B})_{ji}
  = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{jk}\, b_{ki}.
\]
Now compute the $(i,j)$ entry of $\mat{B}\T\mat{A}\T$. The $(i,k)$ entry of $\mat{B}\T$ is $b_{ki}$,
and the $(k,j)$ entry of $\mat{A}\T$ is $a_{jk}$, so
\[
  (\mat{B}\T\mat{A}\T)_{ij}
  = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (\mat{B}\T)_{ik}(\mat{A}\T)_{kj}
  = \sum_{k=1}^{n} b_{ki}\, a_{jk}.
\]
The two sums are identical (multiplication of scalars commutes), so the matrices are equal.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
The order does reverse. $(\mat{A}\mat{B})\T = \mat{B}\T\mat{A}\T$, \emph{not}
$\mat{A}\T\mat{B}\T$. A quick way to see why it \emph{has} to reverse: if $\mat{A}$ is $2\times 3$ and
$\mat{B}$ is $3\times 4$, then $\mat{A}\T\mat{B}\T$ tries to multiply a $3\times 2$ by a $4\times 3$ ---
the inner dimensions $2$ and $4$ do not match, so it is not even defined. Only $\mat{B}\T\mat{A}\T$
($4\times 3$ times $3\times 2$) makes sense.
\end{trapbox}

A standard memory aid: to take off your socks and shoes you reverse the order in which you put
them on, shoes first, then socks. Transposing a product works the same way: whatever went on last
comes off first. The inverse of a product reverses in the same way,
$(\mat{A}\mat{B})\inv = \mat{B}\inv\mat{A}\inv$ (Chapter~8). The two rules combine into a useful
fact. If $\mat{A}$ is invertible, transpose both sides of $\mat{A}\mat{A}\inv=\mat{I}$ using rule (4):
\[
  (\mat{A}\inv)\T\mat{A}\T = \mat{I}\T = \mat{I}.
\]
So $(\mat{A}\inv)\T$ is the inverse of $\mat{A}\T$: transposing and inverting can be done in
either order, $(\mat{A}\T)\inv = (\mat{A}\inv)\T$. This matrix comes up often enough to have its
own short name, $\mat{A}^{-\mathsf{T}}$.

\begin{labbox}
Verify the reversal rule directly. Take
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
  \mat{B}=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Compute $\mat{A}\mat{B}$, then transpose it. Separately compute $\mat{B}\T\mat{A}\T$. They must
match. Then compute the wrong thing, $\mat{A}\T\mat{B}\T$, and watch it disagree. (You should find
$(\mat{A}\mat{B})\T = \begin{bsmallmatrix}11&4\\10&5\end{bsmallmatrix}$.)
\end{labbox}

Why does the order reverse, geometrically? Think of $\mat{A}\mat{B}\vec{x}$ as a two-stage assembly
line: first $\mat{B}$ transforms $\vec{x}$, and then $\mat{A}$ transforms $\mat{B}\vec{x}$.
In the next section we will see that $\mat{A}\T$ carries vectors backward, from the output space of
$\mat{A}$ to its input space. To travel backward along the whole line we must go back through
$\mat{A}$ first, and only then back through $\mat{B}$: the last stage going forward is the first
stage going back (Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-reversal}).

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} What is the transpose of a three-matrix product $(\mat{A}\mat{B}\mat{C})\T$?
\emph{Answer:} Group the first two factors: $((\mat{A}\mat{B})\mat{C})\T = \mat{C}\T(\mat{A}\mat{B})\T = \mat{C}\T\mat{B}\T\mat{A}\T$.
The reversal rule applies to products of any length: reverse the factor order completely and transpose each matrix.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{The transpose is the adjoint}

The next identity shows that the transpose is more than a way of rearranging a table: it tells
you how a matrix can be moved from one side of a dot product to the other.

\begin{theorem}[The transpose moves across the inner product]\label{ch10:thm-adjoint}
Let $\mat{A}$ be an $m\times n$ real matrix, $\vec{x}\in\R^n$, and $\vec{y}\in\R^m$. Then
\[
  \inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} \;=\; \inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\T\vec{y}}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write both sides as row-times-column products and use the reversal rule. The left side is
\[
  \inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}
  = (\mat{A}\vec{x})\T\vec{y}
  = \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\T\vec{y},
\]
using rule (4): $(\mat{A}\vec{x})\T = \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\T$. The right side is
\[
  \inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\T\vec{y}}
  = \vec{x}\T(\mat{A}\T\vec{y})
  = \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\T\vec{y}.
\]
Both equal the same $1\times 1$ quantity $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\T\vec{y}$.
\end{proof}

Here is what the theorem says. The matrix $\mat{A}$ takes vectors from the input
space $\R^n$ over to the output space $\R^m$. The
transpose $\mat{A}\T$ runs the other way, from $\R^m$ back to $\R^n$. It does not
matter whether you push $\vec{x}$ forward through $\mat{A}$ and then take its dot product with
$\vec{y}$ in the output space, or pull $\vec{y}$ backward through $\mat{A}\T$ and then take its
dot product with $\vec{x}$ in the input space. You get the same number
(Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-adjoint}). The transpose is the only matrix with this property for all
$\vec{x}$ and $\vec{y}$. A matrix that partners $\mat{A}$ in this way is called the
\emph{adjoint} of $\mat{A}$; for real matrices the adjoint is the transpose.

\begin{aestheticbox}
In more advanced settings the ``vectors'' can be functions and the ``matrices'' can be
\emph{operators}, rules that turn one function into another, such as taking a derivative. There are
no tables of entries to flip, so the transpose cannot be defined by swapping indices. What survives
is this relation: the adjoint $\mat{A}^{*}$ of an operator $\mat{A}$ is \emph{defined} as the
operator satisfying $\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}=\inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}^{*}\vec{y}}$ for all
$\vec{x},\vec{y}$. The matrix transpose is the first and simplest case. For example, with a suitable
dot product for functions, the adjoint of the derivative $\frac{d}{dx}$ turns out to be
$-\frac{d}{dx}$, a fact you will meet in courses on differential equations and quantum mechanics.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{strangbox}
\textbf{Before Strang:} when he writes $\mat{A}\T$, do not read it only as a flipped array of
numbers. Read it as the matrix that carries vectors from the output space of $\mat{A}$ back to its
input space, linked to $\mat{A}$ by Theorem~\ref{ch10:thm-adjoint}. That reading prepares you for
the four fundamental subspaces of Chapter~15 (four sets of vectors attached to every matrix). For
instance, the column space of $\mat{A}\T$ (all the vectors $\mat{A}\T\vec{y}$ can produce) is the
row space of $\mat{A}$ (all combinations of the rows of $\mat{A}$), and the right angles between
these subspaces come from Theorem~\ref{ch10:thm-adjoint} with one side set to zero.
\textbf{After Strang:} the same identity drives backpropagation in neural networks (next box) and
the \emph{normal equations} $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$ used for least-squares
fitting in Chapter~19.
\end{strangbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Backpropagation is a transpose.} A neural network is built from layers; a basic layer takes
an input vector $\vec{x}$ and computes $\vec{z}=\mat{W}\vec{x}$ for a matrix $\mat{W}$ of adjustable
numbers (followed by a simple nonlinear function that we ignore here). Training adjusts $\mat{W}$ to
make a single number $L$, the \emph{loss} (a measure of how wrong the network's answers are), as
small as possible. For that, it needs the \emph{gradient} of $L$ with respect to $\vec{x}$: the
vector of rates of change $\partial L/\partial x_i$, one for each entry of $\vec{x}$. Suppose the
gradient with respect to the output, $\vec{g}=\partial L/\partial \vec{z}$, is already known. The
chain rule from calculus then gives the gradient with respect to the input:
\[
  \frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec{x}} = \mat{W}\T \vec{g}.
\]
The forward pass multiplies by $\mat{W}$; the backward pass multiplies by $\mat{W}\T$, carrying
the gradient from the output space back to the input space, as in Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-adjoint}.
Deep-learning software implements this rule for every matrix product: ``to go backward through a
multiplication by $\mat{W}$, multiply by $\mat{W}\T$.'' Training a network applies it layer by
layer, from the last layer to the first.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If $\mat{A}$ is a $3\times 2$ matrix, what spaces do $\vec{x}$ and $\vec{y}$
live in for the adjoint identity $\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\T\vec{y}}$?
In which space is each dot product evaluated?
\emph{Answer:} For $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ to make sense, $\vec{x}\in\R^2$, so the output $\mat{A}\vec{x}\in\R^3$.
Thus $\vec{y}\in\R^3$. The left-hand dot product $\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}$ takes place in the
$3$-dimensional output space $\R^3$. On the right-hand side, $\mat{A}\T$ has shape $2\times 3$, so
$\mat{A}\T\vec{y}\in\R^2$; the dot product $\inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\T\vec{y}}$ takes place in the
$2$-dimensional input space $\R^2$. Two dot products in two different dimensions, yet the resulting
number is identical.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Symmetric matrices}

A natural question: what if transposing a matrix does not change it at all?

\subsection{When a matrix equals its own mirror image}

Matrices that are unchanged by transposing turn up throughout the rest of the book.

\begin{definition}[Symmetric matrix]\label{ch10:def-symmetric}
A square matrix $\mat{A}$ is \emph{symmetric} if $\mat{A}\T=\mat{A}$, that is, $a_{ij}=a_{ji}$ for all
$i,j$. Geometrically, it is unchanged when reflected across its main diagonal.
\end{definition}

For example,
\[
  \mat{S}=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 & -1 \\ 7 & 0 & 3 \\ -1 & 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
\]
is symmetric: the entry above the diagonal at position $(1,2)$ is $7$, and so is the entry below at
$(2,1)$; likewise $s_{13}=s_{31}=-1$ and $s_{23}=s_{32}=3$. The whole matrix is determined by its
diagonal and the triangle above it; the lower triangle is a copy folded over. Counting the $n$
diagonal entries plus the $n(n-1)/2$ entries above the diagonal, an $n\times n$ symmetric matrix
has only
\[
  n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}
\]
free numbers instead of $n^2$ (for $n=3$: six instead of nine).

\begin{intuitionbox}
A symmetric matrix is one where ``the influence of $i$ on $j$ equals the influence of $j$ on $i$.''
Think of a table of road distances, where the distance from town $i$ to town $j$ is
the same as from $j$ to $i$, or a table recording who is friends with whom. Symmetry encodes a
two-way relationship. Such matrices also have especially well-behaved \emph{eigenvectors}
(the directions a matrix only stretches, without turning; see Chapter~1): they can always be chosen
perpendicular to each other, a fact proved in Chapter~26.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$}{ATA} is always symmetric}

There is a way to build a symmetric matrix from \emph{any} matrix, square or
not. Take any $m\times n$ matrix $\mat{A}$ and form $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$.

\begin{proposition}[The fundamental symmetric matrices]\label{ch10:prop-ATA}
For any $m\times n$ matrix $\mat{A}$, both $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ ($n\times n$) and $\mat{A}\mat{A}\T$
($m\times m$) are symmetric.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply the transpose rules. Using $(\mat{X}\mat{Y})\T=\mat{Y}\T\mat{X}\T$ and $(\mat{X}\T)\T=\mat{X}$,
\[
  (\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\T
  = \mat{A}\T(\mat{A}\T)\T
  = \mat{A}\T\mat{A}.
\]
Since $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ equals its own transpose, it is symmetric. The same computation with the
roles swapped gives $(\mat{A}\mat{A}\T)\T=\mat{A}\mat{A}\T$.
\end{proof}

Work one out and see what the entries mean.

\begin{example}[$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ collects all the dot products of the columns]\label{ch10:ex-ATA}
Let
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
  \quad\text{with columns}\quad
  \vec{a}_1=\colvec{1\\1\\0},\ \vec{a}_2=\colvec{0\\1\\2}.
\]
Then
\[
  \mat{A}\T\mat{A}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Read the entries: the $(1,1)$ entry $2=\vec{a}_1\T\vec{a}_1=\norm{\vec{a}_1}^2$; the $(2,2)$ entry
$5=\norm{\vec{a}_2}^2$; the off-diagonal $1=\vec{a}_1\T\vec{a}_2=\vec{a}_2\T\vec{a}_1$. In general the
$(i,j)$ entry of $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is the dot product $\vec{a}_i\T\vec{a}_j$ of column $i$ with column
$j$. The matrix is symmetric because $\vec{a}_i\T\vec{a}_j=\vec{a}_j\T\vec{a}_i$. This grid
of all pairwise dot products is called the \emph{Gram matrix} of the columns.
\end{example}

\begin{focusbox}
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is the table of all dot products between the columns of $\mat{A}$. Diagonal entries
are squared lengths; off-diagonal entries measure how aligned two columns are (zero means
perpendicular). Symmetry is automatic because dot products do not care about order
(Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-gram}).
\end{focusbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Covariance matrices and PCA.} Suppose you have a data set: $m$ samples (say, $m$ people),
each with $n$ measured features (height, weight, age, \dots), stacked as the rows of a matrix
$\mat{X}$ (so $\mat{X}$ is $m\times n$). First subtract from each column its average, so that every
feature is measured as a deviation from its mean. Then the matrix
\[
  \mat{C} = \frac{1}{m-1}\,\mat{X}\T\mat{X}
\]
is the \emph{covariance matrix} of the features. Its diagonal entry $(i,i)$ is the \emph{variance}
of feature $i$, the average squared deviation, which measures how spread out that feature is. Its
off-diagonal entry $(i,j)$ is the \emph{covariance} of features $i$ and $j$: positive when the two
tend to be above average together, negative when one tends to be high while the other is low. It
is symmetric for the same reason as in Example~\ref{ch10:ex-ATA}. Because it is symmetric, its
eigenvectors can be chosen perpendicular to each other; these are the \emph{principal components},
the directions in which the data varies most. \emph{Principal component analysis} (PCA) uses them
to describe data with many features using only a few directions (Chapter~30).
\end{applicationbox}

\subsection{Splitting any square matrix into symmetric plus antisymmetric}

This fact is a good exercise in the rules. A matrix with $\mat{A}\T=-\mat{A}$ is called
\emph{antisymmetric} (or skew-symmetric); its diagonal must be zero and entries flip sign across the
fold. Every square matrix splits uniquely into a symmetric part and an antisymmetric part:
\[
  \mat{A}
  = \underbrace{\tfrac12(\mat{A}+\mat{A}\T)}_{\text{symmetric}}
  + \underbrace{\tfrac12(\mat{A}-\mat{A}\T)}_{\text{antisymmetric}}.
\]
Check the first piece is symmetric:
\[
  \Bigl(\tfrac12(\mat{A}+\mat{A}\T)\Bigr)\T = \tfrac12(\mat{A}\T+\mat{A}),
\]
the same matrix. Check the second is antisymmetric:
\[
  \Bigl(\tfrac12(\mat{A}-\mat{A}\T)\Bigr)\T = \tfrac12(\mat{A}\T-\mat{A}),
\]
which is the negative of the original. Add the two pieces and the $\mat{A}\T$ terms cancel,
returning $\mat{A}$.

\begin{connectionbox}
This decomposition mirrors a familiar fact about functions. A function is \emph{even} if
$f(-x)=f(x)$ (like $x^2$) and \emph{odd} if $f(-x)=-f(x)$ (like $x^3$). Any function splits as
\[
  f(x)=\tfrac12\bigl(f(x)+f(-x)\bigr)+\tfrac12\bigl(f(x)-f(-x)\bigr),
\]
an even part plus an odd part, in the same way a matrix splits into a part the transpose leaves
alone (symmetric) plus a part the transpose negates (antisymmetric). In both cases the operation
involved (replacing $x$ by $-x$, or transposing) undoes itself when done twice; an operation with
that property is called an \emph{involution}, and every involution splits things this way.
Antisymmetric $3\times 3$ matrices also have a geometric meaning: each one multiplies a vector
$\vec{v}$ the way a cross product $\vec{w}\times\vec{v}$ does, for some fixed vector $\vec{w}$, and
they describe how a rotating object in $\R^3$ is turning at each instant.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Permutation matrices: shuffling without distorting}

\subsection{Reordering the rows of the identity}

Now the second operation of the chapter. A permutation matrix is what you get by taking the
identity matrix and rearranging its rows. These simple matrices move space without stretching,
squashing, or bending it, and they will be our first examples of the \emph{orthogonal} matrices
defined later in this chapter.

\begin{definition}[Permutation matrix]\label{ch10:def-perm}
A \emph{permutation matrix} $\mat{P}$ is a square matrix obtained by permuting the rows (equivalently,
the columns) of the identity $\mat{I}$. Equivalently, $\mat{P}$ has exactly one $1$ in each row and
each column and zeros elsewhere.
\end{definition}

The smallest nontrivial example swaps two coordinates. In $\R^2$,
\[
  \mat{P}=\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{P}\colvec{x\\y}=\colvec{y\\x}.
\]
It takes the point $(x,y)$ to $(y,x)$ --- a reflection across the diagonal line $y=x$. In $\R^3$ a
permutation can do something more interesting, a \emph{cycle}:
\[
  \mat{P}=\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{P}\colvec{x\\y\\z}=\colvec{z\\x\\y}.
\]
The old third coordinate becomes the new first, the old first becomes the new second, and the old
second becomes the new third: a $3$-cycle. Whatever $\mat{P}$ does, it never adds,
scales, or blends coordinates. It only moves them to new positions, which amounts to relabeling
the coordinate axes.

Because $\mat{P}\vec{e}_j$ is the $j$th column of $\mat{P}$, the action on the standard basis is the
fastest way to read a permutation. Here the columns of $\mat{P}$ are $\vec{e}_2,\vec{e}_3,\vec{e}_1$,
so $\vec{e}_1\mapsto\vec{e}_2\mapsto\vec{e}_3\mapsto\vec{e}_1$: the three axes chase each other around
a single loop (Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-cycle}).

A permutation matrix is a perfect shuffle of the coordinate axes. The standard basis vectors
$\vec{e}_1,\dots,\vec{e}_n$ get permuted among themselves and sent back to standard basis vectors ---
none is bent or stretched. The unit cube goes to the unit cube; only the labels on the corners move.
This is why permutations preserve every length and every angle.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Multiplying by $\mat{P}$ on the left vs. on the right}

Keep track of which side $\mat{P}$ is on: multiplying \emph{on the left} by $\mat{P}$ permutes the \emph{rows} of
whatever it hits; multiplying \emph{on the right} permutes the \emph{columns}. Watch it with the swap
matrix:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix}c&d\\a&b\end{bmatrix}
  \quad(\text{rows swapped}),
  \qquad
  \begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix}b&a\\d&c\end{bmatrix}
  \quad(\text{columns swapped}).
\]
This is the row-exchange operation you met during elimination: when a pivot is zero you swap
rows, and that swap is recorded as a left multiplication by a permutation matrix. (In $\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$
the $\mat{P}$ records which rows got exchanged.)

\subsection{How many are there, and how do they multiply?}

There are exactly $n!$ (``$n$ factorial'', $n\cdot(n-1)\cdots 2\cdot 1$) permutation matrices of
size $n\times n$, one for each way to reorder $n$ items: for $n=2$ there are $2!=2$ (the identity
and the swap), for $n=3$ there are $3!=6$ (Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-six-perms}), and so on.

The product of two permutation matrices is another permutation matrix (do one shuffle, then the
other: still a shuffle). The identity matrix is a permutation (the do-nothing shuffle). And every
permutation matrix has an inverse that is also a permutation matrix (reversing the shuffle undoes it).
Matrix multiplication is also \emph{associative}: $(\mat{P}_1\mat{P}_2)\mat{P}_3=\mat{P}_1(\mat{P}_2\mat{P}_3)$.
A collection with these four properties (products stay in the collection, the product is associative,
there is an identity, and every member has an inverse in the collection) is what mathematicians call
a \emph{group}, as in Chapter~1.

\begin{connectionbox}
The $n\times n$ permutation matrices form a group under multiplication, and it is an exact copy of
the \emph{symmetric group} $S_n$, the group of all rearrangements of $n$ symbols (met briefly in
Chapter~9). Group theory began here: in the 1830s \'Evariste Galois studied how the roots of a
polynomial equation can be rearranged. When you multiply two permutation matrices you are
composing two shuffles, and the order matters: doing swap-then-cycle generally differs from
cycle-then-swap (Exercise~\ref{ch10:ex-count}). A group in which order can matter like this is
called \emph{nonabelian} (noncommutative), and $S_3$, with its six members, is the smallest
nonabelian group. In fact, by a theorem of Cayley, every finite group can be realized as a group of
permutation matrices.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{The inverse of a permutation is its transpose}

To undo a shuffle you shuffle back, and the ``shuffle back'' matrix is the \emph{transpose} of the
original. This is where the two operations of the chapter meet.

\begin{theorem}[Permutations are orthogonal]\label{ch10:thm-perm-orth}
Every permutation matrix $\mat{P}$ satisfies
\[
  \mat{P}\T\mat{P} = \mat{I},
  \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
  \mat{P}\inv = \mat{P}\T.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The columns of $\mat{P}$ are the standard basis vectors $\vec{e}_1,\dots,\vec{e}_n$ in \emph{some}
order; say column $j$ of $\mat{P}$ is $\vec{e}_{\sigma(j)}$, where $\sigma(j)$ is the row in which
column $j$ has its $1$. The $(i,j)$ entry of $\mat{P}\T\mat{P}$ is the dot product of column $i$
with column $j$ of $\mat{P}$:
\[
  (\mat{P}\T\mat{P})_{ij} = \vec{e}_{\sigma(i)}\T\,\vec{e}_{\sigma(j)}.
\]
Standard basis vectors have length $1$ and are perpendicular to each other: $\vec{e}_a\T\vec{e}_b$
is $1$ when $a=b$ and $0$ otherwise. Different columns have their $1$ in different rows, so
$\sigma(i)=\sigma(j)$ exactly when $i=j$. So the dot product is $1$ on the diagonal and $0$ off
it. That is the identity: $\mat{P}\T\mat{P}=\mat{I}$. For a square matrix, a one-sided inverse is
automatically the two-sided inverse (Chapter~8), so $\mat{P}\inv=\mat{P}\T$.
\end{proof}

This is your \emph{first orthogonal matrix}. A square matrix $\mat{Q}$ is called \emph{orthogonal} when
$\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$. Since the $(i,j)$ entry of $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}$ is the dot product of
columns $i$ and $j$, this means its columns are \emph{orthonormal}: every column has length $1$
and every pair of distinct columns is perpendicular (dot product zero).
Theorem~\ref{ch10:thm-perm-orth} says
permutation matrices are orthogonal, and they are the simplest example: their columns are
the standard basis vectors, shuffled.

\begin{example}[Transpose undoes the cycle]\label{ch10:ex-cycle-inverse}
Take the $3$-cycle from before and its transpose:
\[
  \mat{P}=\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{P}\T=\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Recall $\mat{P}$ sends $(x,y,z)\mapsto(z,x,y)$. Its transpose sends $(x,y,z)\mapsto(y,z,x)$ --- the
opposite cycle. Compose them:
\[
  \mat{P}\T\mat{P}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mat{I}.
\]
Shuffle forward, shuffle back, home again. For a plain swap like
$\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ the matrix equals its own transpose, so
the swap is its own inverse, which matches the picture: reflecting across $y=x$ twice does nothing.
\end{example}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Here the two halves of this chapter fit together. By Theorem~\ref{ch10:thm-adjoint} (with
$\mat{Q}\vec{x}$ in the role of $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ and $\mat{Q}\vec{y}$ in the role of $\vec{y}$),
\[
  \inner{\mat{Q}\vec{x}}{\mat{Q}\vec{y}} = \inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}\vec{y}}.
\]
So if $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$, the matrix $\mat{Q}$ preserves every dot product, and then
$\mat{Q}\inv=\mat{Q}\T$. (The converse also holds: a matrix that preserves every dot product must
have $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$.) Permutations preserve lengths by shuffling axes; in Chapter~17,
rotations and reflections at any angle will do the same.
\end{aestheticbox}

Orthogonal matrices, and permutations in particular, preserve lengths and angles. The proof is
short.

\begin{proposition}[Orthogonal matrices preserve length and angle]\label{ch10:prop-isometry}
If $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$, then for all $\vec{x},\vec{y}$,
\[
  (\mat{Q}\vec{x})\T(\mat{Q}\vec{y}) = \vec{x}\T\vec{y},
  \qquad\text{and in particular}\qquad
  \norm{\mat{Q}\vec{x}} = \norm{\vec{x}}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Use the reversal rule $(\mat{Q}\vec{x})\T=\vec{x}\T\mat{Q}\T$:
\[
  (\mat{Q}\vec{x})\T(\mat{Q}\vec{y})
  = \vec{x}\T\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}\,\vec{y}
  = \vec{x}\T\mat{I}\,\vec{y}
  = \vec{x}\T\vec{y}.
\]
Setting $\vec{y}=\vec{x}$ gives $\norm{\mat{Q}\vec{x}}^2=\norm{\vec{x}}^2$, and lengths are
nonnegative, so $\norm{\mat{Q}\vec{x}}=\norm{\vec{x}}$. Angles are preserved too, because the angle
between two vectors is computed from their dot product and lengths (Chapter~4), and $\mat{Q}$
changes none of these.
\end{proof}

For a permutation you can see this directly: $\mat{P}\vec{x}$ has the same numbers as $\vec{x}$
in a different order, and reordering the entries cannot change $\sqrt{x_1^2+\cdots+x_n^2}$
(Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-isometry}). The
proposition gives the general reason (orthogonality), and it agrees with the concrete one (same
numbers, reshuffled). The determinant of a permutation matrix is $\pm 1$: as Chapter~9 noted, it
is $+1$ if the permutation can be done by an even number of swaps and $-1$ for an odd number. This
``sign of the permutation'' returns in Chapter~22, where it gives the sign of each term in a formula for the determinant.

\begin{walkbox}
Take a walk and turn this over. A permutation matrix preserves all lengths and angles, so it moves
space rigidly, like a solid object: it is a rotation, a reflection, or a combination of the two.
Question: which permutations are pure rotations (determinant $+1$) and which include a reflection
(determinant $-1$)? Try the $2\times 2$ swap (is exchanging $x$ and $y$ a rotation, or a flip?) and
the $3$-cycle. Can a single \emph{transposition} (a permutation that swaps two entries and leaves
the rest alone) ever be a rotation? What does its determinant tell you?
\end{walkbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Why must every orthogonal matrix have determinant equal to $+1$ or $-1$?
\emph{Answer:} Take the determinant of both sides of $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$.
Using two facts proved in Chapters~21 and~22, $\det(\mat{A}\mat{B})=\det(\mat{A})\det(\mat{B})$ and
$\det(\mat{Q}\T)=\det(\mat{Q})$, we find $\big(\det(\mat{Q})\big)^2 = \det(\mat{I}) = 1$, which
forces $\det(\mat{Q}) = \pm 1$.
A determinant of $+1$ means the transformation preserves orientation (like a pure rotation);
a determinant of $-1$ means orientation is reversed (it includes a reflection, turning a left hand
into a right hand).
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Putting transposes and permutations together}

The two ideas meet in elimination.
When Gaussian elimination needs to avoid a zero pivot, it swaps rows, which is a left multiplication
by a permutation $\mat{P}$. Elimination ``with row exchanges'' is summed up by the
factorization
\[
  \mat{P}\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{U},
\]
where $\mat{P}$ is a permutation collecting all the swaps, $\mat{L}$ is lower triangular, and
$\mat{U}$ is upper triangular. Because $\mat{P}\inv=\mat{P}\T$, you can move the permutation to the
other side at no cost: $\mat{A}=\mat{P}\T\mat{L}\mat{U}$. No inverse needs computing; the transpose
\emph{is} the inverse. Numerical software goes one step further and stores a permutation as a list
of row swaps rather than as a full matrix of mostly zeros; applying $\mat{P}\T$ then means
performing the same swaps in reverse order.

\begin{example}[A row swap rescuing a pivot]\label{ch10:ex-pivot}
Consider
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The top-left pivot is $0$, so elimination cannot proceed. Swap the rows with
$\mat{P}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$:
\[
  \mat{P}\mat{A}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1\\1&0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix},
\]
which now has a nonzero pivot $3$ in the top-left and is already upper triangular (here $\mat{L}=\mat{I}$,
$\mat{U}=\mat{P}\mat{A}$). To recover $\mat{A}$ itself, multiply by $\mat{P}\T=\mat{P}$ on the left
again: the swap is its own undo (Figure~\ref{ch10:fig-pivot-rescue}).
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
A common error is to write $\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}\mat{P}$ or otherwise put the permutation
on the right. The swaps in elimination act on \emph{rows}, so $\mat{P}$ must sit on the \emph{left}:
$\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$. Putting it on the right would permute columns, which is a different
operation (it reorders the unknowns, not the equations). Left for rows, right for columns.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{formalbox}
Collecting the main identities of this chapter, all for real matrices:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $(\mat{A}\mat{B})\T=\mat{B}\T\mat{A}\T$ and $(\mat{A}\T)\T=\mat{A}$.
  \item $\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}=\inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\T\vec{y}}$ for all $\vec{x},\vec{y}$
        (the adjoint relation, which characterizes $\mat{A}\T$ uniquely).
  \item $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ and $\mat{A}\mat{A}\T$ are symmetric; the $(i,j)$ entry of $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$
        is $\vec{a}_i\T\vec{a}_j$ (the Gram matrix of the columns).
  \item A permutation matrix has one $1$ per row and column; it is orthogonal,
        $\mat{P}\T\mat{P}=\mat{I}$, so $\mat{P}\inv=\mat{P}\T$.
  \item Orthogonal matrices ($\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$) preserve dot products, lengths, and angles;
        their determinant is $\pm 1$.
\end{itemize}
\end{formalbox}

\subsection{Looking forward: from matrix arithmetic to vector spaces}

Throughout the first ten chapters, our calculations have revolved around concrete systems of equations
and grids of numbers: row reductions, matrix products, inverses, factorizations like $\mat{L}\mat{U}$
and $\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$, and now the transpose $\mat{A}\T$.

Every rectangular matrix $\mat{A}$ of shape $m\times n$ connects two spaces:
it pushes vectors forward from $\R^n$ to $\R^m$, while its transpose $\mat{A}\T$ carries vectors back
from $\R^m$ to $\R^n$. Some inputs get stretched into new directions; others get squashed completely to zero.
What shape does the set of all reachable outputs have? What shape does the set of inputs that get
squashed to zero have?

To answer these questions we move from entry-by-entry arithmetic to \emph{vector spaces and
subspaces}, the subject of Part~III, starting in Chapter~11. That path leads to Gilbert Strang's
``big picture of linear algebra'' in Chapter~15, where the transpose returns: the inputs that
$\mat{A}$ squashes to zero turn out to be perpendicular to every output that $\mat{A}\T$ can produce.

\begin{historybox}
The transpose began as a bookkeeping device. In the 1850s the British mathematician Arthur Cayley
introduced matrices as a compact way to record how algebraic equations change when you change
coordinates. Flipping a table across its main diagonal turned the equations' rows into columns, and
Cayley called the result the ``transposed matrix.'' For decades it was treated mainly as a
convenient piece of notation.

Its deeper meaning emerged in the early twentieth century, when David Hilbert, Stefan Banach,
and Frigyes Riesz developed \emph{functional analysis}, the study of spaces whose ``vectors'' are
functions and whose ``matrices'' are operations such as differentiation or integration. In such
spaces there is no table of entries to flip, so how do you transpose a derivative? The answer was
to define the \emph{adjoint} by the balance
$\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}^{*}\vec{y}}$ from
Theorem~\ref{ch10:thm-adjoint}, using a dot product for functions. (A key result here, the
\emph{Riesz representation theorem}, says that in such a space every linear way of turning a
vector into a number is the dot product with some fixed vector.) The matrix transpose is the
special case of this definition for ordinary vectors in $\R^n$.

Permutation matrices became important when electronic computers arrived in the 1940s. Computers
store numbers with a fixed number of digits, so every arithmetic step rounds slightly. Some early
analyses, Harold Hotelling's in 1943 for one, predicted that these rounding errors would grow so
fast during Gaussian elimination that the answers for large systems would be worthless. In 1961
the British numerical analyst James Wilkinson showed that swapping rows to keep each pivot as large
as possible (\emph{partial pivoting}, recorded as $\mat{P}\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{U}$) keeps the
error under control in practice. He also showed that in the worst case, partial pivoting can still
let errors grow by a factor of $2^{n-1}$ for an $n\times n$ matrix. Such bad cases almost never
occur in real problems, and explaining exactly why is still a research question.

Two open problems involve permutations directly. A network (a \emph{graph}: dots joined by lines)
can be recorded as a matrix with a $1$ in position $(i,j)$ when dots $i$ and $j$ are joined.
Asking whether two networks have the same structure with the dots renamed is the
\emph{graph isomorphism} problem; in matrix terms it asks whether $\mat{P}\T\mat{A}\mat{P} = \mat{B}$
for some permutation matrix $\mat{P}$ (Exercise~\ref{ch10:ex-graph-iso}). In 2015 L\'aszl\'o Babai
announced a method much faster than anything known before, but whether there is a truly efficient
method is still unknown. And when elimination is run on huge matrices that are mostly zeros, the
order of the rows and columns decides how many of those zeros turn into nonzeros (``fill-in'') and
so how much memory and time the computation needs. Finding the best order is known to be
computationally hard, so practical software relies on good rules of thumb instead.
\end{historybox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Warmup flips]\label{ch10:ex-warmup}
Write down the transpose of each matrix and state its shape.
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 5 & 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix},\qquad
  \mat{B}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 4 & 9 \end{bmatrix},\qquad
  \vec{c}=\colvec{3\\-2\\6}.
\]
Which of these (if any) is symmetric, and how can you tell at a glance?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Reversal by hand]\label{ch10:ex-reversal}
Let $\mat{A}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\1&3\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and
$\mat{B}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&5\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Compute $\mat{A}\mat{B}$,
$(\mat{A}\mat{B})\T$, $\mat{B}\T\mat{A}\T$, and $\mat{A}\T\mat{B}\T$. Confirm which two agree and
explain in one sentence why the fourth one is the odd one out.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Dot products as transposes]\label{ch10:ex-dotprod}
Let $\vec{u}=\colvec{1\\2\\-2}$ and $\vec{v}=\colvec{3\\0\\1}$. Compute $\vec{u}\T\vec{v}$,
$\vec{v}\T\vec{u}$, $\norm{\vec{u}}^2=\vec{u}\T\vec{u}$, and the outer product $\vec{u}\vec{v}\T$
(a $3\times 3$ matrix). Is the outer product symmetric? Why or why not?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Building Gram matrices]\label{ch10:ex-gram}
For $\mat{A}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ compute $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$
and $\mat{A}\mat{A}\T$. Verify both are symmetric. Identify which entry of $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is the
dot product of the two columns of $\mat{A}$, and check it by computing that dot product directly.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Symmetric plus antisymmetric]\label{ch10:ex-split}
Split $\mat{A}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}4&1\\7&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ into its symmetric part
$\tfrac12(\mat{A}+\mat{A}\T)$ and its antisymmetric part $\tfrac12(\mat{A}-\mat{A}\T)$. Add them back
to confirm you recover $\mat{A}$. What must the diagonal of any antisymmetric matrix be, and why?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Permutations and their actions]\label{ch10:ex-permaction}
Let $\mat{P}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&1\\0&1&0\\1&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Describe in words
what $\mat{P}\vec{x}$ does to $\vec{x}=(x_1,x_2,x_3)$. Compute $\mat{P}\T$ and verify
$\mat{P}\T\mat{P}=\mat{I}$. Is this $\mat{P}$ its own inverse? Explain using the cycle structure.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Left versus right]\label{ch10:ex-leftright}
With $\mat{P}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and an arbitrary
$\mat{M}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, compute $\mat{P}\mat{M}$ and
$\mat{M}\mat{P}$. State precisely what each does to $\mat{M}$ and connect it to row swaps versus
column swaps in elimination.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Adjoint by example]\label{ch10:ex-adjoint}
Let $\mat{A}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\0&1\\3&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$,
$\vec{x}=\colvec{1\\-1}$, and $\vec{y}=\colvec{2\\0\\1}$. Compute $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ and
$\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}$; then compute $\mat{A}\T\vec{y}$ and $\inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\T\vec{y}}$.
Confirm the two numbers agree, illustrating Theorem~\ref{ch10:thm-adjoint}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Counting and composing]\label{ch10:ex-count}
How many $3\times 3$ permutation matrices are there? Pick the swap of rows $1$ and $2$, call it
$\mat{P}_{12}$, and the swap of rows $2$ and $3$, call it $\mat{P}_{23}$. Compute $\mat{P}_{12}\mat{P}_{23}$
and $\mat{P}_{23}\mat{P}_{12}$. Are they equal? What does your answer say about whether the group of
permutation matrices is commutative?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Orthogonality preserves geometry]\label{ch10:ex-isometry}
Let $\mat{P}$ be any $3\times 3$ permutation matrix and $\vec{x}=(1,-2,2)$. Without choosing a
specific $\mat{P}$, explain why $\norm{\mat{P}\vec{x}}=3$ no matter which permutation you pick. Then
prove in general that if $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$ and $\mat{R}\T\mat{R}=\mat{I}$ then the product
$\mat{Q}\mat{R}$ is also orthogonal.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A proof to finish]\label{ch10:ex-proof}
Prove that if $\mat{A}$ is any $m\times n$ matrix and $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}=\mat{0}$ (the zero matrix),
then $\mat{A}=\mat{0}$. \emph{Hint:} look at the diagonal entries of $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ and recall
what they equal in terms of the columns of $\mat{A}$. In particular, $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$
cannot collapse to zero unless $\mat{A}$ already was zero, which is the seed of why
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is invertible exactly when $\mat{A}$ has independent columns.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Elimination with row swaps: \texorpdfstring{$\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$}{PA=LU}]\label{ch10:ex-pa-lu}
Consider the matrix
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Explain why standard Gaussian elimination cannot begin without swapping rows.
  \item Let $\mat{P}$ be the permutation matrix that exchanges row 1 and row 2. Write down $\mat{P}$
        and compute the product $\mat{P}\mat{A}$.
  \item Perform elimination on $\mat{P}\mat{A}$ to find a unit lower-triangular matrix $\mat{L}$ and
        an upper-triangular matrix $\mat{U}$ such that $\mat{P}\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{U}$.
  \item Using $\mat{P}\inv=\mat{P}\T$, write down the factorization of the original matrix as
        $\mat{A} = \mat{P}\T\mat{L}\mat{U}$, and verify by multiplication that it recovers $\mat{A}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Permutation similarity and graph isomorphism]\label{ch10:ex-graph-iso}
Let a $3$-node network (a \emph{graph}: nodes joined by lines called \emph{edges}) be recorded by
its \emph{adjacency matrix}, the table with a $1$ where two nodes are joined and $0$ elsewhere:
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Here $a_{ij}=1$ means there is an edge between node $i$ and node $j$; edges have no direction, so
$\mat{A}$ is symmetric. This is a line network: node 1 is connected to node 2, which is connected
to node 3.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Let $\mat{P}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ be the
        permutation that exchanges node 1 and node 2. Compute $\mat{B} = \mat{P}\T\mat{A}\mat{P}$.
  \item Explain why left-multiplication by $\mat{P}\T$ permutes the rows while right-multiplication
        by $\mat{P}$ permutes the columns, so that $\mat{P}\T\mat{A}\mat{P}$ renames both source and
        target of every edge consistently.
  \item Verify that $\mat{B}$ is also symmetric. Which node is now the central hub connected to the
        other two? Conclude that $\mat{B}$ represents the exact same network structure, merely with
        the node labels renumbered.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
