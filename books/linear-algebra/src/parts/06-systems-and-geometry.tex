\chapter{Systems of Linear Equations and Their Geometry}

In Chapter~5, we watched matrices act as machines: feed in an input vector
$\vec{x}$, and the machine hands back an output vector $\vec{b} = A\vec{x}$.
That was the forward question -- run the transformation and see where space
lands. Solving a system of linear equations is the reverse question: you are
handed the destination vector $\vec{b}$, and you must deduce which input
vector(s) $\vec{x}$ the machine took there:
\[
  A\vec{x} = \vec{b}.
\]
Did exactly one input land at $\vec{b}$? Did a whole family of inputs
collapse onto it? Or is $\vec{b}$ somewhere the machine can never reach?

Before you ever wrote matrices as machines, you likely first encountered this question
in elementary algebra: ``two pencils and a notebook cost \$5, three pencils and two
notebooks cost \$8, find the price of each.'' You may have solved it by substitution
or by adding equations to eliminate a variable.

This chapter asks you to slow down and \emph{look} at what you were doing,
because that word problem already contains the main geometric ideas of
linear algebra. With $x$ the price of a pencil and $y$ the price of a
notebook, the system is
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    2x + 1y &= 5,\\
    3x + 2y &= 8.
  \end{aligned}
\]
It can be \emph{seen} in two different ways, and the rest of the subject
depends on holding both pictures in your head at once. One picture is about
\emph{rows}: lines and planes crossing. The other is about \emph{columns}:
arrows adding up. They describe the same equations and give the same answer,
but they look different, and much of what comes after this chapter is
easier once you can switch between them.

We draw both pictures in two and three dimensions, with small numbers you
can check by hand, and then answer three questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item When does a system have \emph{exactly one} solution?
  \item When does it have \emph{none}?
  \item When does it have \emph{infinitely many}?
\end{itemize}
Those are the \emph{only} three possibilities: a linear system can never
have, say, exactly two solutions. The answer depends on whether the matrix
squashes space. A \emph{square} matrix (as many rows as columns) is called
\emph{nonsingular} when its columns point in enough different directions to
span the whole space, and \emph{singular} when they collapse onto a smaller
line or plane through the origin.

\section{Two pictures of one system}

We use this system as the running example (the ``flagship'') for the whole
chapter:
\begin{equation}
  \label{ch06:flagship}
  \begin{aligned}
    2x + 1y &= 5,\\
    3x + 2y &= 8.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
There are two ways to read these symbols, and both are correct. Most people
are taught only the first, but the second is the one the rest of the book
builds on.

\subsection{The row picture: lines that cross}

Read \eqref{ch06:flagship} one row at a time. The first row, $2x+1y=5$, is a
\emph{constraint} on the pair $(x,y)$: a condition that only some pairs meet. The set of all points $(x,y)$ that
satisfy it is a straight line in the plane. The second row, $3x+2y=8$, is a
different constraint, a different line. A pair $(x,y)$ solves the
\emph{system} exactly when it sits on \emph{both} lines at once -- that is,
at the point where the two lines cross.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The row picture turns ``solve the system'' into ``find where the lines
meet.'' Each equation is a line (in $2$ unknowns) or a plane (in $3$
unknowns). A solution is a point common to all of them.
\end{intuitionbox}

You can read the answer right off Figure~\ref{ch06:rowpic}: the lines cross
at $(x,y)=(2,1)$. Check it: $2(2)+1=5$, yes; $3(2)+2(1)=8$, yes. Two lines in
a plane, if they are not parallel, meet in exactly one point. Everything that
can go wrong with a linear system comes from that ``if they are not
parallel,'' and we return to it in Section~\ref{ch06:sec-three}.

\subsection{The column picture: arrows that add up}

Now read the \emph{same} system \eqref{ch06:flagship} a different way -- not
row by row, but \emph{column by column}. Stack the two equations
into a single vector equation:
\[
  \colvec{2x+1y\\3x+2y} = \colvec{5\\8}.
\]
The left side is a sum of two pieces, one carrying $x$ and one carrying $y$:
\[
  x\colvec{2\\3} + y\colvec{1\\2} = \colvec{5\\8}.
\]
Name the pieces: $\vec{a}_1=\colvec{2\\3}$ is the column of $x$-coefficients,
$\vec{a}_2=\colvec{1\\2}$ is the column of $y$-coefficients, and
$\vec{b}=\colvec{5\\8}$ is the target. The question is no longer ``where do
two lines cross?'' It is now:

\begin{focusbox}
\emph{How much of $\vec{a}_1$ and how much of $\vec{a}_2$ must I add to land
exactly on $\vec{b}$?} The unknowns $x$ and $y$ are the \emph{amounts}. A
linear system asks you to build the target vector $\vec b$ out of the
\emph{columns} of the matrix.
\end{focusbox}

The columns of the system are the arrows $\vec a_1,\vec a_2$. We are looking
for the recipe $x\,\vec a_1 + y\,\vec a_2$ that reaches $\vec b$. This is a
\emph{linear combination} of the columns, the operation from
Chapter~3. Solving a linear system \emph{is} asking whether $\vec b$ lies in
the span of the columns, and if so, with what weights.
Figure~\ref{ch06:colpic} shows the recipe that works.

The two pictures use the exact same numbers, read across the rows or down the
columns. In the row picture, each row $(2,1)$ and $(3,2)$ defines a \emph{line},
and the right-hand side $(5,8)$ sets where those lines sit. In the column
picture, each column $(2,3)$ and $(1,2)$ is an \emph{arrow} we scale and add
to reach the single \emph{target arrow} $(5,8)$.

\begin{trapbox}
The most common early confusion in linear algebra is mixing up the rows and
the columns of $\colvec{2 & 1\\3 & 2}$. The \emph{rows}
$(2,1),(3,2)$ are coefficients of the two equations -- they make the
\emph{lines}. The \emph{columns} $\colvec{2\\3},\colvec{1\\2}$ are the
arrows you combine -- they make the \emph{span}. When you read off a column
of a matrix, read \emph{down}.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Matrix form: the whole thing as $A\vec x = \vec b$}

Both pictures fit into one short statement. Collect the columns
side by side into a \emph{matrix} $A$, stack the unknowns into a vector
$\vec x$, and the targets into $\vec b$:
\[
  A=\colvec{2 & 1\\3 & 2},\qquad
  \vec x=\colvec{x\\y},\qquad
  \vec b=\colvec{5\\8},
\]
so that the system is
\[
  A\vec x = \vec b.
\]
The short notation hides the two pictures but does not lose them. The
product $A\vec x$ can be computed two ways, and they are exactly our two
pictures (Figure~\ref{ch06:rowcolway}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Row way:} the $i$-th entry of $A\vec x$ is the dot product
  of row $i$ of $A$ with $\vec x$ (multiply matching entries and add).
  Setting each result equal to the matching entry of $\vec b$ gives back the
  original equations (the lines).
  \item \textbf{Column way:} $A\vec x = x\,(\text{column }1) +
  y\,(\text{column }2)$, a linear combination of the columns (the arrows).
\end{itemize}

Keep both readings of $A\vec x$ alive. When we discuss whether a solution
exists, the column reading is the useful one; when we \emph{compute} one by
elimination, the row reading is.

\begin{strangbox}
\emph{Before Strang:} the first lecture in Gilbert Strang's linear algebra
course is ``the geometry of linear equations,'' and it is built on this
row-versus-column split. Strang stresses that students most often overlook
the column picture, and that ``$A\vec x$ is a linear combination of the
columns of $A$'' is the main point to take away. With both pictures in hand,
his first lecture will read easily.
\emph{After Strang:} the column picture is also where the four fundamental
subspaces start: the set of all reachable $\vec b$ is the \emph{column
space}, which you meet properly in Chapter~12.
\end{strangbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: two columns in three dimensions.}
Before reading on, picture a system with $3$ equations and $2$ unknowns:
\[
  x\colvec{a_1\\a_2\\a_3} + y\colvec{b_1\\b_2\\b_3} = \colvec{c_1\\c_2\\c_3}.
\]
What does the row picture look like? Each equation is a plane in $\R^3$.
Now look at the column picture, writing $\vec a_1=(a_1,a_2,a_3)$ and
$\vec a_2=(b_1,b_2,b_3)$ for the two columns: you have just \emph{two} column
arrows in three-dimensional space $\R^3$. All their combinations
$x\vec{a}_1 + y\vec{a}_2$ fill at most a flat 2D sheet through the origin.
Unless the target arrow $\vec b=(c_1,c_2,c_3)$ happens to lie on that sheet,
\emph{no solution exists}. The column picture shows at a glance why
``tall'' systems (more equations than unknowns) rarely have exact solutions.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{A fully worked $2\times 2$, both ways}

Now solve \eqref{ch06:flagship} by hand, once in each picture, so the
geometry has numbers under it.

\begin{example}[Substitution, the row way]
\label{ch06:ex-sub}
From the first equation, $y = 5 - 2x$. Substitute into the second:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  3x + 2(5-2x) &= 8\\
  3x + 10 - 4x &= 8\\
  -x &= -2\\
  x &= 2.
\end{aligned}
\]
Then $y = 5 - 2(2) = 1$. So $(x,y)=(2,1)$, matching the crossing point in
Figure~\ref{ch06:rowpic}. Substitution is the row picture done by algebra:
equation one ties $y$ to $x$ (we stay on the first line), and equation two
picks out the one point of that line that is also on the second line.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Combination, the column way]
\label{ch06:ex-col}
We want weights $x,y$ with $x\colvec{2\\3}+y\colvec{1\\2}=\colvec{5\\8}$.
To get rid of $y$, combine the two coordinates so that the second column
$\colvec{1\\2}$ cancels: take $2$ times the top coordinate minus $1$ times
the bottom coordinate, since $2\cdot 1 - 1\cdot 2 = 0$. Doing this to both
sides gives $(2\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 3)x = 2\cdot 5 - 1\cdot 8$. Cancelling the
first column in the same way (take $2$ times the bottom minus $3$ times the
top) isolates $y$. The results are
\[
  x = \frac{2\cdot 5 - 1\cdot 8}{2\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 3}=\frac{10-8}{4-3}=2,
  \qquad
  y = \frac{2\cdot 8 - 3\cdot 5}{2\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 3}=\frac{16-15}{1}=1.
\]
Same answer, $x=2,\ y=1$. Both fractions have the same denominator,
$2\cdot2-1\cdot3=1$. That number is called the \emph{determinant} of $A$
(written $\det A$), because it \emph{determines} whether the system has
exactly one solution. In Chapter~5 we viewed matrices as machines
transforming space. The determinant is the \emph{area scaling factor} of
that machine: it measures how much any 2D region expands or shrinks under
the transformation (with a minus sign if the transformation flips the plane
over, like a mirror). When this factor is nonzero, the two column arrows
enclose a parallelogram of nonzero area, so they point in different
directions and can reach any target $\vec b$ in the plane. When it is zero,
the area has collapsed: the matrix squashes the whole plane onto a line, and
some targets can no longer be reached. We construct determinants in full
generality in Chapter~21; for $2\times 2$ matrices this is all we need.
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Solve $\colvec{1 & 2\\1 & 3}\colvec{x\\y}=\colvec{4\\5}$ \textbf{twice}:
once by substitution (find the crossing point of the two lines) and once by
combination (what multiples of $\colvec{1\\1}$ and $\colvec{2\\3}$ reach
$\colvec{4\\5}$?). You should get $x=2,\ y=1$ both ways. Then sketch both
pictures on scrap paper, and check that the two different constructions
produce the same numbers.
\end{labbox}

\section{The three possibilities, and why there are only three}
\label{ch06:sec-three}

So far our lines crossed once and our columns reached the target once. But
depending on the matrix and the target vector, a system can also have
\emph{no} solution or \emph{infinitely many}. Below are all three outcomes
in the $2\times 2$ setting, each shown in both pictures.

\subsection{One solution: lines cross, columns spread}

This is the case we just did. Two lines with \emph{different slopes} cross
at exactly one point. In the column picture, the two arrows
$\vec a_1,\vec a_2$ point in \emph{different directions}, so by scaling and
adding them you can reach \emph{any} target $\vec b$ in the plane, and in
exactly one way. Figure~\ref{ch06:one} shows both pictures.

    \draw[vecblue,very thick] (-0.2,2.775)--(3.2,0.225);

\subsection{No solution: parallel lines, collinear columns missing the target}

Take a system whose two lines are \emph{parallel but not equal}:
\begin{equation}
  \label{ch06:nosol}
  \begin{aligned}
    1x + 2y &= 3,\\
    1x + 2y &= 7.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The rows have the same left side, $x+2y$, but demand two different values,
$3$ and $7$. No point can make $x+2y$ equal $3$ and $7$ at once. The two
lines never meet: they are parallel. \emph{No solution.}

Now switch to columns. The system is
$x\colvec{1\\1}+y\colvec{2\\2}=\colvec{3\\7}$. But $\colvec{1\\1}$ and
$\colvec{2\\2}$ point the \emph{same way}: the second is twice the
first. Such vectors are called \emph{collinear}: they lie along one line
through the origin. Every combination
$x\colvec{1\\1}+y\colvec{2\\2}=(x+2y)\colvec{1\\1}$
lands somewhere on the single line through $\colvec{1\\1}$. But the target
$\colvec{3\\7}$ is \emph{off} that line (it would need its two entries equal,
and $3\neq7$). So no combination reaches it, as Figure~\ref{ch06:none}
shows.

\subsection{Infinitely many: same line twice, target on the column line}

Keep the columns collinear, but now put the target \emph{on} their line:
\begin{equation}
  \label{ch06:infsol}
  \begin{aligned}
    1x + 2y &= 3,\\
    2x + 4y &= 6.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The second equation is twice the first, so it describes the \emph{same
line}. Any point on the line $x+2y=3$ satisfies both. There are
infinitely many such points: $x=3-2t,\ y=t$ for every real number $t$ (here
$t$ is a free label that slides you along the line). In the
column picture, $\colvec{1\\2}$ and $\colvec{2\\4}$ are collinear (the second
is twice the first), and the target $\colvec{3\\6}$ \emph{is} on that line,
so it can be reached, but in infinitely many ways, because you can trade
some of one column for some of the other: $\colvec{3\\6}=3\colvec{1\\2}$,
but also $=1\colvec{1\\2}+1\colvec{2\\4}$, and so on. Figure~\ref{ch06:inf}
shows both pictures.

\end{figure}

\begin{focusbox}
Read off the pattern. Whether the system has $0$, $1$, or $\infty$ solutions
is decided by two yes/no questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Are the columns spread out} (point in different
  directions)? If \emph{yes}, you can reach \emph{every} $\vec b$, uniquely:
  one solution, always.
  \item If the columns have \textbf{collapsed} (collinear), then it depends
  on $\vec b$: if $\vec b$ lies on their line, \emph{infinitely many}
  solutions; if not, \emph{none}.
\end{enumerate}
``Spread out'' versus ``collapsed'' is what decides everything, and it has
a name: \emph{nonsingular} versus \emph{singular}.
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{Why never exactly two?}

Why can't a system have exactly two solutions, or seventeen? The reason
takes three lines.

\begin{proposition}[Zero, one, or infinity -- never in between]
\label{ch06:zoi}
If a linear system $A\vec x=\vec b$ has two distinct solutions, it has
infinitely many.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $\vec p$ and $\vec q$ are both solutions, $\vec p\neq\vec q$, so
$A\vec p=\vec b$ and $A\vec q=\vec b$. Consider the whole \emph{line}
through them: for any real number $t$, set
$\vec x_t=\vec p + t(\vec q-\vec p)$. Then, using that $A$ distributes over
sums and pulls out scalars (the two rules of linearity from Chapter~1),
\[
\begin{aligned}
  A\vec x_t &= A\vec p + t\,A(\vec q-\vec p)\\
            &= \vec b + t(A\vec q - A\vec p)\\
            &= \vec b + t(\vec b-\vec b) = \vec b.
\end{aligned}
\]
So \emph{every} point $\vec x_t$ on the line is a solution, and since
$\vec q-\vec p\neq\vec 0$ these are all different points. Two solutions
force a whole line of them, hence infinitely many.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Geometrically: the solution set of a linear system is never a scattering of
isolated dots. It is always ``flat'': a single point, a whole line, a
plane (or a higher-dimensional flat piece), or empty. That flatness is why
the number of solutions is $0$, $1$, or $\infty$ and nothing else.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: circles vs.\ lines.}
A straight line and a circle in the plane can cross at \emph{two} distinct
points. Why can't two equations in a linear system ever do that? Where in
the proof of Proposition~\ref{ch06:zoi} did linearity rule out two dots?
Linearity entered at $A\bigl(\vec p + t(\vec q - \vec p)\bigr) = A\vec p + t\,A(\vec q - \vec p)$.
Because the matrix acts linearly on combinations, every point on the line
through $\vec p$ and $\vec q$ is forced to map to $\vec b$. A circle equation
like $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ is \emph{nonlinear} (it squares the unknowns), so the
points between two solutions fall off the circle, as
Figure~\ref{ch06:circle} shows. Linear equations cannot curve back like that.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Singular versus nonsingular: the determinant in 2D}

We keep saying ``spread out'' and ``collapsed.'' Here is a number that measures it.
For a $2\times 2$ matrix
\[
  A=\colvec{a & b\\ c & d},
\]
the \emph{determinant} is $\det A = ad-bc$. As previewed in Chapters~3
and~5 (and treated in full in Chapter~21), this number is the \emph{signed
area} of the parallelogram whose sides are the two column vectors: its size
is the area, and its sign is $+$ or $-$ depending on whether the second
column lies counterclockwise or clockwise from the first. Here we use it as
a \emph{collapse detector}: it tells us whether the matrix keeps the plane
spread out or squashes it flat.

A nonsingular matrix can be undone. The \emph{inverse} of $A$, written
$A\inv$, is the matrix that reverses what $A$ does: if $A$ sends $\vec x$ to
$\vec b$, then $A\inv$ sends $\vec b$ back to $\vec x$. A matrix that has an
inverse is called \emph{invertible}. (Chapter~8 studies inverses in
detail.) Below, ``iff'' is short for ``if and only if.''

\begin{formalbox}
The $2\times 2$ matrix $A=\colvec{a&b\\c&d}$ is \textbf{nonsingular}
(invertible) iff $\det A = ad-bc \neq 0$, and \textbf{singular} iff
$\det A = 0$. When nonsingular, $A\vec x=\vec b$ has the unique solution
\[
  \vec x = A\inv \vec b,\qquad
  A\inv = \frac{1}{ad-bc}\colvec{d & -b\\ -c & a}.
\]
\end{formalbox}

Why is $ad-bc$ the right number? Look at the geometry. The two
columns of $A$ are $\vec a_1=\colvec{a\\c}$ and $\vec a_2=\colvec{b\\d}$.
These two arrows are the two sides of a parallelogram. The
\emph{area} of that parallelogram is exactly $\lvert ad-bc\rvert$
(Figure~\ref{ch06:detarea}). So:
\begin{itemize}
  \item If the columns point in different directions, the parallelogram has
  positive area, $ad-bc\neq0$: \emph{spread out}, nonsingular.
  \item If the columns are collinear, the parallelogram is squashed flat,
  area $0$, so $ad-bc=0$: \emph{collapsed}, singular.
\end{itemize}

Check our three examples against $ad-bc$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Flagship \eqref{ch06:flagship}: $\det\colvec{2&1\\3&2}=2\cdot2-1\cdot3=1\neq0$.
  Nonsingular: one solution.
  \item No-solution \eqref{ch06:nosol}: $\det\colvec{1&2\\1&2}=1\cdot2-2\cdot1=0$.
  Singular, and here $\vec b$ missed the line, so zero solutions.
  \item Infinite \eqref{ch06:infsol}: $\det\colvec{1&2\\2&4}=1\cdot4-2\cdot2=0$.
  Singular, and here $\vec b$ sat on the line, so infinitely many.
\end{itemize}
The determinant alone separates ``nonsingular'' (always one solution) from
``singular'' (either zero or infinitely many, depending on $\vec b$). To
decide between those last two you must look at $\vec b$; the determinant
cannot, because it knows only about $A$.

\subsection{Deriving the $2\times 2$ inverse formula}

Where does the formula
\[
  A\inv=\frac{1}{ad-bc}\colvec{d&-b\\-c&a}
\]
come from? Solve $\colvec{a&b\\c&d}\colvec{x\\y}=\colvec{e\\f}$ by
elimination (subtracting multiples of one equation from another to remove an
unknown), assuming $ad-bc\neq0$. Here $e,f$ are the two entries of the
target $\vec b$. The equations are
\[
  ax+by=e,\qquad cx+dy=f.
\]
Multiply the first by $d$, the second by $b$, and subtract to eliminate $y$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  d(ax+by) - b(cx+dy) &= de - bf\\
  (ad-bc)\,x &= de - bf\\
  x &= \frac{de-bf}{ad-bc}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Multiply the first by $c$, the second by $a$, and subtract to eliminate $x$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  c(ax+by) - a(cx+dy) &= ce - af\\
  (bc-ad)\,y &= ce - af\\
  y &= \frac{af-ce}{ad-bc}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Stacking these,
\[
  \colvec{x\\y}=\frac{1}{ad-bc}\colvec{d & -b\\ -c & a}\colvec{e\\f},
\]
which is $\vec x=A\inv\vec b$. The formula is two lines of elimination
with the division by $ad-bc$ done at the end. When $ad-bc=0$ that last
division is impossible, which is why singular matrices have no inverse.

\begin{trapbox}
``$\det A=0$ means no solution'' is \emph{wrong}. $\det A = 0$ means
there is no longer exactly one solution; the system then has \emph{either} no solution \emph{or} infinitely many. You
must inspect $\vec b$ (does it lie in the span of the columns?) to know
which. Nonsingular is a property of $A$ alone; the zero-versus-infinity split
needs $\vec b$.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Determinants appear well beyond linear algebra. In multivariable
\emph{calculus}, a change of variables $(u,v)\mapsto(x,y)$ looks linear
when you zoom in close enough, and the matrix of that local linear map is
the \emph{Jacobian matrix} $J$ (Chapter~5). Its determinant is exactly this
area factor: it tells you how the change of variables stretches or squashes
tiny areas, which is why
$dx\,dy = \lvert\det J\rvert\,du\,dv$ appears in every change-of-variables
integral. A coordinate change is ``nonsingular'' (locally invertible)
precisely where its Jacobian determinant is nonzero: the same
spread-out-versus-collapsed test as here, applied to tiny boxes.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Up to three dimensions: planes instead of lines}

Everything carries over to three unknowns. An equation like $a x + b y + c z = d$ in three unknowns is
no longer a line; it is a \emph{plane} in $\R^3$. A system of three such
equations asks for a point lying on all three planes at once.

\begin{intuitionbox}
In $\R^3$, each linear equation is a plane. The row picture of a
$3\times3$ system is \emph{three planes}, and a solution is a point common to
all three. Usually three planes meet at a single point, like the corner of
a room where two walls and the floor come together
(Figure~\ref{ch06:corner}).
\end{intuitionbox}

The column picture also carries over, and becomes more useful, because
three planes are hard to draw but three arrows are not. Write $a_{ij}$ for
the entry of $A$ in row $i$ and column $j$. A $3\times3$ system
\[
  A\vec x=\vec b,\qquad
  x\colvec{a_{11}\\a_{21}\\a_{31}} + y\colvec{a_{12}\\a_{22}\\a_{32}}
  + z\colvec{a_{13}\\a_{23}\\a_{33}} = \vec b,
\]
asks: can the target arrow $\vec b\in\R^3$ be built as a combination of the
three column arrows? If the three columns are \emph{spread out} (not lying
in any common plane through the origin), they span all of $\R^3$, and every $\vec b$ is
reachable in exactly one way. That is the nonsingular case.

\subsection{A worked $3\times3$ with a clean answer}

\begin{example}[Three planes, one corner]
\label{ch06:ex-3x3}
Solve
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    x + y + z &= 6,\\
    \phantom{x +{}} 2y + z &= 5,\\
    \phantom{x + y +{}} 2z &= 4.
  \end{aligned}
\]
This system is already \emph{triangular}: each equation has one fewer
unknown than the one above, so the coefficients form a triangle. We can
solve it from the bottom up, a method called \emph{back-substitution}. The
last equation gives $2z=4$, so
$z=2$. Plug into the middle: $2y+2=5$, so $y=\tfrac32$. Plug both into the
top: $x+\tfrac32+2=6$, so $x=\tfrac52$. The unique solution is
$(x,y,z)=\bigl(\tfrac52,\tfrac32,2\bigr)$.

The \emph{diagonal} of a square matrix is the line of entries running from
top left to bottom right; here it is $1,2,2$. For a triangular matrix the
determinant is the product of the diagonal entries (a fact proved in
Chapter~21), so $\det A = 1\cdot2\cdot2=4\neq0$. The system is nonsingular:
the three planes cross at the single corner
$\bigl(\tfrac52,\tfrac32,2\bigr)$. Elimination, the method of the next
chapter (also called \emph{Gaussian elimination}), works by turning any
system into a triangular one like this.
\end{example}

\subsection{How a $3\times3$ system can fail}

In $\R^3$ there are more ways to fail, but they still sort into ``no
solution'' and ``infinitely many,'' both cases of a \emph{singular} matrix.
Figure~\ref{ch06:spine} shows one of each.

\textbf{No solution.} Three planes can fail to share a common point in
several ways. Two of them can be parallel. Or the three can form a
triangular prism: each pair meets in a line, but the three lines run
parallel, like the three long edges of a tent, so no single point
lies on all three. Algebraically, elimination produces a contradiction such
as $0=1$.

\textbf{Infinitely many.} If the three planes share a whole \emph{line}
(think of three pages of a book hinged on the same spine), every point on
that line solves the system: a line's worth of solutions. If they all
coincide as the \emph{same} plane, you get a plane's worth. Elimination
reveals this as a \emph{free variable}: an unknown you may set to any
value, with the others then determined by it (like $t$ in
\eqref{ch06:infsol}).

\begin{focusbox}
The rule ``$0$, $1$, or $\infty$ solutions'' holds in every dimension. Below, a
\emph{flat} means a line, plane, or higher-dimensional analogue through the
origin. For an $n\times n$ matrix $A$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nonsingular} ($\det A\neq0$, columns span $\R^n$): exactly
  one solution for \emph{every} $\vec b$.
  \item \textbf{Singular} ($\det A=0$, columns trapped in a smaller flat):
  \emph{either} no solution (when $\vec b$ escapes that flat) \emph{or}
  infinitely many (when $\vec b$ lies inside it).
\end{itemize}
This is the $2\times2$ story again, with ``line'' replaced by ``plane'' and
the parallelogram replaced by a \emph{parallelepiped} (a slanted box whose
three edges from one corner are the three columns), whose volume is
$\lvert\det A\rvert$.
\end{focusbox}

\section{The homogeneous shadow: $A\vec x=\vec 0$}

There is a special right-hand side that controls all the others: the zero
vector. The system $A\vec x=\vec 0$ is called \emph{homogeneous} (every
equation has $0$ on the right-hand side). It always has the obvious
solution $\vec x=\vec 0$, called the \emph{trivial} solution, since
$A\vec 0=\vec 0$. The question is whether it has \emph{nontrivial}
solutions: nonzero inputs that the matrix still sends to the origin.

\begin{theorem}[Singular $\Leftrightarrow$ nonzero solutions to $A\vec x=\vec 0$]
\label{ch06:homog}
A square matrix $A$ is singular if and only if $A\vec x=\vec 0$ has a
solution $\vec x\neq\vec 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
If $A$ is nonsingular it has an inverse, so $\vec x=A\inv\vec 0=\vec 0$ is
the \emph{only} solution; no nontrivial one exists. Conversely, if the
columns of $A$ are collapsed (singular), some nontrivial combination of them
equals $\vec 0$; the weights of that combination form an $\vec x\neq\vec0$
with $A\vec x=\vec0$. For our flagship $A=\colvec{2&1\\3&2}$ (nonsingular),
only $\vec0$ works. For the collapsed $\colvec{1&2\\2&4}$, the combination
$2\colvec{1\\2}-1\colvec{2\\4}=\vec0$ exposes $\vec x=\colvec{2\\-1}\neq\vec0$.
\end{proof}

The set of all input vectors that $A$ sends to the zero vector is called
the \emph{null space} of $A$ (another name is the \emph{kernel}). We study
it properly in Chapter~12; for now, picture it as the directions that get
flattened to a point. When $A$ is nonsingular, only $\vec 0$ maps to
$\vec 0$, so the null space is just $\{\vec 0\}$, the \emph{trivial} null
space. When $A$ is singular, at least one direction is flattened: a whole
line, plane, or higher-dimensional flat of nonzero vectors is sent to the
origin. This links back to Proposition~\ref{ch06:zoi}. If
$\vec x_{\text{particular}}$ is one solution of $A\vec x=\vec b$ and $\vec x$
is any other, then
$A(\vec x-\vec x_{\text{particular}})=\vec b-\vec b=\vec 0$, so the two
differ by a vector in the null space:

\begin{aestheticbox}
Every solution of $A\vec x=\vec b$ has the shape
\[
  \vec x = \underbrace{\vec x_{\text{particular}}}_{\text{one solution}}
         + \underbrace{\vec x_{\text{null}}}_{\text{anything killed by }A}.
\]
The particular part gets you to $\vec b$; the null-space part is the freedom
to wander without changing where you land. If the null space is just
$\{\vec0\}$ (nonsingular), there is no freedom: one solution. If the null
space is a line or a plane (singular), that same flat shape is copied onto
the solution set, giving infinitely many. \emph{The geometry of the
solutions is the geometry of the null space, shifted.} Chapter~13 builds
the complete solution of $A\vec x=\vec b$ on this decomposition.
\end{aestheticbox}

a line: the full solution set is that same line, picked up and slid over so
it passes through one particular solution $\vec x_{\text{particular}}$.

pass through one particular solution $\vec x_{\text{particular}}$. A trivial null space
(just $\{\vec 0\}$) shifts to a single solution.}
\label{ch06:shift}
\end{figure}

\begin{connectionbox}
This ``particular plus homogeneous'' shape is not special to matrices; it
holds for \emph{any} linear map. In a first course on
\emph{differential equations} (equations whose unknown is a function
$y(t)$, with $y''$ its second derivative) you meet the same theorem: the
general solution of $y'' + y = \cos t$ is one particular solution (say
$y_p=\tfrac{t}{2}\sin t$) plus the general solution of the homogeneous
equation $y''+y=0$ (here $c_1\cos t + c_2\sin t$ for any constants
$c_1,c_2$). The homogeneous solutions
play the role of the null space, and the particular solution plays the role
of $\vec x_{\text{particular}}$. The map $L$ that sends a function $y$ to
$y''+y$ is linear: $L(\alpha f+\beta g)=\alpha L f+\beta Lg$ for any
functions $f,g$ and numbers $\alpha,\beta$. So the argument is word for word
the one we just gave for $A\vec x=\vec b$: the solutions of any linear
problem are ``a null space, shifted.''
\end{connectionbox}

\section{A modern application: when ``solve $A\vec x=\vec b$'' is the whole job}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Networks, circuits, graphics, and machine learning.}
Solving linear systems is the main computational step in
many areas of science and engineering.

\emph{Circuits and networks.} Kirchhoff's laws for an electrical network
(``current in equals current out at every junction; voltage drops around
every closed loop add to zero'') form a linear system $A\vec x=\vec b$ whose
unknowns are the currents. The \emph{same} kind of matrix, which records
which wires meet at which junctions (an \emph{incidence matrix}), governs
water in pipes, traffic on roads, and forces in a bridge truss. Engineers
asking ``will this bridge stand?'' solve a system whose matrix (the
\emph{stiffness matrix}) says how hard each joint pushes back when moved;
they need it to be nonsingular, and $A\inv\vec b$ gives how far each joint
bends under the load $\vec b$.

\emph{Computer graphics.} To draw a 3D scene, a renderer traces rays of
light and solves small linear systems to find where each ray passes through
each triangle of the scene's surfaces. The ``does the ray hit?'' test is a
$3\times3$ singular-versus-nonsingular check: a singular system means the
ray runs parallel to the triangle's plane.

\emph{Machine learning.} \emph{Linear regression} fits a linear model to
data: it predicts an output as a weighted sum of input measurements, for
example a house price from its size and age. It underlies much of applied
machine learning (ML) and is set up as $A\vec x=\vec b$, where each row of
$A$ holds one data example, $\vec b$ holds the observed outputs, and $\vec x$
is the vector of weights. Usually
there are far more equations than unknowns and \emph{no exact solution
exists} (the $\vec b$ of real data does not lie in the column space). The
fix, \emph{least squares}, finds the $\vec x$ that gets closest: it asks
``what is the nearest reachable point to $\vec b$ in the span of the
columns?'' That question is the column picture of this chapter, asked when
the target is out of reach. Large nonlinear models are also trained by
repeatedly solving, or approximately solving, linear systems to choose each
update step. The split between spread-out and collapsed columns reappears
as the question ``is this problem \emph{well-conditioned} (small errors in
the data cause only small errors in $\vec x$), or are the columns nearly
collapsed onto a smaller flat?'' When the input columns of the data are
nearly collinear, statisticians call it \emph{multicollinearity}; the
weights then swing wildly with tiny changes in the data, a common way for a
real-world model to fail.
\end{applicationbox}

Figure~\ref{ch06:leastsq} shows the least-squares idea. The reachable
targets (all combinations of the columns) fill a plane, the column space.
When the data vector $\vec b$ lies \emph{off} that plane, no exact solution
exists, so we drop a perpendicular from $\vec b$ to the plane and solve for
the nearest reachable point $\hat{\vec b}$ (read ``$b$-hat'') instead. The
leftover gap $\vec b-\hat{\vec b}$ is called the \emph{residual}. Dropping
a perpendicular onto the column space is how linear regression works; we
make it precise in Chapters~18 and~19 (projections and least squares).

\section{Building intuition that lasts}

\begin{walkbox}
Take a walk and carry exactly this image. You are handed two arrows on the
ground, $\vec a_1$ and $\vec a_2$, and a flag planted at point $\vec b$. Your
job: reach the flag by walking some amount along $\vec a_1$ and some amount
along $\vec a_2$ (backwards is allowed). Ask yourself:
\begin{itemize}
  \item If the two arrows point in genuinely different directions, can you
  always reach \emph{any} flag? In how many ways?
  \item If the two arrows point the \emph{same} way, which flags can you
  reach, and which are forever out of reach?
\end{itemize}
When you can answer these without paper, you understand the column
picture, and with it the answer to the two basic questions about any linear
system: does a solution exist, and is it unique?
\end{walkbox}

\begin{remark}[Two pictures, one habit]
Whenever you meet $A\vec x=\vec b$, use both pictures.
To decide whether a solution \emph{exists}, look at the columns: is $\vec b$ in their
span? To \emph{compute} the solution, work with the rows using elimination.
To decide whether the solution is \emph{unique}, check whether the columns are
linearly independent (for a square matrix, $\det A \neq 0$).
\end{remark}

\section{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A linear system $A\vec x=\vec b$ has \textbf{two geometric readings}.
  The \emph{row picture} sees each equation as a line (in $\R^2$) or plane
  (in $\R^3$); a solution is a common intersection point. The \emph{column
  picture} sees $A\vec x$ as a linear combination of the columns; a solution
  is a recipe of weights reaching $\vec b$.
  \item Compute $A\vec x$ two ways: \emph{rows dotted with $\vec x$}, or
  \emph{a combination of the columns}. Both are the same product.
  \item Exactly three outcomes are possible: \textbf{one}, \textbf{none}, or
  \textbf{infinitely many} solutions. Two distinct solutions force a whole
  line of them (Proposition~\ref{ch06:zoi}).
  \item \textbf{Nonsingular} ($\det A\neq0$): columns spread out / span the
  space; one solution for every $\vec b$; $\vec x=A\inv\vec b$.
  \textbf{Singular} ($\det A=0$): columns collapsed onto a smaller flat;
  then \emph{no} solution if $\vec b$ escapes that flat, \emph{infinitely
  many} if $\vec b$ lies inside it.
  \item The determinant $ad-bc$ (in $2\times2$) is the signed area of the
  column parallelogram: zero area means the columns have collapsed, so the
  matrix is singular.
  \item The homogeneous system $A\vec x=\vec0$ measures the freedom: its
  solutions form the null space, and every solution of $A\vec x=\vec b$ is one
  particular solution plus a null-space vector. A nonzero null space turns
  one solution into infinitely many.
\end{itemize}

\begin{marginnotebox}
\textbf{Cost recap.} The $2\times2$ inverse formula costs a handful of
multiplications. Formulas of the same kind, built from determinants, exist
in every dimension, but their cost explodes: computing an $n\times n$
determinant by the direct formula takes on the order of $n!$ operations
(``$n$ factorial'', $n! = 1\cdot2\cdot3\cdots n$), which is hopeless past
$n\approx 10$. The practical route is \emph{elimination}, which solves
$A\vec x=\vec b$ in about $\tfrac13 n^3$ operations; it is the main
algorithm of the next chapter. Figure~\ref{ch06:cost} compares the two
counts. So carry two things forward: the geometric picture (rows are
lines or planes, columns are arrows, singular means collapsed) and the fact
that the method of solution matters a great deal as $n$ grows.
\end{marginnotebox}

\begin{historybox}
Solving linear systems systematically is one of the oldest mathematical
practices on record. Long before European algebraists wrote with abstract
symbols, Chinese mathematicians of the Han Dynasty recorded the method in
the \emph{Jiuzhang Suanshu} (\emph{The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical
Art}), a collection traditionally associated with the officials Zhang Cang
and Geng Shouchang and compiled over the last centuries BCE. In
Chapter~8, titled \emph{Fangcheng} (``The Rectangular Array''), the authors
solved a system of three linear equations in three agricultural unknowns
(bundles of good, medium, and poor grain) by laying out the coefficients on a
counting board using bamboo rods.

The Han mathematicians arranged equations into columns on the board and used
red bamboo rods for positive quantities (surpluses) and black rods for negative
quantities (deficits). This is among the earliest recorded uses of negative
numbers. Han scholars worked with negative numbers naturally, as debts and
deficits, while European mathematicians resisted them for well over a
thousand years more. As late as the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries,
Girolamo Cardano called negative solutions ``fictitious'' and Ren\'e Descartes
labeled them ``false roots'' in his algebra. By systematically subtracting
multiples of one column from another, the Han mathematicians reduced their
counting board to triangular form and read off the answers by back-substitution.

In Europe, linear equations were long solved by cancelling variables by
hand on paper. In the eighteenth century, Colin Maclaurin and Gabriel Cramer
published determinant formulas (\emph{Cramer's rule}, which writes each
unknown as a ratio of two determinants, just as in
Example~\ref{ch06:ex-col}). The formula is neat, but expensive: the direct
formula for an $n\times n$ determinant is a sum of $n!$ terms, each a
product of $n$ entries. For a $30\times 30$ system,
$30! \approx 2.7 \times 10^{32}$ terms would take millions of years even
on a supercomputer doing $10^{18}$ operations per second. When Carl
Friedrich Gauss computed the orbit of the newly discovered asteroid Ceres
in 1801 from a handful of noisy telescope sightings, he used systematic
elimination instead, which costs roughly $\tfrac{1}{3}n^3$ operations, and
his methods became a foundation of computational astronomy.

When geometry entered the subject, textbooks almost always taught the
\emph{row picture}: lines crossing at a point, or planes meeting at a corner.
But the row picture breaks down in higher dimensions: we can picture
three planes in 3D, but nobody can visualize four \emph{hyperplanes} (flat
three-dimensional slices of four-dimensional space) crossing in $\R^4$, let
alone fifty hyperplanes in $\R^{50}$. The modern approach, championed by
Gilbert Strang, puts the \textbf{column picture} first:
\[
  x_1\vec{a}_1 + x_2\vec{a}_2 + \cdots + x_n\vec{a}_n = \vec{b}.
\]
Here $x_1,\dots,x_n$ are the unknowns and $\vec a_1,\dots,\vec a_n$ the
columns. In the column picture, the question does not change as the
dimension grows. Whether in $\R^2$ or $\R^{1000}$, you ask: can you scale
the given arrows $\vec{a}_i$ and link them tip to tail to reach the target
arrow $\vec{b}$? A system is solvable exactly when $\vec b$ is in the span
of the columns.

What remains open? Elimination solves an $n\times n$ system in $O(n^3)$
operations (in the big-O notation of Chapter~4: at most a fixed constant times $n^3$).
As Chapter~5 mentioned, Volker Strassen showed in 1969 that this is not the
limit: his recursive method multiplies and inverts matrices in
$O(n^{2.807})$ operations. Later work (by Don Coppersmith, Shmuel Winograd,
Virginia Vassilevska Williams, Josh Alman, and others) has pushed the best
known exponent, called $\omega$ (the smallest power with cost
$O(n^{\omega})$), below $2.372$. Whether $\omega = 2$ is still open: that
would mean solving an $n\times n$ system could take not much longer than
reading its $n^2$ entries. Chapter~7 starts elimination with the two-line
case of Figure~\ref{ch07:two-lines}.
\end{historybox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental warm-ups]
\label{ch06:ex-warmup}
Answer each question in your head before touching paper:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item A system has $4$ equations and $2$ unknowns. In the column picture, can the two column arrows ever span all of four-dimensional space $\R^4$? What does this tell you about whether a typical target $\vec b\in\R^4$, picked with no special relation to the columns, has an exact solution?
  \item If a $2\times 2$ matrix $A$ has $\det A = 0$, does $A\vec x = \vec b$ necessarily have no solution? Explain in one sentence.
  \item If $A\vec x = \vec 0$ has \emph{only} the trivial solution $\vec x = \vec 0$, can $A\vec x = \vec b$ ever have two distinct solutions? Why or why not?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Both pictures, by hand]
\label{ch06:ex-both}
For the system $\;3x+y=9,\ \ x+2y=8$:
(a) draw the row picture (two lines) and read off the crossing point;
(b) write it as $x\colvec{3\\1}+y\colvec{1\\2}=\colvec{9\\8}$ and find the
weights; (c) confirm both answers agree.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The determinant decides]
\label{ch06:ex-det}
Compute $\det A$ for each matrix and state, \emph{without solving}, whether
$A\vec x=\vec b$ is guaranteed to have a unique solution for every $\vec b$:
\[
  \colvec{4 & 2\\ 1 & 3},\qquad
  \colvec{2 & 6\\ 1 & 3},\qquad
  \colvec{0 & 5\\ -2 & 0}.
\]
For any singular one, find a $\vec b$ giving no solution and a $\vec b$
giving infinitely many.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Make it fail two ways]
\label{ch06:ex-fail}
Start from $\;x+3y=4$. Add a \emph{second} equation so that the system has
(a) no solution; (b) infinitely many solutions; (c) exactly one solution.
For each, name the geometric relationship between the two lines.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Column reach]
\label{ch06:ex-reach}
The columns of $A$ are $\vec a_1=\colvec{1\\2}$ and $\vec a_2=\colvec{2\\4}$.
(a) Describe, as a set, all vectors $\vec b$ for which $A\vec x=\vec b$ has a
solution. (b) For $\vec b=\colvec{3\\6}$, give \emph{two} different solutions
$\vec x$, and then describe \emph{all} of them in the form
$\vec x_{\text{particular}}+t\,\vec x_{\text{null}}$ (where $A\vec x_{\text{null}}=\vec 0$).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Back-substitution in $\R^3$]
\label{ch06:ex-back}
Solve by back-substitution (start from the last equation),
\[
  2x + y - z = 3,\qquad 3y + 2z = 4,\qquad 4z = 8,
\]
and verify your point satisfies all three equations. What is the determinant
of the coefficient matrix, and why does that guarantee uniqueness?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Three planes, no corner]
\label{ch06:ex-prism}
Consider $\;x+y+z=1,\ \ x+y+z=2,\ \ x-y=0$. Show by inspection that the
system has no solution, and explain in words what the three planes are doing
(which two are parallel?).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Derive your own inverse]
\label{ch06:ex-inverse}
Without quoting the formula, solve $\;ax+by=e,\ cx+dy=f$ by elimination to
re-derive $x=\frac{de-bf}{ad-bc}$ and $y=\frac{af-ce}{ad-bc}$. At which step
does the assumption $ad-bc\neq0$ become essential, and what goes wrong if it
fails?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Zero, one, or infinity -- a proof]
\label{ch06:ex-proof}
Suppose $A\vec p=\vec b$ and $A\vec q=\vec b$ with $\vec p\neq\vec q$. Prove
that $\vec p + t(\vec q-\vec p)$ solves $A\vec x=\vec b$ for \emph{every} real
$t$, citing the two linearity rules you use. Conclude that a linear system
cannot have exactly two solutions.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The homogeneous shadow]
\label{ch06:ex-homog}
For $A=\colvec{1 & 2 & 3\\ 2 & 4 & 6\\ 1 & 1 & 1}$:
(a) Row $2$ is exactly twice row $1$, meaning the second equation
provides no new constraint beyond the first. Use this geometric redundancy to
explain why the three planes cannot intersect at a single unique corner, and
conclude that $A$ must be singular. (b) Now find a nonzero $\vec x$ with
$A\vec x=\vec0$. (Hint: a nonzero vector with $A\vec x=\vec 0$ records a
combination of the \emph{columns} that cancels out to zero; look for weights
$x_1,x_2,x_3$, not all zero, with
$x_1\colvec{1\\2\\1}+x_2\colvec{2\\4\\1}+x_3\colvec{3\\6\\1}=\vec0$. You can
check that $\vec x=\colvec{1\\-2\\1}$ works.) (c) What does the existence of
this $\vec x$ tell you about the number of solutions of $A\vec x=\vec b$
\emph{when a solution exists at all}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Challenge: a parametric crossing]
\label{ch06:ex-param}
Consider $\;x+y=1,\ \ x+ky=2$, where $k$ is a real parameter.
(a) For which value(s) of $k$ is the coefficient matrix singular?
(b) For that $k$, does the system have no solution or infinitely many?
(c) For all other $k$, solve for $(x,y)$ in terms of $k$ and describe what
happens to the solution geometrically as $k\to1$. (Watch the crossing point
fly off to infinity as the lines become parallel.)
\end{exercise}
