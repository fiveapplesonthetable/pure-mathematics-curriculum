\chapter{Complex Vectors, Hermitian Matrices, and the Fourier Matrix}

In Chapter~31 we diagonalized linear transformations by changing coordinates to a
basis of eigenvectors. This chapter asks what to do when a real matrix has no real
eigenvalues at all, so that no real direction is left on its own line.

So far our vectors lived in $\R^n$, our matrices had real entries, and the dot
product $\vec u\cdot\vec v=\sum u_iv_i$ answered every question about length and
angle. Most of this book stays in that setting.

In Chapters~23 and~24, however, we kept meeting real matrices whose eigenvalues
are not real. A rotation of the plane has no real eigenvectors: turn every arrow
by ninety degrees and no arrow stays on its own line. Yet its characteristic
polynomial still has roots, $\pm i$, once complex numbers are allowed. To use
those roots we must let vectors have \emph{complex} entries.

Swapping $\R$ for $\C$ forces one change to the dot product: the entries of one of
the two vectors must be conjugated (their imaginary part negated). That change leads to two new families of
matrices: the \textbf{Hermitian} matrices, the complex version of symmetric
matrices, and the \textbf{unitary} matrices, the complex version of orthogonal
matrices. Both are used throughout quantum mechanics and signal processing.

The chapter ends with the most widely used unitary matrix, the \textbf{Fourier
matrix}. It turns a list of signal values into a list of frequencies, and the fast
algorithm for applying it, the fast Fourier transform (FFT), is one of the most
frequently run numerical algorithms in the world.



\chapterline

\section{Why real numbers are not enough}

Start with the rotation. The matrix
\[
  \mat R = \colvec{0 & -1 \\ 1 & \phantom{-}0}
\]
spins every vector in the plane by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise. Geometrically,
\emph{no} nonzero real vector $\vec x$ satisfies $\mat R\vec x =
\lambda\vec x$: an eigenvector would have to point along the same line after
rotation, and a quarter turn sends every line to a different line. So over $\R$,
this matrix has no eigenvectors.

The algebra still finds eigenvalues. The characteristic polynomial is
\begin{align*}
  \det(\mat R - \lambda\mat I) &= \det\colvec{-\lambda & -1 \\ 1 & -\lambda} \\
  &= \lambda^2 + 1,
\end{align*}
and $\lambda^2+1=0$ has solutions $\lambda = \pm i$, where $i=\sqrt{-1}$. The
eigenvalues exist, but they are complex. Once we allow complex entries in
the eigenvector too, eigenvectors appear:
\begin{align*}
  \mat R \colvec{1 \\ -i} &= \colvec{0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0}\colvec{1\\-i} \\
  &= \colvec{i \\ 1} \\
  &= i\colvec{1 \\ -i},
\end{align*}
so $\colvec{1\\-i}$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $i$. ``No fixed direction''
in the picture and ``complex eigenvalues'' in the algebra describe the same fact.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A real matrix can rotate the plane spanned by two coordinates, and a rotation has
no real eigenvector. Complex numbers describe rotation algebraically:
multiplication by $e^{i\theta}$ \emph{is} rotation by the angle $\theta$. So when a
real matrix rotates, its eigenvalues come out as complex numbers $re^{i\theta}$,
where $\theta$ records the angle of the turn and $r$ any stretching that comes
with it.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{A two-minute refresher on complex numbers}

A complex
number is $z = a + bi$ with $a,b\in\R$ and $i^2=-1$. We call $a=\operatorname{Re}z$
the real part and $b=\operatorname{Im}z$ the imaginary part. Two operations matter
here.

The \textbf{conjugate} flips the sign of the imaginary part:
\[
  \bar z = a - bi.
\]
The \textbf{modulus} (or absolute value) is the length of $z$ viewed as the point
$(a,b)$ in the plane:
\[
  \abs{z} = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}.
\]
They are linked by one identity:
\begin{align*}
  z\bar z &= (a+bi)(a-bi) \\
  &= a^2 - (bi)^2 \\
  &= a^2 + b^2 \\
  &= \abs{z}^2.
\end{align*}
Multiplying a number by its own conjugate removes the imaginary part and
leaves a real, nonnegative number: the squared length. The rest of the chapter
uses this fact again and again.

\centering

  \draw[->,very thick,vecred] (0,0) -- (2.4,-1.4) node[below right]{$\bar z=a-bi$};
  \draw[dashed,gray!60] (2.4,1.4) -- (2.4,-1.4);

\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{A complex number $z$ and its conjugate $\bar z$ are mirror images across
the real axis. Both have the same modulus $\abs z$; conjugation flips the angle
from $+\theta$ to $-\theta$. The product $z\bar z=\abs z^2$ is real.}
\label{ch32:conjugate}
\end{figure}

As Figure~\ref{ch32:conjugate} shows, $z$ and $\bar z$ are mirror images
across the real axis, sharing a modulus but carrying opposite angles.
Polar form (Chapter~23) describes the same picture with angles. Writing
$z = r e^{i\theta}$ with $r=\abs z$ and $\theta$ the angle from the positive real
axis, Euler's formula $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ says $z$ is the point
at distance $r$ and angle $\theta$. Then
$\bar z = r e^{-i\theta}$ (same radius, opposite angle), and multiplication adds
angles: $(r_1e^{i\theta_1})(r_2e^{i\theta_2}) = r_1r_2\,e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}$.
Multiplying by a number of modulus $1$ -- a number $e^{i\theta}$ on the unit
circle -- is a pure rotation. Keep that picture in mind for the Fourier section.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If $z = 3 + 4i$, compute its square $z^2 = (3+4i)^2 = 9 + 24i - 16 = -7 + 24i$.
The result $z^2$ is not real, and its real part is negative, so it cannot measure size.
Now compute $z\bar z = (3+4i)(3-4i) = 9 + 16 = 25$. It is real, positive, and equals $\abs{z}^2$.
Squaring doubles the angle ($2\theta$); multiplying by the conjugate cancels the angle ($\theta - \theta = 0$).
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Complex vectors and the right inner product}

Now stack complex numbers into a vector. A vector in $\C^n$ is a list of $n$
complex numbers,
\[
  \vec z = \colvec{z_1 \\ z_2 \\ \vdots \\ z_n}, \qquad z_k\in\C.
\]
Addition and scalar multiplication work exactly as before, now with complex
scalars. The trouble starts when we ask for length.

\subsection{The naive dot product fails}

In $\R^n$ we defined $\norm{\vec u}^2 = \vec u\cdot\vec u = \sum u_i^2$, and all
of our geometry rested on one fact: $\vec u\cdot\vec u \ge 0$, with zero only
for the zero vector. Copy the formula into $\C^n$ unchanged, as $\sum z_k^2$, and
this fact fails. Take the nonzero vector $\vec z = \colvec{1 \\ i}$. Then
\[
  \sum z_k^2 = 1^2 + i^2 = 1 + (-1) = 0,
\]
so a nonzero vector would have ``length squared'' zero. Worse, the
vector $\colvec{1\\1+i}$ gives $1^2 + (1+i)^2 = 1 + 2i$, which is not real, so
there is no length to take the square root of. The direct copy does not work.

\begin{trapbox}
The number-one mistake when first meeting $\C^n$ is to write $\norm{\vec z}^2 =
\sum z_k^2$. Squaring a complex number does \emph{not} produce a length; $i^2=-1$,
not $+1$. Length must come from $\abs{z_k}^2 = z_k\bar z_k$, never from $z_k^2$.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{The fix: conjugate one side}

The refresher above already contains the cure. For a single number, the real,
nonnegative ``length squared'' is $z\bar z = \abs z^2$, not $z^2$. So for a vector
we should sum $z_k\bar z_k$:
\begin{align*}
  \norm{\vec z}^2 &= \sum_{k=1}^n z_k\bar z_k \\
  &= \sum_{k=1}^n \abs{z_k}^2 \\
  &\ge 0,
\end{align*}
and this is zero exactly when every $\abs{z_k}=0$, i.e. when $\vec z = \vec 0$.
Positivity is restored. Check our broken example: $\vec z=\colvec{1\\i}$ now has
$\norm{\vec z}^2 = \abs 1^2 + \abs i^2 = 1 + 1 = 2$, a length of
$\sqrt 2$.

Generalizing from $\vec z$ with itself to two different vectors gives the
\textbf{complex (or Hermitian) inner product}. We conjugate the entries of the
first vector and pair them with the second:
\[
  \boxed{\;\inner{\vec u}{\vec v} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^n \bar u_k\, v_k
  \;=\; \bar u_1 v_1 + \bar u_2 v_2 + \cdots + \bar u_n v_n. \;}
\]
(Convention warning: mathematicians often conjugate the second slot instead. We pick
the first. Either way, exactly one side is
conjugated.) With this definition $\inner{\vec z}{\vec z} = \sum\bar z_kz_k =
\sum\abs{z_k}^2 = \norm{\vec z}^2$ is real and nonnegative, as a squared length
must be.

\begin{definition}\label{ch32:inner}
For $\vec u,\vec v\in\C^n$, the \textbf{Hermitian inner product} is
$\inner{\vec u}{\vec v} = \sum_{k=1}^n \bar u_k v_k$, and the \textbf{norm} is
$\norm{\vec z} = \sqrt{\inner{\vec z}{\vec z}} = \sqrt{\sum_k\abs{z_k}^2}$.
Two vectors are \textbf{orthogonal} when $\inner{\vec u}{\vec v}=0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}\label{ch32:innerex}
Let $\vec u = \colvec{1+i \\ 2}$ and $\vec v = \colvec{3 \\ 1-i}$. Conjugate the
\emph{first} vector's entries: $\bar u_1 = 1-i$, $\bar u_2 = 2$. Then
\begin{align*}
  \inner{\vec u}{\vec v} &= (1-i)(3) + (2)(1-i) \\
  &= (3-3i) + (2-2i) \\
  &= 5 - 5i.
\end{align*}
And the squared length of $\vec u$ is
\begin{align*}
  \norm{\vec u}^2 &= \inner{\vec u}{\vec u} \\
  &= (1-i)(1+i) + (2)(2) \\
  &= (1+1) + 4 \\
  &= 6,
\end{align*}
so $\norm{\vec u} = \sqrt 6$. The inner product of $\vec u$ with itself came out
real and positive even though $\vec u$ has complex entries.
\end{example}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} What is the length of $\vec w = \colvec{i \\ 0}$?
Using our definition, $\norm{\vec w}^2 = \inner{\vec w}{\vec w} = \bar i \cdot i = (-i)(i) = 1$, giving $\norm{\vec w} = 1$.
What would the naive sum of squares produce? It would give $i^2 + 0 = -1$, a negative squared length for a nonzero vector.
Conjugating the first vector's entries is what keeps lengths positive.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Conjugate symmetry: the price of the fix}

We lose one property of the real dot product.
In $\R^n$ the dot product was symmetric: $\vec u\cdot\vec v = \vec v\cdot\vec u$.
In $\C^n$ symmetry becomes \textbf{conjugate symmetry}:
\[
  \inner{\vec v}{\vec u} \;=\; \overline{\inner{\vec u}{\vec v}}.
\]
Swapping the two vectors conjugates the answer. You can read it straight off the
definition: $\inner{\vec v}{\vec u} = \sum\bar v_k u_k$, and conjugating
$\inner{\vec u}{\vec v}=\sum\bar u_kv_k$ gives $\sum u_k\bar v_k =
\sum\bar v_k u_k$, the same thing. In Example~\ref{ch32:innerex} we found
$\inner{\vec u}{\vec v} = 5-5i$; you can check directly that
$\inner{\vec v}{\vec u} = (3)(1+i)+(1+i)(2) = 5+5i$, the conjugate.

Conjugate symmetry fits with lengths being real. Put $\vec u=\vec v=\vec z$ in it:
it says $\inner{\vec z}{\vec z} = \overline{\inner{\vec z}{\vec z}}$, and a number
equal to its own conjugate is a real number.

\begin{formalbox}
The Hermitian inner product on $\C^n$ satisfies, for all $\vec u,\vec v,\vec w\in
\C^n$ and $c\in\C$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Conjugate symmetry:} $\inner{\vec v}{\vec u} =
        \overline{\inner{\vec u}{\vec v}}$.
  \item \textbf{Linearity in the second slot:} $\inner{\vec u}{\vec v + \vec w} =
        \inner{\vec u}{\vec v} + \inner{\vec u}{\vec w}$ and
        $\inner{\vec u}{c\,\vec v} = c\,\inner{\vec u}{\vec v}$.
  \item \textbf{Conjugate-linearity in the first slot:}
        $\inner{c\,\vec u}{\vec v} = \bar c\,\inner{\vec u}{\vec v}$.
  \item \textbf{Positivity:} $\inner{\vec z}{\vec z} = \sum\abs{z_k}^2 \ge 0$, with
        equality only when $\vec z = \vec 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{formalbox}

Property 3 is the one that catches people out: a scalar pulled out of the
\emph{first} slot comes out conjugated, because that slot is the conjugated one.
If you scale the first vector by $i$, the inner product scales by $\bar i = -i$.
Everything else behaves like the real dot product.

\begin{walkbox}
Take a walk and convince yourself of this without any formula: ``length must be a
real, nonnegative number, and it can only be zero for the zero vector.'' Now ask:
what is the \emph{simplest} change to $\sum u_kv_k$ that guarantees
$\inner{\vec z}{\vec z}\ge 0$ for every complex vector? You will rediscover the
conjugate.
\end{walkbox}

\section{The conjugate transpose}

We packaged the real dot product as a matrix product: $\vec u\cdot\vec v =
\vec u\T\vec v$, a row times a column. The complex inner product wants the same
tidy packaging, but the row has to carry that conjugate. So we bundle ``transpose
and conjugate'' into a single operation.

\begin{definition}\label{ch32:adjoint}
The \textbf{conjugate transpose} (or \textbf{Hermitian adjoint}) of a matrix
$\mat A\in\C^{m\times n}$ is $\mat A^{*}\in\C^{n\times m}$, obtained by transposing
$\mat A$ and conjugating every entry: $(\mat A^{*})_{jk} = \overline{(\mat A)_{kj}}$.
For a vector, $\vec u^{*}$ is the conjugated row.
\end{definition}

Many books write $\mat A^{H}$ (``$H$'' for Hermite) or $\mat A^{\dagger}$ (``dagger,''
the physics standard) for the same thing. We use the star $\mat A^{*}$. With it,
the inner product becomes a matrix product:
\begin{align*}
  \inner{\vec u}{\vec v} &= \vec u^{*}\vec v \\
  &= \begin{bmatrix}\bar u_1 & \cdots & \bar u_n\end{bmatrix}
        \colvec{v_1\\\vdots\\v_n}.
\end{align*}
Everywhere a real formula had a transpose $\mat A\T$, the complex version
has a conjugate transpose $\mat A^{*}$. The reversal rule for transposes
(Chapter~10) survives unchanged, $(\mat A\mat B)^{*} = \mat B^{*}\mat A^{*}$, and
$(\mat A^{*})^{*} = \mat A$.

\begin{example}\label{ch32:starex}
\[
  \mat A = \colvec{1+i & 2 \\ 0 & 3-i}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \mat A^{*} = \colvec{1-i & 0 \\ 2 & 3+i}.
\]
Transpose (swap across the diagonal), then conjugate every entry. The $2$ and $0$
are real, so they only move; the $1+i$ and $3-i$ sit on the diagonal so they do not move, they only flip the sign
of their imaginary part.
\end{example}

\begin{focusbox}
Translate ``real $\to$ complex'' by one rule: \textbf{every transpose becomes a
conjugate transpose.} Dot product $\vec u\T\vec v\to\vec u^{*}\vec v$. Symmetric
$\mat A\T=\mat A\to$ Hermitian $\mat A^{*}=\mat A$. Orthogonal
$\mat Q\T\mat Q=\mat I\to$ unitary $\mat U^{*}\mat U=\mat I$. The geometry is
identical; only the star changed.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Hermitian matrices: the complex symmetric}

A real symmetric matrix satisfies $\mat A\T=\mat A$, and the Spectral Theorem of
Chapter~26 gives it real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.
The complex matrices with the same behavior are the ones equal to their own
conjugate transpose.

\begin{definition}\label{ch32:hermdef}
A square matrix $\mat A\in\C^{n\times n}$ is \textbf{Hermitian} if
$\mat A^{*} = \mat A$, that is, $a_{jk} = \overline{a_{kj}}$ for every entry.
\end{definition}

Two consequences are visible immediately from $a_{jk}=\overline{a_{kj}}$. On the
diagonal, $j=k$ forces $a_{kk}=\overline{a_{kk}}$, so \emph{the diagonal entries
are real}. Off the diagonal, entries come in conjugate-mirror pairs across the
diagonal. A real symmetric matrix is the special case where the off-diagonal pairs
are equal real numbers.

\begin{example}\label{ch32:hermex}
\[
  \mat A = \colvec{2 & 3-i \\ 3+i & 5}
\]
is Hermitian: the diagonal $2,5$ are real, and the off-diagonal entries $3-i$ and
$3+i$ are conjugates of each other. Transpose-and-conjugate and you get $\mat A$
back. By contrast $\colvec{2 & 3-i\\3-i & 5}$ is \emph{not} Hermitian -- the
off-diagonal pair must be conjugates, not equal.
\end{example}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Could a Hermitian matrix have $4 + 2i$ on its main diagonal?
No: the definition $\mat A^* = \mat A$ requires $(\mat A)_{kk} = \overline{(\mat A)_{kk}}$.
The only complex numbers that equal their own conjugates are real numbers ($\operatorname{Im}(z) = 0$).
Every diagonal entry of a Hermitian matrix is real.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Real eigenvalues}

Hermitian matrices share the main property of symmetric matrices, and the short
proof shows why the conjugate inner product was needed.

\begin{theorem}\label{ch32:realeig}
Every eigenvalue of a Hermitian matrix is real.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\mat A^{*}=\mat A$ and suppose $\mat A\vec x = \lambda\vec x$ with
$\vec x\ne\vec 0$. Form the number $\vec x^{*}\mat A\vec x$ and compute it two
ways. First, using $\mat A\vec x=\lambda\vec x$,
\[
  \vec x^{*}\mat A\vec x = \vec x^{*}(\lambda\vec x)
  = \lambda\,\vec x^{*}\vec x = \lambda\,\norm{\vec x}^2.
\]
Second, take the conjugate transpose of the scalar $\vec x^{*}\mat A\vec x$. A
$1\times 1$ matrix is its own transpose, so applying the star only conjugates the
number, while on the right it distributes by the reversal rule and uses
$\mat A^{*}=\mat A$:
\[
  \overline{\vec x^{*}\mat A\vec x} = (\vec x^{*}\mat A\vec x)^{*}
  = \vec x^{*}\mat A^{*}\vec x = \vec x^{*}\mat A\vec x.
\]
So $\vec x^{*}\mat A\vec x$ equals its own conjugate, hence is real. But it also
equals $\lambda\norm{\vec x}^2$, and $\norm{\vec x}^2>0$ is a positive real. A real
number divided by a positive real is real, so $\lambda$ is real.
\end{proof}

This is the complex version of the proof for symmetric matrices in Chapter~26.
The key step is that $\norm{\vec x}^2$ is real and positive, which holds because
we built the inner product that way. With the naive $\sum z_k^2$ in its place the
proof would fail.

\begin{aestheticbox}
This theorem is why Hermitian matrices appear in physics. In quantum mechanics,
each measurable quantity is represented by a Hermitian matrix whose eigenvalues
are the possible measurement results, and Theorem~\ref{ch32:realeig} guarantees
that these are real numbers, as readings on an instrument must be.
Section~\ref{ch32:quantum-bridge} returns to this.
\end{aestheticbox}

\subsection{Orthogonal eigenvectors and the complex spectral theorem}

As in the real case, eigenvectors of a Hermitian matrix for \emph{different}
eigenvalues are orthogonal. Suppose $\mat A\vec x =
\lambda\vec x$ and $\mat A\vec y = \mu\vec y$ with $\lambda\ne\mu$ (both real, by
the theorem). Then
\begin{align*}
  \lambda\inner{\vec x}{\vec y}
  &= \inner{\lambda\vec x}{\vec y} && \text{($\lambda$ is real, so it leaves the conjugated slot unchanged)}\\
  &= \inner{\mat A\vec x}{\vec y} \\
  &= \vec x^{*}\mat A^{*}\vec y \\
  &= \vec x^{*}\mat A\vec y && \text{($\mat A^{*}=\mat A$)}\\
  &= \inner{\vec x}{\mat A\vec y} \\
  &= \inner{\vec x}{\mu\vec y} \\
  &= \mu\inner{\vec x}{\vec y}.
\end{align*}
Thus $(\lambda-\mu)\inner{\vec x}{\vec y}=0$,
and since $\lambda\ne\mu$ we get $\inner{\vec x}{\vec y}=0$. Distinct eigenvalues
give orthogonal eigenvectors, exactly as before.

Together these facts give the complex Spectral Theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Spectral Theorem, complex form]\label{ch32:spectral}
Every Hermitian matrix $\mat A\in\C^{n\times n}$ can be written
\[
  \mat A = \mat U\,\mat\Lambda\,\mat U^{*},
\]
where $\mat\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$ has the (real) eigenvalues
on its diagonal and the columns of $\mat U$ form an orthonormal basis of
eigenvectors. (Such a $\mat U$ satisfies $\mat U^{*}\mat U=\mat I$; it is called
\emph{unitary}, the subject of the next section.)
\end{theorem}

We skip the proof, which follows the real proof of Chapter~26 step by step.
The summary: \textbf{Hermitian is to $\C$ as symmetric is to $\R$.} Every
result about symmetric matrices holds for Hermitian matrices, with stars in place
of transposes.

\begin{strangbox}
Strang presents the real Spectral Theorem $\mat S=\mat Q\mat\Lambda\mat Q\T$ as the
high point of his eigenvalue chapters, then notes briefly that for complex
matrices the transpose is replaced by the conjugate transpose. If you read this
chapter \emph{before} Strang, you will recognize his $\mat S=\mat Q\Lambda\mat Q\T$
as the real special case of $\mat A=\mat U\Lambda\mat U^{*}$. If you read it
\emph{after}, you now have the complex version, the one used by the Fourier
transform and by quantum mechanics.
\end{strangbox}

\section{Unitary matrices: the complex orthogonal}

The other key matrix family of the real theory was the orthogonal matrix $\mat Q$
with $\mat Q\T\mat Q=\mat I$: the rotations and reflections, which preserve every
length and angle. Their complex version is defined by the same equation with a
star.

\begin{definition}\label{ch32:unitarydef}
A square matrix $\mat U\in\C^{n\times n}$ is \textbf{unitary} if
$\mat U^{*}\mat U = \mat I$, equivalently $\mat U^{-1} = \mat U^{*}$. Its columns
form an orthonormal set in $\C^n$.
\end{definition}

Why ``orthonormal columns''? Read off the $(j,k)$ entry of $\mat U^{*}\mat U$: it
is (row $j$ of $\mat U^{*}$) times (column $k$ of $\mat U$) $=
\inner{\vec u_j}{\vec u_k}$, the inner product of two columns of $\mat U$.
Setting $\mat U^{*}\mat U=\mat I$ says $\inner{\vec u_j}{\vec u_k} = \delta_{jk}$,
where $\delta_{jk}$ is the Kronecker delta of Chapter~17 ($1$ if $j=k$, $0$
otherwise): each column has length $1$, and different columns are orthogonal.
This is the real argument with the Hermitian inner product in place of the dot
product.

\subsection{Unitary means length-preserving}

The geometric property that makes these matrices useful is that they
preserve the complex inner product, hence all lengths and angles.

\begin{theorem}\label{ch32:unitarynorm}
If $\mat U$ is unitary then $\inner{\mat U\vec x}{\mat U\vec y} =
\inner{\vec x}{\vec y}$ for all $\vec x,\vec y$, and in particular
$\norm{\mat U\vec x} = \norm{\vec x}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Use the reversal rule $(\mat U\vec x)^{*}=\vec x^{*}\mat U^{*}$:
\begin{align*}
  \inner{\mat U\vec x}{\mat U\vec y} &= (\mat U\vec x)^{*}(\mat U\vec y) \\
  &= \vec x^{*}\mat U^{*}\mat U\vec y \\
  &= \vec x^{*}\mat I\vec y \\
  &= \vec x^{*}\vec y \\
  &= \inner{\vec x}{\vec y}.
\end{align*}
Setting $\vec y=\vec x$ gives $\norm{\mat U\vec x}^2 = \norm{\vec x}^2$.
\end{proof}

So a unitary matrix is a \textbf{rigid motion of $\C^n$}: it may rotate, reflect
and mix the coordinates, but it never changes a length. This is why unitary
matrices are the safe transformations of numerical computing, where applying one
cannot enlarge rounding errors, and of quantum mechanics, where (as
Section~\ref{ch32:quantum-bridge} explains) the length of a state vector must stay
equal to $1$.

\begin{corollary}\label{ch32:unitaryeig}
Every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of a unitary matrix has $\abs\lambda = 1$: the
eigenvalues live on the unit circle.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If $\mat U\vec x=\lambda\vec x$ with $\vec x\ne\vec 0$, then
$\norm{\vec x} = \norm{\mat U\vec x} = \norm{\lambda\vec x} =
\abs\lambda\,\norm{\vec x}$, so $\abs\lambda = 1$.
\end{proof}

Compare this with Hermitian matrices. Hermitian eigenvalues are

real ones being $\pm 1$). Figure~\ref{ch32:eiglocus} puts the

\begin{example}\label{ch32:unitaryex}
The matrix
\[
  \mat U = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\colvec{1 & 1 \\ i & -i}
\]
is unitary. Its columns are $\vec u_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\colvec{1\\i}$ and
$\vec u_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\colvec{1\\-i}$. Check:
\begin{align*}
  \inner{\vec u_1}{\vec u_1} &= \tfrac12(\bar 1\cdot 1 + \bar i\cdot i) \\
  &= \tfrac12\bigl(1 + (-i)(i)\bigr) \\
  &= \tfrac12(1+1) \\
  &= 1, && \text{(length one)}\\
  \inner{\vec u_1}{\vec u_2} &= \tfrac12\bigl(\bar 1\cdot 1 + \bar i\cdot(-i)\bigr) \\
  &= \tfrac12\bigl(1 + (-i)(-i)\bigr) \\
  &= \tfrac12(1-1) \\
  &= 0. && \text{(orthogonal)}
\end{align*}
Two orthonormal columns, so $\mat U^{*}\mat U=\mat I$. (Watch the conjugate on the
$i$ in the first slot: without it you would wrongly get $\inner{\vec u_1}{\vec
u_1}=\tfrac12(1+i^2)=0$, the same trap as before.)
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
Unitary matrices lead into \emph{group theory}. A \emph{group} is a set of
objects with a way of combining two of them (here, matrix multiplication) such that
combining stays inside the set, the set contains an identity element ($\mat I$),
and every member has an inverse in the set. The product of two unitary matrices is
unitary (a rigid motion followed by a rigid motion is a rigid motion), and so is
the inverse $\mat U^{-1}=\mat U^{*}$. So the $n\times n$ unitary matrices form a
group, written $U(n)$, the \emph{unitary group}. Its members of determinant $1$
form $SU(n)$, and $SU(2)$ and $SU(3)$ are symmetry groups used in the Standard
Model, the physicists' theory of elementary particles. The real version
$\mat Q\T\mat Q=\mat I$ gives the groups $O(n)$ (all orthogonal matrices) and
$SO(n)$ (the rotations, those of determinant $1$), the symmetries of spheres and
rigid bodies.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{The discrete Fourier matrix}

If you record a chord played on a piano, the microphone measures the air pressure thousands of times a second. The result is a list of numbers: a sampled sound wave. The natural question to ask about a chord is: \emph{which frequencies (musical notes) are in it, and how much of each?}

The \textbf{discrete Fourier transform} (DFT) answers this question. It takes a signal stored as $n$ samples $x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}$ and changes its basis. Instead of representing the signal as ``value at time $0$, value at time $1$, \dots,'' it represents it as ``amount of frequency $0$, amount of frequency $1$, \dots.'' This change of basis is done by the \textbf{Fourier matrix}. Because the columns of this matrix will turn out to be orthogonal, this is exactly the projection onto an orthogonal basis that we studied in Chapters 17 and 18. And because it is a unitary matrix (after scaling), it preserves lengths: the total ``energy'' of the signal is the same whether we measure it in time or in frequency.

\subsection{Euler's formula and walking around the circle}

To build the ``frequencies'' we project onto, we need complex numbers. In Chapter~23 we met Euler's formula:
\[
  e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta.
\]
This equation says that $e^{i\theta}$ is the point on the unit circle at angle $\theta$.

If we want to split a circle into $n$ equal steps, the angle for one step is $2\pi/n$. The complex number that takes this step is
\[
  \omega_n = e^{2\pi i / n} = \cos\frac{2\pi}{n} + i\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}.
\]
This $\omega_n$ is called a \textbf{primitive $n$-th root of unity}. Its powers, $\omega_n^0, \omega_n^1, \omega_n^2, \dots$, walk around the unit circle, taking steps of size $2\pi/n$. After $n$ steps, $\omega_n^n = e^{2\pi i} = 1$, returning exactly to the start.

\subsection{Sampled waves as vectors}

A ``frequency $k$'' wave completes $k$ full cycles across our $n$ samples. We represent this wave as a vector $\vec f_k$ in $\C^n$. Its $m$-th entry (at time step $m$) is $\omega_n^{km}$:
\[
  \vec f_k = \colvec{1 \\ \omega_n^k \\ \omega_n^{2k} \\ \vdots \\ \omega_n^{(n-1)k}}.
\]
This vector steps around the circle taking jumps of size $k$.
- \textbf{Frequency 0:} $\vec f_0 = \colvec{1 & 1 & \dots & 1}\T$. A flat, non-oscillating signal.
- \textbf{Frequency 1:} $\vec f_1 = \colvec{1 & \omega_n & \omega_n^2 & \dots}\T$. Completes exactly 1 cycle.
- \textbf{Frequency 2:} $\vec f_2 = \colvec{1 & \omega_n^2 & \omega_n^4 & \dots}\T$. Completes 2 cycles, stepping twice as fast.

\subsection{Why the frequency vectors are orthogonal}

To find ``how much of $\vec f_k$'' is in our signal $\vec x$, we want to project $\vec x$ onto $\vec f_k$. In Chapters 17 and 18, we saw that projections are easy (just a dot product) \emph{if} the basis vectors are orthogonal. Are the frequency vectors orthogonal?

Take the Hermitian inner product of $\vec f_j$ and $\vec f_k$. Remember to conjugate the first vector, which turns $\omega_n$ into $\overline{\omega_n} = \omega_n^{-1}$:
\begin{align*}
  \inner{\vec f_j}{\vec f_k} &= \vec f_j^* \vec f_k \\
  &= \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} \overline{\omega_n^{mj}} \, \omega_n^{mk} \\
  &= \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} \omega_n^{m(k-j)}.
\end{align*}
This is a sum of powers of a single complex number $q = \omega_n^{k-j}$.
If $j=k$, then $q = \omega_n^0 = 1$, and we are summing $1 + 1 + \dots + 1 = n$. So the squared length of each frequency vector is $n$.

If $j \neq k$, then $q$ is some root of unity other than 1. As we add its powers, we are summing points equally spaced around the circle. Picture adding the arrows of a fast and a slow walk: by symmetry, they pull equally in all directions and cancel out perfectly to $0$. (Algebraically, it is a geometric series $(q^n-1)/(q-1) = 0$ because $q^n = 1$). 

\begin{intuitionbox}
The points of any $n$-th root of unity (except 1) sum to zero, because they form a symmetric star or polygon in the complex plane. This geometric cancellation is why different frequency waves are perfectly orthogonal.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Building the Fourier matrix and computing the DFT}

We pack these orthogonal frequency vectors into the rows of a matrix. (By convention, the DFT matrix puts them in rows so that multiplying $\mat F_n \vec x$ takes the inner product of each frequency with the signal $\vec x$).

The \textbf{$n \times n$ Fourier matrix} $\mat F_n$ has entries:
\[
  (\mat F_n)_{jk} = \omega_n^{jk}, \qquad j,k = 0,1,\dots,n-1.
\]
Let's build $\mat F_4$. Here $\omega_4 = e^{2\pi i/4} = i$. The powers of $i$ are $i^0=1$, $i^1=i$, $i^2=-1$, $i^3=-i$.
\begin{align*}
  \mat F_4 &= \begin{bmatrix}
    i^0 & i^0 & i^0 & i^0 \\
    i^0 & i^1 & i^2 & i^3 \\
    i^0 & i^2 & i^4 & i^6 \\
    i^0 & i^3 & i^6 & i^9
  \end{bmatrix} \\
  &= \begin{bmatrix}
    1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
    1 & i & -1 & -i \\
    1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\
    1 & -i & -1 & i
  \end{bmatrix}.
\end{align*}
Notice that row 0 is flat. Row 1 walks counterclockwise ($1, i, -1, -i$). Row 2 alternates ($1, -1, 1, -1$). Row 3 walks clockwise.

Let's compute the DFT of a concrete signal $\vec x = \colvec{1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0}$ (a single sharp spike at time 0).
\begin{align*}
  \mat F_4 \vec x &= \begin{bmatrix}
    1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
    1 & i & -1 & -i \\
    1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\
    1 & -i & -1 & i
  \end{bmatrix} \colvec{1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0} \\
  &= \colvec{1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1}.
\end{align*}
The output tells us that a sudden spike in time contains \emph{every frequency} in equal amounts! A sharp corner cannot be built from just slow waves; it requires all the fast waves too.

\subsection{The inverse DFT and energy preservation}

How do we get our signal back? We know the rows of $\mat F_n$ are orthogonal and each has length squared $n$. This means $\mat F_n^* \mat F_n = n \mat I$. 
Therefore, the inverse matrix is just the conjugate transpose divided by $n$:
\[
  \mat F_n^{-1} = \frac{1}{n} \mat F_n^*.
\]
To go from frequencies back to time, you conjugate the matrix (which reverses the direction of all the waves) and divide by $n$.

Because $\mat F_n^* \mat F_n = n \mat I$, the normalized matrix $\mat U = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \mat F_n$ satisfies $\mat U^* \mat U = \mat I$. It is a \textbf{unitary matrix}. By Theorem~\ref{ch32:unitarynorm}, unitary matrices preserve the length of vectors: $\norm{\mat U \vec x} = \norm{\vec x}$. In physics and engineering, the squared length of a signal is its \emph{energy}. The unitary property proves \textbf{Parseval's theorem}: the total energy of a signal in the time domain exactly equals its total energy in the frequency domain.

\begin{historybox}
Joseph Fourier introduced this idea in 1807 to solve equations describing how heat flows through a metal bar. He boldly claimed that \emph{any} signal, even one with sharp jumps, could be built by adding up smooth sine and cosine waves. The examining committee, including mathematical giants Lagrange and Laplace, refused to publish it for years, citing a lack of rigor. But Fourier was right, and his transform became the foundation of modern signal processing.
\end{historybox}

\section{From the matrix to the FFT}

Multiplying an $n \times n$ matrix by an $n$-vector directly takes $n^2$ multiplications. For a one-second CD audio clip ($n = 44,100$), $n^2$ is about 2 billion operations. For a long time, this was hopelessly slow.

The \textbf{fast Fourier transform} (FFT) computes exactly the same $\mat F_n \vec x$, but in about $n \log_2 n$ operations. For $n = 44,100$, that is $44,100 \times 15 \approx 660,000$ operations---a speedup of 3,000 times! It turns an hour of computing into a fraction of a second.

\subsection{The divide-and-conquer trick}

The FFT achieves this by splitting the $n$-sample signal into two $n/2$-sample signals: the even-indexed samples $\vec x_{\text{even}} = (x_0, x_2, \dots)$ and the odd-indexed samples $\vec x_{\text{odd}} = (x_1, x_3, \dots)$.

Let's compute the $k$-th frequency output, $X_k = (\mat F_n \vec x)_k$, separating the evens and odds:
\begin{align*}
  X_k &= \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} x_m \omega_n^{km} \\
      &= \sum_{m=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2m} \omega_n^{k(2m)} + \sum_{m=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2m+1} \omega_n^{k(2m+1)} \\
      &= \sum_{m=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2m} (\omega_n^2)^{km} + \omega_n^k \sum_{m=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2m+1} (\omega_n^2)^{km}.
\end{align*}
Notice that $\omega_n^2 = (e^{2\pi i/n})^2 = e^{2\pi i / (n/2)} = \omega_{n/2}$. Taking two steps on an $n$-step circle is exactly the same as taking one step on a half-size circle!
\begin{align*}
  X_k &= \underbrace{\sum_{m=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2m} \omega_{n/2}^{km}}_{E_k} + \omega_n^k \underbrace{\sum_{m=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2m+1} \omega_{n/2}^{km}}_{O_k} \\
      &= E_k + \omega_n^k O_k.
\end{align*}
Here, $E_k$ is exactly the $k$-th output of the half-size DFT of the evens, and $O_k$ is the half-size DFT of the odds. $\omega_n^k$ is called the \textbf{twiddle factor}.

\subsection{The Butterfly: Reusing work}

This formula gives us the first half of the outputs ($k = 0 \dots n/2-1$). What about the second half, $X_{k+n/2}$?
The half-size transforms $E$ and $O$ repeat themselves after $n/2$ steps, so $E_{k+n/2} = E_k$ and $O_{k+n/2} = O_k$.
What about the twiddle factor? Adding $n/2$ to the exponent gives:
\[
  \omega_n^{k+n/2} = \omega_n^k \cdot \omega_n^{n/2} = \omega_n^k \cdot e^{i\pi} = -\omega_n^k.
\]
A half-turn around the circle exactly flips the sign!

\end{align*}

\subsection{Recursion to $O(n \log n)$}

We reduced a size-$n$ transform to two size-$n/2$ transforms plus $n/2$ butterflies. Now we do it again: split each size-$n/2$ transform into two size-$n/4$ transforms. We keep dividing by 2 until we reach size 1 (where the DFT of a single number is just the number itself).

For $n=8$, we split into $4$ and $4$, then $2,2,2,2$, then $1,1,\dots,1$. The number of splitting levels is the number of times you can divide $n$ by $2$, which is exactly $\log_2 n$.

Carl Friedrich Gauss discovered the FFT algorithm around 1805 while interpolating the orbits of asteroids, but he never published it. It was independently rediscovered in 1965 by James Cooley and John Tukey, who were figuring out how to analyze seismometer data to detect offshore Soviet nuclear tests. Today, this algorithm is built into almost everything (see Algorithms Chapter 29 and Signal Processing Chapters 8--9).
\end{historybox}

\section{A unified picture}

The table collects the translation from real to complex: replace every transpose
by a conjugate transpose, and each theorem carries over.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Concept} & \textbf{Real ($\R^n$)} & \textbf{Complex ($\C^n$)}\\
\midrule
Inner product & $\vec u\T\vec v=\sum u_iv_i$ & $\vec u^{*}\vec v=\sum\bar u_iv_i$\\
``Transpose'' & $\mat A\T$ & $\mat A^{*}$ (conjugate transpose)\\
Equal to own ``transpose'' & symmetric $\mat A\T=\mat A$ & Hermitian $\mat A^{*}=\mat A$\\
\quad eigenvalues & real & real\\
Length-preserving & orthogonal $\mat Q\T\mat Q=\mat I$ & unitary $\mat U^{*}\mat U=\mat I$\\
\quad eigenvalues & on unit circle ($\pm 1$ if real) & on unit circle, $\abs\lambda=1$\\
Spectral theorem & $\mat A=\mat Q\Lambda\mat Q\T$ & $\mat A=\mat U\Lambda\mat U^{*}$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{focusbox}
If you remember one thing from this chapter, remember the table above. Complex
linear algebra follows real linear algebra once the conjugate is put on one factor
of every inner product and every transpose becomes a conjugate transpose: star
replaces transpose, and the conjugate goes on exactly one side. The Spectral
Theorem and the geometry of rigid motions carry over unchanged.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Cost and intuition recap.} The conjugate inner product
$\inner{\vec u}{\vec v}=\sum\bar u_kv_k$ costs the same $n$ multiplications as the
real dot product (plus one conjugation per entry), and it keeps
$\norm{\vec z}^2$ real and nonnegative. A Hermitian
matrix gives \emph{real} eigenvalues; a unitary matrix preserves length and gives
eigenvalues of modulus one. Multiplying by the Fourier matrix directly costs $O(n^2)$;
the FFT uses the structure of the roots of unity to bring this down to
$O(n\log n)$, which makes frequency analysis of long signals practical.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
The Fourier matrix combines three central ideas of this book. It is
a \emph{change of basis}: from samples to frequencies. After scaling it is a
\emph{unitary matrix}: a rigid motion of $\C^n$ that loses no information. And it
\emph{diagonalizes} every circular convolution at once, so filtering a signal
becomes entry-by-entry multiplication.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Unitary and Hermitian Matrices as the Language of Quantum Mechanics}
\label{ch32:quantum-bridge}

This chapter has centered on two matrix families:
Hermitian matrices ($\mat A^* = \mat A$), whose eigenvalues are always real,
and unitary matrices ($\mat U^*\mat U = \mat I$), whose columns are orthonormal
and which preserve every complex length and angle.

These are the complex versions of symmetric and orthogonal matrices. They are also
the mathematical language of quantum mechanics and quantum computing. Classical
mechanics describes a particle by real numbers such as its position and velocity;
quantum mechanics describes the state of a system by a complex vector, and each
measurement by a matrix acting on $\C^n$.

\subsection{Quantum states as unit vectors in $\C^n$}

In ordinary digital electronics, a bit is either $0$ or $1$. In a quantum
computer, the basic unit of storage is a \textbf{qubit}, whose state is a vector
in $\C^2$ of length $1$ (the reason for length $1$ comes below). Physicists write
such vectors in the bra-ket notation of Paul Dirac. A column vector is called a
``ket'' and written $\ket{\psi}$ (the Greek letter $\psi$, ``psi'', is the usual
name for a state).
The standard basis vectors of $\C^2$ are labeled
\[
  \ket{0} = \colvec{1\\0}, \qquad \ket{1} = \colvec{0\\1}.
\]
An arbitrary qubit state is a linear combination of these basis vectors:
\[
  \ket{\psi} = \alpha\ket{0} + \beta\ket{1} = \colvec{\alpha \\ \beta},
  \qquad \alpha,\beta \in \C.
\]

\centering

  \draw[->,gray!55] (0,-1.2) -- (0,1.2);

  \draw[->,thick,black] (0,0) -- (1,0) node[right] {$\ket{0}$};

\label{ch32:qubit-real}
\end{figure}


The complex scalars $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are called \textbf{probability
amplitudes}; the Born rule below says how they give probabilities.

The conjugate transpose of the column vector $\ket{\psi}$ is the row vector
$\bra{\psi} = \ket{\psi}^* = \begin{bmatrix}\bar\alpha & \bar\beta\end{bmatrix}$,
called a ``bra.'' When we take the Hermitian inner product
$\inner{\phi}{\psi} = \vec\phi^*\vec\psi$ from Definition~\ref{ch32:inner}, Dirac's
notation snaps together as $\braket{\phi}{\psi}$---a ``bra'' followed by a
``ket'' forming a closed bracket.

The physical meaning of amplitudes is given by the \textbf{Born rule}: when a
qubit in state $\ket{\psi}$ is measured, the result is $0$ with probability
$\abs{\alpha}^2$ and $1$ with probability $\abs{\beta}^2$, and afterwards the qubit
is in state $\ket{0}$ or $\ket{1}$ accordingly (physicists say the state
\emph{collapses}). Exactly one of the outcomes happens, so their probabilities
must add up to $1$, and every state must have length $1$:
\[
  \norm{\ket{\psi}}^2 = \braket{\psi}{\psi} = \abs{\alpha}^2 + \abs{\beta}^2 = 1.
\]
A single qubit is a unit vector on the unit sphere in $\C^2$. When we combine
$n$ qubits (a \emph{register}), the state vector lives in $\C^{2^n}$. Each of the
$2^n$ strings of $n$ bits corresponds to a standard basis vector, and the state
of the register is a unit vector whose $2^n$ complex components are the
amplitudes of those bit strings.

\subsection{Quantum gates as unitary matrices}

If an isolated quantum system (one not disturbed by its surroundings) begins in a
state of length $1$, any physical change must keep that length. If a transformation is represented by
a linear operator $\mat U$, we require
$\norm{\mat U\ket{\psi}} = \norm{\ket{\psi}} = 1$ for every state vector
$\ket{\psi}$.

By Theorem~\ref{ch32:unitarynorm}, \textbf{unitary matrices} preserve length, and
conversely a square matrix that preserves every length is unitary (we do not prove
this direction here). So every basic operation of a quantum computer (called a
\emph{gate}), and every change of an isolated system over time (its \emph{time
evolution}), is given by a unitary matrix.

Three single-qubit unitary matrices appear constantly:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{Pauli-$\mat X$ gate} (the quantum NOT):
  \[
    \mat X = \colvec{0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0}, \qquad
    \mat X\ket{0} = \ket{1}, \quad \mat X\ket{1} = \ket{0}.
  \]
  It swaps the two basis vectors.
  \item The \textbf{Pauli-$\mat Z$ gate} (the phase flip):
  \[
    \mat Z = \colvec{1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1}, \qquad
    \mat Z\ket{0} = \ket{0}, \quad \mat Z\ket{1} = -\ket{1}.
  \]
  It leaves $\ket{0}$ unaffected and negates the amplitude of $\ket{1}$.
  \item The \textbf{Hadamard gate} $\mat H$:
  \[
    \mat H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\colvec{1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1}.
  \]
\end{itemize}
Because $\mat H$ has real symmetric entries, $\mat H^* = \mat H$, and its columns
are orthonormal, $\mat H^*\mat H = \mat I$. The Hadamard gate is simultaneously
unitary and Hermitian. When $\mat H$ acts on the standard basis vector $\ket{0}$,
it creates an equal \emph{superposition}, a state that combines both basis
states:
\[
  \mat H\ket{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\colvec{1\\1}
  = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{1}.
\]
Measuring this state gives $0$ or $1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each.
Unitary matrices preserve inner products:
$\inner{\mat U\vec u}{\mat U\vec v} = \inner{\vec u}{\vec v}$. Two orthogonal
states remain orthogonal after any unitary operation. Because every unitary matrix
satisfies $\mat U^{-1} = \mat U^*$, every quantum gate can be undone:
applying the conjugate transpose $\mat U^*$ runs the gate backwards, restoring
the original state with zero loss of information.

\subsection{Physical observables as Hermitian matrices}

When an experimenter measures a physical quantity such as energy, position or
spin, the instrument always shows a \textbf{real number}. No measurement ever
gives an energy of $2 + 5i$ units.

This is why each measurable quantity (an \emph{observable}) is represented by a
\textbf{Hermitian matrix} $\mat A^* = \mat A$. The properties
proven in Theorem~\ref{ch32:realeig} and Theorem~\ref{ch32:spectral} provide the
exact physical interpretation:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Real eigenvalues as observable values:} The eigenvalues
  $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ of $\mat A$ are real. These numbers are
  the only values the detector can report.
  \item \textbf{Orthonormal eigenvectors as definite states:} By the Spectral
  Theorem, $\mat A$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors
  $\vec v_1, \dots, \vec v_n$ with $\mat A\vec v_k = \lambda_k\vec v_k$. A
  system in state $\vec v_k$ is guaranteed to yield the value $\lambda_k$ with
  $100\%$ certainty.
  \item \textbf{Measurement probabilities:} Write the eigenvectors as kets
  $\ket{v_k}=\vec v_k$ and expand any state in this orthonormal basis,
  $\ket{\psi} = \sum_{k=1}^n c_k \ket{v_k}$, where $c_k = \braket{v_k}{\psi}$
  (coordinates in an orthonormal basis are inner products, Chapter~17).
  Measuring $\mat A$ gives the value $\lambda_k$ with probability
  $\abs{c_k}^2 = \abs{\braket{v_k}{\psi}}^2$, and afterwards the state is
  $\ket{v_k}$.
  \item \textbf{Real expectation values:} The \emph{expectation value}
  $\langle \mat A \rangle$ is the long-run average of the measured values over
  many identical trials. It is
  \[
    \langle \mat A \rangle = \braket{\psi}{\mat A\psi} = \ket{\psi}^*\mat A\ket{\psi}
    = \sum_{k=1}^n \lambda_k \abs{\braket{v_k}{\psi}}^2.
  \]
  Because $\mat A^* = \mat A$, this number is real, by the same argument as in
  Theorem~\ref{ch32:realeig}:
  \begin{align*}
    \bigl(\ket{\psi}^*\mat A\ket{\psi}\bigr)^* &= \ket{\psi}^*\mat A^*\ket{\psi} \\
    &= \ket{\psi}^*\mat A\ket{\psi}.
\end{align*}
\end{enumerate}

In summary: complex unit vectors represent physical states, unitary matrices represent time evolutions and
computational gates, and Hermitian matrices represent observable quantities.

In Chapter 42 of the companion computer science volume (\emph{Algorithms From the
Ground Up}), these unitary matrices become algorithms. A quantum computation is
arranged so that the amplitudes of wrong answers cancel and those of right answers
add up. That is how Shor's algorithm factors large whole numbers far faster than
any known ordinary method, and how Grover's algorithm searches $N$ unsorted items
in about $\sqrt N$ steps instead of $N$.

\section{Power and limits}

Conjugating one factor of the inner product gave complex vectors lengths and
angles, with $\norm{\vec z}^2 > 0$ for every $\vec z \ne \vec 0$. Hermitian matrices
then behave like real symmetric matrices, with real eigenvalues, and unitary
matrices preserve every length and angle in $\C^n$. That gave us the discrete
Fourier transform and the language of quantum states, gates and measurements.

The Fourier basis has a limitation. Its basis vectors are waves that last for the
whole duration of the signal.
If an audio recording contains a single sharp pop, or an image contains a sharp
vertical edge, that short spike spreads across every
frequency coefficient in the transform (as in Figure~\ref{ch32:spikeflat}). The standard Fourier matrix tells you
which notes were sounded, but nothing about \emph{when} they
were played. Two tools address this: windowed Fourier transforms, which
transform short stretches of the signal one at a time, and wavelets, basis
vectors that are short bursts rather than endless waves.

A second limitation concerns computation. On paper every formula in this chapter
is exact, but a computer stores numbers in floating-point form, with a fixed
number of digits, and rounds after each operation. Unitary transforms such as the
normalized FFT cope well, because a length-preserving map never enlarges rounding
errors. Most matrices are not unitary, and solving $\mat A\vec x=\vec b$ with them
can lose many digits. Chapter~33, \emph{Numerical Linear Algebra}, studies
condition numbers (which measure how much a matrix can magnify errors), the
stability of algorithms under rounding, and the matrix factorizations that make
scientific computing reliable.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-innerprod}
Let $\vec u=\colvec{2i\\1}$ and $\vec v=\colvec{1+i\\3i}$ in $\C^2$. Compute
$\inner{\vec u}{\vec v}$ and $\inner{\vec v}{\vec u}$, and verify that
$\inner{\vec v}{\vec u}=\overline{\inner{\vec u}{\vec v}}$. Then compute
$\norm{\vec u}$ and $\norm{\vec v}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-naivefail}
Find a nonzero vector $\vec z\in\C^2$ with $\sum z_k^2 = 0$ but
$\norm{\vec z}^2=\sum\abs{z_k}^2 \ne 0$. Explain in one sentence why this shows the
naive ``dot product'' $\sum z_k^2$ cannot define a length.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-star}
For $\mat A=\colvec{2 & i\\ -i & 4}$ and $\mat B=\colvec{1 & 1+i\\ 1-i & 0}$,
compute $\mat A^{*}$ and $\mat B^{*}$. Which of $\mat A,\mat B$ are Hermitian?
For the Hermitian one(s), confirm by inspection that the diagonal is real and the
off-diagonal entries are conjugates.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-hermeig}
The matrix $\mat A=\colvec{3 & 2i\\ -2i & 3}$ is Hermitian. Find its eigenvalues
(they should come out real, by Theorem~\ref{ch32:realeig}) and a pair of orthogonal
eigenvectors. Verify the orthogonality with the Hermitian inner product.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-unitarycheck}
Show that $\mat Q=\colvec{\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta}$ is
unitary for every real $\theta$ (it is even orthogonal). Then show that
$\mat V=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\colvec{1 & i\\ i & 1}$ is unitary, and find a vector whose
length it preserves to double-check Theorem~\ref{ch32:unitarynorm}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-rootsum}
Using $\omega=e^{2\pi i/6}$, list the six sixth-roots of unity in the form
$e^{2\pi ik/6}$ and plot them on the unit circle. Verify (by the geometric-series
argument, or numerically) that they sum to zero. What is $\omega^3$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-f4}
Using $\mat F_4$ from the text, compute $\mat F_4\vec x$ for
$\vec x=\colvec{1\\-1\\1\\-1}$. Which single frequency does the answer pick out, and
why does that match the fact that $\vec x$ alternates sign every step (the
\emph{highest} frequency representable on $4$ points)?

\textbf{Answer:} We multiply:
\[
  \mat F_4 \vec x = \begin{bmatrix}
    1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
    1 & i & -1 & -i \\
    1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\
    1 & -i & -1 & i
  \end{bmatrix} \colvec{1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \\ -1} = \colvec{1 - 1 + 1 - 1 \\ 1 - i - 1 + i \\ 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 \\ 1 + i - 1 - i} = \colvec{0 \\ 0 \\ 4 \\ 0}.
\]
The output picks out exactly frequency 2 (the third entry, index 2). This matches because $\vec x$ completes exactly two full cycles (up, down, up, down) across the 4 points, which is the highest oscillation possible on a 4-point grid.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-f4unitary}
Verify directly that $\tfrac14\mat F_4^{*}\mat F_4=\mat I$ by computing the inner
product of column $1$ with column $1$ (should be $4$) and column $1$ with column $3$
(should be $0$) of $\mat F_4$. Identify the roots-of-unity cancellation in the
second computation.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-unitarysim}
\textbf{(Proof.)} Prove that unitary similarity preserves eigenvalues: if $\mat U$
is unitary and $\mat A=\mat U^{*}\mat A_0\mat U$, then $\mat A$ and $\mat A_0$ have
the same characteristic polynomial, hence the same eigenvalues. Then prove the
special case used in the chapter: if $\mat A_0$ is Hermitian, so is $\mat A$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-normalproof}
\textbf{(Challenge.)} A matrix is called \textbf{normal} if $\mat A^{*}\mat A=
\mat A\mat A^{*}$. Verify that every Hermitian matrix and every unitary matrix is
normal. Then explain, in a sentence or two, why ``normal'' is exactly the class of
matrices to which the complex Spectral Theorem applies (eigenvectors form an
orthonormal basis) -- making Hermitian and unitary two special cases of one bigger
truth.
\end{exercise}


\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-butterfly}
\textbf{(One FFT butterfly.)} In an 8-point FFT, at the last step, you are computing the full outputs $X_k$ for $k=0 \dots 7$ from the half-size outputs $E_k$ and $O_k$. Suppose for $k=1$, the half-size transforms gave $E_1 = 4$ and $O_1 = 2i$. The twiddle factor is $\omega_8^1 = e^{2\pi i/8} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. Use the butterfly formula to compute the two outputs $X_1$ and $X_5$.

\textbf{Answer:} 
The butterfly pairs output $k=1$ and $k+4=5$.
The formulas are $X_1 = E_1 + \omega_8^1 O_1$ and $X_5 = E_1 - \omega_8^1 O_1$.
First compute the twiddle multiplication:
\[
  \omega_8^1 O_1 = \left( \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right) (2i) = i\sqrt{2} + i^2\sqrt{2} = -\sqrt{2} + i\sqrt{2}.
\]
Then add and subtract this from $E_1$:
\begin{align*}
  X_1 &= 4 + (-\sqrt{2} + i\sqrt{2}) = (4 - \sqrt{2}) + i\sqrt{2}, \\
  X_5 &= 4 - (-\sqrt{2} + i\sqrt{2}) = (4 + \sqrt{2}) - i\sqrt{2}.
\end{align*}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-fft-count}
\textbf{(FFT counting.)} Let $T(n)$ be the number of combine operations the FFT
uses on a signal of length $n=2^m$. The divide-and-conquer step gives roughly
$T(n)=2\,T(n/2)+n$ with $T(1)=0$. Unroll this recurrence to show $T(n)=n\log_2 n$,
and compare $T(1024)$ with the naive $1024^2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch32:ex-hadamard-quantum}
\textbf{(Hadamard gate as an observable and a gate.)} The Hadamard matrix
$\mat H = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\colvec{1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1}$ is both unitary
($\mat H^*\mat H = \mat I$) and Hermitian ($\mat H^* = \mat H$).
Find its eigenvalues and verify they are real. Find a pair of orthonormal
eigenvectors. Then, for the state $\ket{0} = \colvec{1\\0}$, compute the
expectation value $\langle \mat H \rangle = \ket{0}^*\mat H\ket{0}$.
\end{exercise}
