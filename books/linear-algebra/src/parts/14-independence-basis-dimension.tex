\chapter{Independence, Basis, and Dimension}

In Chapters~12 and~13, we used Gaussian elimination to describe the column space and nullspace
of an $m\times n$ matrix $\mat{A}$ with $r$ pivots,
finding $r$ pivot columns for $\col(\mat{A})$ and $n - r$ special solutions for $\nullsp(\mat{A})$.
In each case, a small set of vectors generated an entire subspace.

This chapter answers the question behind those recipes: \emph{how many vectors do you
need to build a subspace, and how do you know when you have exactly enough, with no wasted vectors
and no missing directions?} The answer uses three ideas:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Independence}: no vector in your set is redundant.
  \item \textbf{Spanning}: combinations of your set reach every vector in the space.
  \item \textbf{Basis}: a set that is both independent and spanning.
\end{itemize}
Every basis of a given space turns out to have the same number of vectors. That number is
the \textbf{dimension} of the space, and it measures its size.

\section{The redundant vector: a picture of dependence}
\label{ch14:redundant}

Given a collection of vectors, are any of them redundant? A vector is redundant
if it can be written as a linear combination of the others, so it adds no new
direction to the span.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of each vector as a direction of motion from the origin. A set of vectors is
\textbf{dependent} if at least one vector points somewhere you could already
reach by combining the others. That vector is redundant: removing it does not
change the span. A set is \textbf{independent} if every vector provides a
new direction: remove any one of them and the span gets smaller.
\end{intuitionbox}

Here is an example in $\R^2$. Take
\[
  \vec{v}_1 = \colvec{2 \\ 1}, \qquad
  \vec{v}_2 = \colvec{-1 \\ 1}, \qquad
  \vec{v}_3 = \colvec{1 \\ 5}.
\]
The first two point in different directions: neither is a scalar multiple of the
other, so together they reach every point of the plane. The third vector adds nothing:
\[
  2\,\vec{v}_1 + 3\,\vec{v}_2
  = 2\colvec{2\\1} + 3\colvec{-1\\1}
  = \colvec{4\\2} + \colvec{-3\\3}
  = \colvec{1\\5}
  = \vec{v}_3 .
\]
So any linear combination of $\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3$ is already in the
span of $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$. The three vectors are \textbf{dependent}:
$\vec{v}_3$ is redundant. Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-redundant} draws the walk that
reaches $\vec{v}_3$.

There is a symmetric way to state this redundancy without singling out one
particular vector. Move everything to one side of the equation
$\vec{v}_3 = 2\vec{v}_1 + 3\vec{v}_2$:
\[
  2\,\vec{v}_1 + 3\,\vec{v}_2 - \vec{v}_3 = \vec{0}.
\]
We have written the zero vector as a combination of $\vec{v}_1,\vec{v}_2,
\vec{v}_3$ \emph{without using all-zero coefficients}. That is the defining
condition for linear dependence, and it is the definition used for
the rest of the book.

\begin{focusbox}
Dependence means the zero vector can be reached in a \emph{nontrivial} way,
with coefficients that are not all zero. Independence means the \emph{only}
linear combination that gives $\vec{0}$ has every coefficient equal to zero
(the \emph{trivial} combination).
\end{focusbox}

\section{Linear independence, defined precisely}
\label{ch14:def}

\begin{definition}[Linear independence]
\label{ch14:def-indep}
A list of vectors $\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \dots, \vec{v}_k$ in a vector space $V$
is \textbf{linearly independent} if the only scalars $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k$
satisfying
\[
  c_1 \vec{v}_1 + c_2 \vec{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k \vec{v}_k = \vec{0}
\]
are $c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_k = 0$. If \emph{any} choice of scalars that are not
all zero produces $\vec{0}$, the list is \textbf{linearly dependent}.
\end{definition}

The definition is stated in terms of the zero vector, but its main use is
\emph{uniqueness}: each vector in the span can be built from the list in only one way.
Suppose some vector $\vec{w}$ in the span could be written two ways, with
coefficients $a_1,\dots,a_k$ and $b_1,\dots,b_k$:
\[
  \vec{w} = a_1\vec{v}_1 + \cdots + a_k\vec{v}_k
        = b_1\vec{v}_1 + \cdots + b_k\vec{v}_k .
\]
Subtract the second expression from the first:
\[
  (a_1-b_1)\vec{v}_1 + \cdots + (a_k-b_k)\vec{v}_k = \vec{0}.
\]
If the
$\vec{v}_i$ are independent, every difference $a_i - b_i$ must be zero, so the
two ``different'' representations were the same all along. So independence
means the recipe (the list of coefficients) for building $\vec{w}$ is unambiguous.
This is what will let us use a basis as a coordinate system in
Section~\ref{ch14:coords}.

\begin{trapbox}
``Linearly independent'' is a property of the \emph{whole list together}, not of
individual vectors. No single vector is ``independent.'' And pairwise checks are
not enough: three vectors can be independent two-at-a-time yet dependent as a
trio. In $\R^2$,
\[
  \colvec{1\\0},\ \colvec{0\\1},\ \colvec{1\\1}
\]
has no two parallel vectors, yet $\colvec{1\\0}+\colvec{0\\1}-\colvec{1\\1}=\vec{0}$,
so the three are dependent. Always test the full set at once.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection*{Two vectors: independence is non-parallelism}

For two vectors the definition becomes something you can see.
$\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2\}$ is dependent exactly when one is a scalar multiple of
the other, that is, when they are parallel (this includes the case where one of them is
$\vec{0}$, since $\vec{0} = 0\cdot\vec{v}$ for any $\vec{v}$). If $c_1\vec{v}_1 + c_2\vec{v}_2 = \vec{0}$ with, say, $c_1 \ne 0$,
then $\vec{v}_1 = -(c_2/c_1)\vec{v}_2$, a scalar multiple. Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-pairs}
shows both cases.

\begin{focusbox}
Two nonzero vectors in $\R^2$ or $\R^3$ are independent $\iff$ they are not parallel $\iff$ they span
a plane (not just a line).
\end{focusbox}

\subsection*{Three vectors: trapped in a plane versus stepping into 3D}

Two independent vectors $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$ in $\R^3$ span a flat two-dimensional sheet (a plane) passing through the origin. What happens when you bring in a third vector $\vec{v}_3$?

There are two possibilities, illustrated in Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-3d-indep}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item If $\vec{v}_3$ lies flat inside that plane, it adds no new directions: every multiple of $\vec{v}_3$ could already be reached using $\vec{v}_1$ and $\vec{v}_2$. The trio $\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3\}$ is \textbf{linearly dependent}, even though no two vectors are parallel.
  \item If $\vec{v}_3$ points up or down out of the plane, it adds the third direction. Combinations of the three now reach every point in space. The trio $\{\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2, \vec{v}_3\}$ is \textbf{linearly independent}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{labbox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} What if you bring a \emph{fourth} vector $\vec{v}_4$ into $\R^3$?
Three independent vectors in $\R^3$ span all of $\R^3$. Any fourth vector must
already lie in their span, so any four vectors in $\R^3$ are linearly dependent.
Theorem~\ref{ch14:thm-toomany} below proves this with elimination.
\end{labbox}

\subsection*{Any set containing the zero vector is dependent}

If $\vec{v}_1 = \vec{0}$, then $1\cdot\vec{v}_1 + 0\cdot\vec{v}_2 + \cdots +
0\cdot\vec{v}_k = \vec{0}$ uses a nonzero coefficient (the leading $1$), so the
set is dependent. The same works wherever $\vec{0}$ sits in the list. In pictures:
the zero vector points in no direction, so it adds nothing to the span.

\section{The independence test you will actually run}
\label{ch14:test}

To test whether a given list of vectors is linearly independent, put the
vectors in the columns of a matrix and use Gaussian elimination.

The equation $c_1\vec{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\vec{v}_k = \vec{0}$ is the
matrix equation $\mat{A}\vec{c} = \vec{0}$, where the columns of $\mat{A}$ are the
$\vec{v}_i$ and $\vec{c} = (c_1,\dots,c_k)$ is the vector of unknown coefficients. So:

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Independence as a nullspace condition.} Let $\mat{A}$ have columns
$\vec{v}_1,\dots,\vec{v}_k$. The columns are linearly independent if and only if
$\nullsp(\mat{A}) = \{\vec{0}\}$, i.e.\ $\mat{A}\vec{c}=\vec{0}$ has \emph{only}
the trivial solution $\vec{c}=\vec{0}$. Equivalently, after elimination, \emph{every column is a
pivot column}: there are no free variables.
\end{formalbox}

A free variable can be set to $1$, which produces a nonzero solution of $\mat{A}\vec{c}=\vec{0}$:
a nontrivial linear combination that sums to zero. With no free
variables, the only solution is $\vec{c}=\vec{0}$, so the columns are
independent.

\begin{example}[Testing three vectors in $\R^3$]
\label{ch14:ex-test3}
Are these independent?
\[
  \vec{v}_1=\colvec{1\\2\\3},\quad
  \vec{v}_2=\colvec{1\\1\\1},\quad
  \vec{v}_3=\colvec{3\\5\\7}.
\]
Put them in the columns of $\mat{A}$ and eliminate:
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3\\ 2 & 1 & 5\\ 3 & 1 & 7\end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow[R_3-3R_1]{R_2-2R_1}
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3\\ 0 & -1 & -1\\ 0 & -2 & -2\end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{R_3-2R_2}
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3\\ 0 & -1 & -1\\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The third column has no pivot, so $c_3$ is a free variable. So there is a
nontrivial solution and the vectors are \textbf{dependent}. To find the dependence,
set the free variable $c_3 = 1$ and back-substitute:
\begin{align*}
  \text{row } 2:&\quad -c_2 - c_3 = 0 &&\Rightarrow\quad c_2 = -1,\\
  \text{row } 1:&\quad c_1 + c_2 + 3c_3 = 0 &&\Rightarrow\quad c_1 = 1 - 3 = -2.
\end{align*}
Check:
\[
  -2\vec{v}_1 - \vec{v}_2 + \vec{v}_3
  = -2\colvec{1\\2\\3} - \colvec{1\\1\\1} + \colvec{3\\5\\7}
  = \colvec{0\\0\\0}. \checkmark
\]
Rearranged, $\vec{v}_3 = 2\vec{v}_1 + \vec{v}_2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[An independent set of three vectors]
\label{ch14:ex-indep3}
Change one entry (the $7$ in the corner becomes $8$):
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3\\ 2 & 1 & 5\\ 3 & 1 & 8\end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow[R_3-3R_1]{R_2-2R_1}
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3\\ 0 & -1 & -1\\ 0 & -2 & -1\end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{R_3-2R_2}
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3\\ 0 & -1 & -1\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Now there are three pivots, one in every column. No free variables, so
$\mat{A}\vec{c}=\vec{0}$ forces $\vec{c}=\vec{0}$: the columns are
\textbf{independent}. (Section~\ref{ch14:dim} will show that three independent
vectors in $\R^3$ always form a basis; and this $\mat{A}$ is invertible.)
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Test $\colvec{1\\3},\ \colvec{2\\6},\ \colvec{0\\1}$ by hand. (i) Are the first
two independent? (ii) Is the whole set? (iii) You have three vectors in $\R^2$ ---
predict the answer \emph{before} eliminating, then confirm. What general rule
about ``more vectors than dimensions'' are you discovering?
\end{labbox}

\subsection*{More vectors than components: dependence is forced}

The lab points at a theorem used throughout the book: if you have more vectors
than each vector has components, some vector is redundant.

\begin{theorem}[Too many vectors must be dependent]
\label{ch14:thm-toomany}
Any list of $k$ vectors in $\R^n$ with $k > n$ is linearly dependent.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Form the $n \times k$ matrix $\mat{A}$ whose columns are the vectors. The
equation $\mat{A}\vec{c} = \vec{0}$ is a homogeneous system with $n$ equations
and $k$ unknowns. Elimination produces at most $n$ pivots (one per row), so at
most $n$ of the $k$ columns are pivot columns. Since $k > n$, at least one column
is free, giving a free variable and hence a nonzero solution $\vec{c}$. That is a
nontrivial dependence.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
In $\R^3$, three independent vectors already span everything, so a fourth vector
is a combination of those three. In general, $\R^n$ has room for at most $n$
independent vectors. The same counting argument, made slightly more general in
Lemma~\ref{ch14:lem-steinitz}, is what makes dimension well-defined.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Spanning sets: reaching everything}
\label{ch14:span}

Independence means no redundancy. Spanning is the other requirement:
no missing directions. We met span in Chapter~3; here is the definition again
for a general vector space.

\begin{definition}[Spanning set]
\label{ch14:def-span}
Vectors $\vec{v}_1,\dots,\vec{v}_k$ \textbf{span} a vector space $V$ if every
$\vec{w}\in V$ can be written as some linear combination
$\vec{w} = c_1\vec{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\vec{v}_k$. We write $V = \spn\{\vec{v}_1,
\dots,\vec{v}_k\}$ and call $\{\vec{v}_1,\dots,\vec{v}_k\}$ a spanning set for $V$.
\end{definition}

Spanning says: \emph{no vector in $V$ is out of reach}. In matrix language, put
$\vec{v}_1,\dots,\vec{v}_k\in\R^n$ in the columns of $\mat{A}$. They span $\R^n$ if and only if $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$
has a solution for \emph{every} $\vec{b}\in\R^n$. Equivalently, $\col(\mat{A})
= \R^n$, which (Chapter~13) happens exactly when $\mat{A}$ has a pivot in every
\emph{row}.

\begin{focusbox}
Two questions, two pivot patterns:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Independence} (no redundant vectors) $\iff$ a pivot in every
        \emph{column} (no free variables).
  \item \textbf{Spanning} $\R^n$ (no missing directions) $\iff$ a pivot in every
        \emph{row} (no unsolvable $\vec{b}$).
\end{itemize}
A basis of $\R^n$ needs both at once: a pivot in every row \emph{and} every column.
That is possible only when $\mat{A}$ is square ($k=n$) with $n$ pivots.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}[Spanning but not independent]
\label{ch14:ex-span-not-indep}
In $\R^2$ the three vectors $\colvec{1\\0},\ \colvec{0\\1},\ \colvec{1\\1}$ span
$\R^2$, because the first two alone already reach everything. But we saw they are
dependent. So a spanning set may contain redundant vectors. Going the other way,
$\colvec{1\\0}$ alone is independent but spans only the $x$-axis, not all of
$\R^2$. A basis sits between these: it spans the entire space without
containing any redundant vectors.
\end{example}

Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-pivotshape} shows how the shape of $\mat{A}$ limits where
those pivots can sit. Write $m$ for the number of rows and $n$ for the number of columns.
Each row and each column holds at most one pivot, so there are at most
$\min(m,n)$ pivots.

\section{Basis: independent and spanning}
\label{ch14:basis}

\begin{definition}[Basis]
\label{ch14:def-basis}
A \textbf{basis} of a vector space $V$ is a list of vectors that is
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item linearly independent, and
  \item spans $V$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Independent, so nothing is redundant; spanning, so nothing is missing.
Drop any vector from a basis and it no longer spans; add any vector to it and it is no
longer independent. Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-venn} pictures the two conditions as overlapping
regions.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\begin{intuitionbox}
A basis is a \emph{coordinate frame} for the space: a set of independent
directions such that every point has \emph{exactly one} address (list of coefficients). Independence
makes the address unique (no two combinations give the same vector);
spanning makes sure every point has an address at all. A basis connects
abstract vectors to the lists of numbers you compute with.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{example}[The standard basis of $\R^n$]
\label{ch14:ex-standard}
The vectors
\[
  \vec{e}_1=\colvec{1\\0\\0},\quad
  \vec{e}_2=\colvec{0\\1\\0},\quad
  \vec{e}_3=\colvec{0\\0\\1}
\]
form the \textbf{standard basis} of $\R^3$. They span (any $\vec{w} = w_1\vec{e}_1
+ w_2\vec{e}_2 + w_3\vec{e}_3$) and they are independent (as columns they form the
identity matrix, which has three pivots). The coordinates of $\vec{w}$ in this basis are
its ordinary entries, which is why we usually do not notice we are using a
basis.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A non-standard basis of $\R^2$]
\label{ch14:ex-nonstandard}
Consider $\vec{b}_1 = \colvec{1\\1}$, $\vec{b}_2 = \colvec{1\\-1}$. As the columns of a matrix,
\[
  \mat{B}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{R_2-R_1}
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 0 & -2\end{bmatrix},
\]
two pivots in a $2\times 2$ matrix: independent and spanning, hence a basis. It
is a valid coordinate frame for the plane, with axes tilted by $45^\circ$ and
stretched compared with the standard one. Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-frames} compares the
two frames; Section~\ref{ch14:coords} computes the coordinates shown there.
\end{example}

\section{Dimension is well-defined}
\label{ch14:dim}

A space has many different bases (we just saw two for
$\R^2$). But \emph{every basis of a given space has the same number of vectors}.
That common number gets a name.

\begin{definition}[Dimension]
\label{ch14:def-dim}
The \textbf{dimension} of a vector space $V$, written $\dimn V$, is the number of
vectors in any basis of $V$. By Theorem~\ref{ch14:thm-dim} below this number does not depend on
which basis you pick.
\end{definition}

The definition would make no sense if two bases could have different sizes.
The proof that they cannot is usually credited to Ernst Steinitz, whose original
argument swaps spanning vectors for independent ones one at a time (hence the name
\emph{exchange lemma}). We give a shorter proof that reuses
Theorem~\ref{ch14:thm-toomany}.

\begin{lemma}[Independent $\le$ spanning]
\label{ch14:lem-steinitz}
If $\vec{u}_1,\dots,\vec{u}_p$ are linearly independent in $V$, and
$\vec{w}_1,\dots,\vec{w}_q$ span $V$, then $p \le q$. In words: you can never
have \emph{more} independent vectors than you have spanning vectors.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Because the $\vec{w}_j$ span $V$, each $\vec{u}_i$ is a combination of
them. Write $a_{ji}$ for the coefficient of $\vec{w}_j$ in the recipe for $\vec{u}_i$:
\[
  \vec{u}_i = a_{1i}\vec{w}_1 + a_{2i}\vec{w}_2 + \cdots + a_{qi}\vec{w}_q
  = \sum_{j=1}^{q} a_{ji}\,\vec{w}_j ,
\]
where $\sum_{j=1}^{q}$ means ``add up the terms for $j=1,2,\dots,q$''. Collect these coefficients
into a $q \times p$ matrix $\mat{A}$ whose entry in row $j$, column $i$ is $a_{ji}$; column $i$ holds
the recipe for $\vec{u}_i$. Now suppose, for contradiction,
that $p > q$. Then $\mat{A}$ has more columns than rows, so by
Theorem~\ref{ch14:thm-toomany} (applied to its columns) the system
$\mat{A}\vec{c}=\vec{0}$ has a nonzero solution $\vec{c}=(c_1,\dots,c_p)$. Use the
same numbers $c_i$ to combine the $\vec{u}_i$, and regroup by $\vec{w}_j$:
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{i=1}^{p} c_i \vec{u}_i
  &= \sum_{i=1}^{p} c_i \sum_{j=1}^{q} a_{ji}\vec{w}_j
     && \text{(substitute each } \vec{u}_i\text{)}\\
  &= \sum_{j=1}^{q}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{p} a_{ji} c_i\Bigr)\vec{w}_j
     && \text{(collect the terms with the same } \vec{w}_j\text{)}\\
  &= \sum_{j=1}^{q} 0\cdot\vec{w}_j = \vec{0},
     && \text{(the bracket is entry } j \text{ of } \mat{A}\vec{c}=\vec{0}\text{)}
\end{align*}
So a nonzero
$\vec{c}$ gives a nontrivial combination of the $\vec{u}_i$ equal to $\vec{0}$,
contradicting their independence. Hence $p \le q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Dimension is well-defined]
\label{ch14:thm-dim}
Any two bases of a vector space $V$ have the same number of vectors.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal B = \{\vec{b}_1,\dots,\vec{b}_p\}$ and $\mathcal C =
\{\vec{c}_1,\dots,\vec{c}_q\}$ both be bases of $V$ (the curly letters $\mathcal B$,
$\mathcal C$ are names for the two lists). Then $\mathcal B$ is
independent and $\mathcal C$ spans, so Lemma~\ref{ch14:lem-steinitz} gives $p \le
q$. Swapping the roles ($\mathcal C$ independent, $\mathcal B$ spanning) gives $q \le p$.
Therefore $p = q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{aestheticbox}
A subspace (other than $\{\vec{0}\}$) contains infinitely many vectors and has infinitely
many different bases, yet every basis contains the same number of vectors.
So dimension belongs to the subspace itself, not to whichever basis you
happen to choose. This one whole number controls whether linear systems can be solved,
the rank of a matrix, and the size of its nullspace.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{historybox}
The idea of dimension seems obvious in daily life: a line is one-dimensional, a sheet of paper is two-dimensional, and a room is three-dimensional. But what gives a mathematical space an \emph{intrinsic} dimension, independent of any coordinate grid you happen to draw on it?

In 1844, the German schoolteacher Hermann Grassmann published \emph{Die Lineare Ausdehnungslehre} (The Theory of Linear Extension). At the time, mathematicians were stuck believing that geometry required fixing an arbitrary set of coordinate axes like $x, y, z$. Without an external grid, they had no mathematical vocabulary to describe lines, planes, and higher-dimensional spaces. Armed only with Euclidean geometry and early algebra, Grassmann worked without fixed coordinate axes. He invented linear combinations, linear independence, and spanning sets, showing that an $n$-dimensional space could be understood purely through its internal directions. Because Grassmann wrote in dense, metaphysical prose, the mathematical establishment, including Carl Friedrich Gauss and August Möbius, ignored or rejected the work for over thirty years. Only in 1888 did Italian mathematician Giuseppe Peano distill Grassmann's ideas into the modern axiomatic definition of a vector space (a set where vectors can be added and scaled according to clean algebraic rules).

In 1910, Ernst Steinitz published his memoir \emph{Algebraische Theorie der Körper} (Algebraic Theory of Fields---the study of number systems where addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division are well-behaved). Steinitz proved the exchange lemma (Lemma~\ref{ch14:lem-steinitz}), establishing once and for all that no matter how two people choose a basis for the same finite-dimensional space, they must always count the exact same number of vectors. Steinitz established dimension as a structural invariant of finite-dimensional vector spaces.

Extending bases to infinite-dimensional spaces---such as $C[0,1]$, the space of continuous functions on an interval (curves drawn without lifting your pencil)---raised a subtler question. In 1905, Georg Hamel defined a basis using strictly finite sums of basis elements (a \emph{Hamel basis}). Proving that every vector space has a Hamel basis requires the controversial Axiom of Choice---the mathematical assumption that one can make infinitely many arbitrary selections without giving an explicit rule. The resulting Hamel bases for function spaces are enormous, and not a single concrete example can ever be written down. To escape this non-constructive trap, Polish mathematicians Juliusz Schauder and Stefan Banach in 1927 introduced \emph{Schauder bases}, which allow infinite convergent series (building blocks that approximate any function more and more closely, like Fourier series).

Banach posed a challenge in his notebook at the Scottish Café in Lwów: does \emph{every} reasonable infinite-dimensional space admit a Schauder basis? This ``Basis Problem'' resisted all attempts for over forty years, until Swedish mathematician Per Enflo solved it negatively in 1972 by constructing a space with no Schauder basis at all (winning the live goose promised by Stanisław Mazur in 1936). Today, the active frontier has shifted to computation: in machine learning and data compression, researchers work on \emph{dictionary learning}, searching for algorithms that find, for a given collection of images or signals, a set of building blocks in which each item needs only a few nonzero coefficients (a \emph{sparse} representation).
\end{historybox}

\subsection*{Counting consequences}

\begin{corollary}[Counting shortcuts]
\label{ch14:cor-count}
In an $n$-dimensional space:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Any independent list has at most $n$ vectors; a list of more than $n$ vectors is
        dependent.
  \item Any spanning list has at least $n$ vectors; fewer than $n$ cannot span.
  \item Any \emph{independent list of exactly $n$ vectors is automatically a
        basis} (it must already span).
  \item Any \emph{spanning list of exactly $n$ vectors is automatically a basis}
        (it must already be independent).
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

(A \emph{corollary} is a result that follows quickly from a theorem just proved; here,
parts (a) and (b) are Lemma~\ref{ch14:lem-steinitz} with a basis of $n$ vectors
playing one of the two roles.)
Parts (c) and (d) save work: in an $n$-dimensional space, once a
list has exactly $n$ vectors, checking one of the two conditions is enough.

\begin{example}[Using the count]
\label{ch14:ex-count}
Are $\colvec{2\\1\\0},\colvec{0\\3\\1},\colvec{1\\0\\2}$ a basis of $\R^3$? There
are exactly three of them in a $3$-dimensional space, so by
Corollary~\ref{ch14:cor-count}(c) it is enough to check independence. Eliminate:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1\\ 1 & 3 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{R_2-\frac12 R_1}
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1\\ 0 & 3 & -\frac12\\ 0 & 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{R_3-\frac13 R_2}
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1\\ 0 & 3 & -\frac12\\ 0 & 0 & \frac{13}{6}\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Three pivots, so independent, so (no spanning check needed) a basis.
\end{example}

\begin{walkbox}
Convince yourself geometrically of each of the four parts of
Corollary~\ref{ch14:cor-count} using the principle from
Theorem~\ref{ch14:thm-toomany}: a space cannot contain more independent
directions than its dimension, and fewer vectors cannot span the space. Which
inequality in Lemma~\ref{ch14:lem-steinitz} establishes this balance?
\end{walkbox}

\section{Coordinates in a basis}
\label{ch14:coords}

Because a basis is linearly independent and spans $V$, every vector has a unique
representation as a linear combination of basis vectors. The coefficients in
this representation form its \textbf{coordinates}.

\begin{definition}[Coordinates]
\label{ch14:def-coords}
Let $\mathcal B = \{\vec{b}_1,\dots,\vec{b}_n\}$ be a basis of $V$. For $\vec{w}
\in V$ there are unique scalars $c_1,\dots,c_n$ with $\vec{w} = c_1\vec{b}_1 +
\cdots + c_n\vec{b}_n$. The vector
\[
  [\vec{w}]_{\mathcal B} = \colvec{c_1\\\vdots\\c_n}
\]
is the \textbf{coordinate vector} of $\vec{w}$ with respect to $\mathcal B$.
The order of the basis vectors matters here: $c_1$ goes with $\vec{b}_1$, and so on.
\end{definition}

Uniqueness is the independence argument from Section~\ref{ch14:def}: two
addresses for the same point would subtract to a nontrivial dependence, which a
basis forbids. So coordinates are well-defined, and finding them means solving a
square system.

\begin{example}[Coordinates in the frame $\{\vec{b}_1,\vec{b}_2\}$]
\label{ch14:ex-coords}
Use $\vec{b}_1 = \colvec{1\\1}$, $\vec{b}_2 = \colvec{1\\-1}$ from
Example~\ref{ch14:ex-nonstandard}, and find the coordinates of $\vec{w} =
\colvec{2\\3}$. We need $c_1\vec{b}_1 + c_2\vec{b}_2 = \vec{w}$, i.e.
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}\colvec{c_1\\c_2} = \colvec{2\\3}.
\]
The equations are $c_1 + c_2 = 2$ and $c_1 - c_2 = 3$.
Adding them gives $2c_1 = 5$, so $c_1 = \tfrac52$. Subtracting the second from the first gives
$2c_2 = -1$, so $c_2 = -\tfrac12$. So
\[
  [\vec{w}]_{\mathcal B} = \colvec{\tfrac52\\[2pt]-\tfrac12},
  \qquad\text{meaning}\qquad
  \tfrac52\colvec{1\\1} - \tfrac12\colvec{1\\-1} = \colvec{2\\3}.\ \checkmark
\]
The point $\vec{w}$ never moved. In the standard frame its address is $(2,3)$; in
the $\mathcal B$ frame its address is $(\tfrac52,-\tfrac12)$
(Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-frames}).
\end{example}

\begin{focusbox}
To find coordinates, put the basis vectors in the columns of $\mat{B}$ and solve
$\mat{B}\,[\vec{w}]_{\mathcal B} = \vec{w}$. Because $\mathcal B$ is a basis,
$\mat{B}$ is square with a full set of pivots, hence invertible, so $[\vec{w}]_{\mathcal B} = \mat{B}\inv\vec{w}$ and the
answer is unique. Converting standard coordinates into $\mathcal B$-coordinates (a
\emph{change of basis}) is multiplication by $\mat{B}\inv$; Chapter~31 develops this fully.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The same idea drives \emph{Fourier series} (Chapter~1) and \emph{wavelets}. There, the
``vectors'' are functions such as sound signals, and the basis is a family of sine and cosine waves (or,
for wavelets, short localized bumps). The Fourier coefficients of a signal are its
\emph{coordinates} in that basis, in the sense of Definition~\ref{ch14:def-coords},
but in a space where a basis needs infinitely many vectors. The sines and cosines are
perpendicular to one another in a sense made precise in Chapter~37, and that is
what lets each coefficient be computed by a single integral instead of by solving
a system $\mat{B}\vec{c}=\vec{w}$. JPEG image compression uses a finite version,
the \emph{discrete cosine transform}: it rewrites each $8\times 8$ block of pixel
values (a vector with $64$ entries) in coordinates with respect to a basis of $64$
cosine patterns, then stores the important coordinates and drops the tiny ones.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{A basis for the column space}
\label{ch14:colbasis}

Now we apply the theory to the two subspaces met in Chapter~12. There we introduced the column space $\col(\mat{A})$ as the span
of the columns of $\mat{A}$: every output $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ the matrix can produce.
That chapter also used the pivot columns to pick out the non-redundant directions.

The columns of $\mat{A}$ span $\col(\mat{A})$ by definition, but if some columns
are combinations of earlier ones, the set contains redundant vectors. To reduce
that spanning set to a basis, remove the redundant vectors while keeping
the span. Elimination tells us which columns to keep.

\begin{theorem}[Pivot columns are a basis for $\col(\mat{A})$]
\label{ch14:thm-colbasis}
The pivot columns of $\mat{A}$ --- the columns of the \emph{original} matrix in
the positions where its row-reduced form has pivots --- form a basis for
$\col(\mat{A})$. Hence $\dimn \col(\mat{A})$ equals the number of pivots, which is
the rank of $\mat{A}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Why it works]
Row operations do not change which combinations of columns give zero: if $\mat{R}$
is the reduced form of $\mat{A}$, then $\mat{A}\vec{c}=\vec{0}$ and
$\mat{R}\vec{c}=\vec{0}$ have the same solutions $\vec{c}$. In $\mat{R}$ the
pivot columns are independent (each has a pivot in a row where the other pivot
columns have $0$), and each free column is a combination of earlier pivot columns. The same
combinations $\vec{c}$ give zero among the columns of $\mat{A}$, so the
pivot columns of $\mat{A}$ are independent, and the free columns of $\mat{A}$ are
combinations of them. So the pivot columns are independent and span the column space: a basis. (A
common mistake is discussed in the box below.)
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Reading a column-space basis]
\label{ch14:ex-colbasis}
Let
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 3\\ 2 & 4 & 3 & 7\\ 1 & 2 & 2 & 4\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Eliminate. Subtract $2R_1$ from $R_2$ and $R_1$ from $R_3$:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 3\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{R_3-R_2}
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 3\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Pivots sit in columns $1$ and $3$. So a basis for $\col(\mat{A})$ is
\[
  \left\{\ \colvec{1\\2\\1},\ \colvec{1\\3\\2}\ \right\}
  \qquad(\text{columns } 1 \text{ and } 3 \text{ of the \emph{original} } \mat{A}),
\]
and $\dimn\col(\mat{A}) = \rank(\mat{A}) = 2$. The column space is a plane in
$\R^3$. Columns $2$ and $4$ were redundant: column $2 = 2\times$ column $1$,
and you can check column $4 = 2(\text{col }1) + (\text{col }3)$.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
Take the pivot columns from the \textbf{original} $\mat{A}$, \emph{never} the
columns of the row-reduced $\mat{R}$. Row operations keep the column space's
\emph{dimension} and the \emph{dependence pattern} (which columns are combinations of which), but they change the actual
column vectors, so $\col(\mat{R})$ is usually a different subspace from
$\col(\mat{A})$. In Example~\ref{ch14:ex-colbasis}, $\mat{R}$'s columns all have a
$0$ in the last slot, so $\col(\mat{R})$ is the $xy$-plane; but $\col(\mat{A})$ is
a \emph{tilted} plane. Same dimension, different plane. Use $\mat{A}$'s columns.
\end{trapbox}

Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-tilted} draws the two planes. Both are $2$-dimensional, but they sit in $\R^3$ at different tilts: the
column space of $\mat{A}$ is spanned by the two pivot columns
$\colvec{1\\2\\1}$ and $\colvec{1\\3\\2}$, while the columns of $\mat{R}$ lie flat
on the floor $z=0$.

\section{A basis for the nullspace}
\label{ch14:nullbasis}

The nullspace $\nullsp(\mat{A})$ is the set of all vectors $\vec{x}$ with $\mat{A}\vec{x}
= \vec{0}$: the coefficients of every combination of columns that adds up to zero. In Chapters~12 and~13,
you solved this system by identifying free variables and producing the
\textbf{special solutions}: set one free variable to $1$, set the remaining free
variables to $0$, and solve for the pivot variables.

The special solutions form a basis for $\nullsp(\mat{A})$.

\begin{theorem}[Special solutions are a basis for $\nullsp(\mat{A})$]
\label{ch14:thm-nullbasis}
If $\mat{A}$ has $r$ pivots and $f$ free columns (so the number of columns is $n =
r + f$), then the $f$ special solutions form a basis for $\nullsp(\mat{A})$. Hence
\[
  \dimn\nullsp(\mat{A}) = f = n - r = (\#\text{columns}) - \rank(\mat{A}).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Why it works]
Call the special solutions $\vec{s}_1,\dots,\vec{s}_f$, where $\vec{s}_i$ is the one
with the $i$-th free variable set to $1$.
\emph{They span.} Every solution of $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{0}$ is determined by its
free-variable values, and it equals the combination of the
special solutions weighted by those free values; that is how they were built.
\emph{They are independent.} Look at the free-variable slots: special solution $i$
has a $1$ in free slot $i$ and $0$ in the other free slots. If a combination
$c_1\vec{s}_1 + \cdots + c_f\vec{s}_f = \vec{0}$, then reading off free slot $i$ gives $c_i = 0$.
So all $c_i$ are zero: independent. Independent and spanning the nullspace: a basis.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Reading a nullspace basis]
\label{ch14:ex-nullbasis}
Reuse $\mat{A}$ from Example~\ref{ch14:ex-colbasis}. One more step, $R_1 - R_2$,
clears the entry above the second pivot and gives the reduced form
\[
  \mat{R}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 2\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Pivot variables: $x_1, x_3$. Free variables: $x_2, x_4$. The equations are
\[
  x_1 + 2x_2 + 2x_4 = 0,\qquad x_3 + x_4 = 0.
\]
\textbf{Special solution 1} ($x_2=1,\ x_4=0$): $x_3 = 0$, $x_1 = -2$, giving
$\vec{s}_1 = (-2,1,0,0)$.
\textbf{Special solution 2} ($x_2=0,\ x_4=1$): $x_3 = -1$, $x_1 = -2$, giving
$\vec{s}_2 = (-2,0,-1,1)$. So
\[
  \nullsp(\mat{A}) = \spn\!\left\{\ \colvec{-2\\1\\0\\0},\ \colvec{-2\\0\\-1\\1}\ \right\},
  \qquad \dimn\nullsp(\mat{A}) = 2.
\]
Check the count: $n = 4$ columns, $r = 2$ pivots, so $f = n - r = 2$
free variables, matching the two basis vectors we found. And $2 + 2 = 4$: the
dimension of the column space plus the dimension of the nullspace equals the
number of columns. That balance is the \emph{Rank--Nullity Theorem}, which
Chapter~16 proves for every matrix.
\end{example}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Why data scientists count dimensions.} A data matrix $\mat{X}$ stores one
example per row and one feature (measured quantity) per column. If some features are linearly dependent,
say \texttt{height\_cm} and \texttt{height\_in}, or
\texttt{revenue}, \texttt{cost}, and \texttt{profit} $=$ \texttt{revenue} $-$
\texttt{cost}, then those columns are redundant and $\rank(\mat{X})$ is
less than the number of features. \emph{Linear regression} looks for weights $\vec{w}$
so that $\mat{X}\vec{w}$ is as close as possible to the observed outcomes $\vec{y}$. Redundant
columns make the normal equations (the system $\mat{X}\T\mat{X}\vec{w} = \mat{X}\T\vec{y}$ used to compute the best-fit weights) \emph{singular} (the matrix $\mat{X}\T\mat{X}$ is not invertible): adding any vector from $\nullsp(\mat{X})$ to $\vec{w}$ gives the same predictions, so the ``best weights'' are not unique. When features are only nearly dependent, as real measurements usually are, the computed weights can swing wildly with tiny changes in the data.

The fix comes from this chapter: find a \emph{basis} for the column space of $\mat{X}$ (pivot columns, which form a largest independent set of features), keep those features, and drop the rest. The number of independent features is $\rank(\mat{X})$, and the special solutions in $\nullsp(\mat{X})$ record how each dropped feature is built from the kept ones. Chapters~29 and~30 (the singular value decomposition and principal component analysis) handle the nearly dependent case.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Bases in other vector spaces}
\label{ch14:other}

Nothing in Definition~\ref{ch14:def-basis} is restricted to $\R^n$. Independence,
spanning, basis, and dimension make sense in \emph{any} vector space.

\begin{example}[Polynomials of degree $\le 2$]
\label{ch14:ex-poly}
Let $V = \mathcal P_2$, the polynomials $a + bx + cx^2$ (any real numbers $a,b,c$; ``degree $\le 2$'' means
no power of $x$ higher than $x^2$). A natural basis is
$\{1,\, x,\, x^2\}$: any polynomial is a unique combination of them (spanning $+$
independence), so $\dimn \mathcal P_2 = 3$. The coordinate vector of $3 - 5x +
2x^2$ in this basis is $(3,-5,2)$. A different basis gives different coordinates: in the
basis $\{1,\, x-1,\, (x-1)^2\}$, which measures everything from the point $x=1$, the same polynomial is
\[
  3 - 5x + 2x^2 = 0\cdot 1 - 1\cdot(x-1) + 2\,(x-1)^2,
\]
so its coordinates are $(0,-1,2)$. (Expand the right side to check.) Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-poly}
shows the first combination as graphs. Once a basis is chosen, each polynomial in $\mathcal P_2$
corresponds to exactly one vector in $\R^3$ and back; the spaces are
isomorphic (Chapter~11). So questions about polynomials can be answered with
matrix computations in $\R^3$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A two-dimensional space of matrices]
\label{ch14:ex-matrices}
The symmetric $2\times 2$ matrices with trace zero (trace $=$ sum of the diagonal entries, Chapter~8), $\begin{bmatrix} a & b\\ b &
-a\end{bmatrix}$, form a vector space. A basis is
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
  \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix},
\]
since every such matrix is $a$ times the first plus $b$ times the second, uniquely.
Dimension $2$. (In quantum physics these two matrices are two of the three \emph{Pauli matrices},
which describe the spin of an electron.)
\end{example}

\begin{focusbox}
Whenever you can add and scale (arrows, polynomials,
matrices, functions, signals), you can ask ``what is a basis?'' and ``what is the
dimension?'' The \emph{answers} differ, but the questions, and the
elimination-style tools for answering them, are the ones in this chapter.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Putting it together}
\label{ch14:together}

\begin{recoverybox}
A quick self-check before the exercises. Make sure you can explain each line:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Independent} $=$ no redundant vector $=$ only the trivial
        combination gives $\vec{0}$ $=$ a pivot in every column $=$
        $\nullsp(\mat{A}) = \{\vec{0}\}$.
  \item \textbf{Spans $\R^n$} $=$ reaches every $\vec{b}$ $=$ a pivot in every row.
  \item \textbf{Basis} $=$ independent $+$ spanning $=$ a spanning list with nothing to remove $=$
        an independent list with no room to add $=$ unique coordinates for every vector.
  \item \textbf{Dimension} $=$ size of any basis (the same for all bases) $=$ number of
        pivots, for a column space.
  \item \textbf{Column-space basis} $=$ pivot columns of the \emph{original}
        $\mat{A}$. \textbf{Nullspace basis} $=$ the special solutions.
\end{itemize}
\end{recoverybox}

Elimination on $\mat{A}$ splits its columns into pivot columns and free columns
(Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-pipeline}).
The pivot columns, taken from the original matrix, are a basis for the column
space; their count is the rank $r$. Each free column gives one special
solution, and those are a basis for the nullspace; their count is $n - r$. So a single row
reduction gives bases and dimensions for both subspaces.
In the next chapter, these subspaces and the matching pair for $\mat{A}\T$
form the four fundamental subspaces.

\begin{connectionbox}
We can now find the size of any subspace ($\dimn V$), and we know that every vector has exactly one list of coordinates relative to any chosen basis. The pivot columns of $\mat{A}$ give an explicit basis for $\col(\mat{A})$, and the special solutions give an explicit basis for $\nullsp(\mat{A})$.

However, a general basis can be badly skewed. If two basis vectors point in nearly the same direction, computing coordinates requires inverting an ill-conditioned matrix (a matrix where tiny errors in the input blow up into huge errors in the output). A general basis also says nothing about right angles, distances, or the closest point in a subspace. In Chapters~15 and~16, we see how the dimensions of the four fundamental subspaces fit together in the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra. Then, in Part~IV (Chapters 17--20), we fix the problem of skewed bases by building orthonormal bases (coordinate frames where every vector has length $1$ and is perpendicular to all the others), using methods called the Gram--Schmidt process and the QR factorization, which are explained there.
\end{connectionbox}

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Warmup: parallel or not]
\label{ch14:exr-parallel}
Decide whether each pair is independent. (a) $\colvec{2\\-1},\colvec{-6\\3}$.
(b) $\colvec{1\\2},\colvec{2\\1}$. (c) $\colvec{0\\0},\colvec{4\\7}$. For each,
state the reason in one sentence using the word ``parallel'' or ``zero.''
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Run the test]
\label{ch14:exr-test}
Use elimination to decide whether $\colvec{1\\0\\2},\colvec{2\\1\\1},
\colvec{4\\1\\5}$ are independent. If they are dependent, find an explicit
nontrivial combination equal to $\vec{0}$ and verify it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Count first, then check]
\label{ch14:exr-count}
You are handed four vectors in $\R^3$. Without any computation, what can you
conclude about their independence, and which theorem says so? Now: are five
vectors enough to \emph{span} $\R^3$? Could three? Could two? Justify each with
Corollary~\ref{ch14:cor-count}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Build a basis by trimming]
\label{ch14:exr-trim}
The set $\colvec{1\\1\\0},\colvec{2\\2\\0},\colvec{0\\1\\1},\colvec{1\\0\\-1}$
spans some subspace of $\R^3$. Find a basis for that subspace by selecting pivot
columns, and state its dimension. Is the subspace all of $\R^3$, a plane, or a
line?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Coordinates]
\label{ch14:exr-coords}
With the basis $\vec{b}_1=\colvec{2\\1},\vec{b}_2=\colvec{1\\3}$ of $\R^2$, find
$[\vec{w}]_{\mathcal B}$ for $\vec{w}=\colvec{5\\5}$. Then find the vector whose
$\mathcal B$-coordinates are $(1,-1)$. Sketch both in the $\mathcal B$ frame.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Column space and nullspace together]
\label{ch14:exr-both}
For
\[
  \mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 1 & 4\\ 2 & 6 & 1 & 7\\ 1 & 3 & 0 & 3\end{bmatrix},
\]
find a basis for $\col(\mat{A})$ and a basis for $\nullsp(\mat{A})$. Verify that
$\dimn\col(\mat{A}) + \dimn\nullsp(\mat{A})$ equals the number of columns.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A basis outside $\R^n$]
\label{ch14:exr-poly}
Show that $\{1+x,\ 1-x,\ x^2\}$ is a basis of $\mathcal P_2$ (polynomials of
degree $\le 2$). Then find the coordinates of $p(x) = 4 + 2x + 3x^2$ in this
basis.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Extend to a basis]
\label{ch14:exr-extend}
The vectors $\colvec{1\\1\\1}$ and $\colvec{1\\0\\-1}$ are independent in $\R^3$.
Find a third vector that makes them a basis of $\R^3$, and prove your choice
works. (Hint: you only need to add one direction not already in their span; the
count then guarantees a basis.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: a spanning set can be shrunk to a basis]
\label{ch14:exr-shrink}
Suppose $\vec{v}_1,\dots,\vec{v}_k$ span a space $V$ but are dependent. Prove that
some $\vec{v}_i$ is a combination of the others, and that deleting it leaves a set
that still spans $V$. (This is the trimming process; iterating it reaches a
basis.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: independence and uniqueness are the same thing]
\label{ch14:exr-unique}
Prove that $\vec{v}_1,\dots,\vec{v}_k$ are linearly independent if and only if
every vector in $\spn\{\vec{v}_1,\dots,\vec{v}_k\}$ has \emph{exactly one}
expression as a combination $c_1\vec{v}_1+\cdots+c_k\vec{v}_k$. (One direction is
the argument in Section~\ref{ch14:def}; supply both.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Challenge: dimension by two routes]
\label{ch14:exr-challenge}
Let $W = \spn\{\vec{u}_1,\vec{u}_2,\vec{u}_3\}$ where $\vec{u}_1=\colvec{1\\2\\0\\1},
\vec{u}_2=\colvec{2\\4\\1\\3},\vec{u}_3=\colvec{0\\0\\1\\1}$ in $\R^4$. (a) Find
$\dimn W$ by selecting pivot columns. (b) Independently, write $W$ as the
nullspace of some matrix --- i.e. find the linear equations cutting out $W$ ---
and recompute $\dimn W$ from that description as $4 - (\text{number of independent
equations})$. Confirm the two answers agree, and explain in a sentence why they
must.
\end{exercise}
