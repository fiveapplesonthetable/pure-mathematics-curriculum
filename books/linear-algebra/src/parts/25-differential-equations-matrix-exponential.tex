\chapter{Differential Equations and the Matrix Exponential}

In the previous chapter, diagonalization split the discrete matrix steps
$\vec{u}_{k+1} = \mat{A}\vec{u}_k$ into independent pieces, one along each
eigenvector. This chapter asks the same question in continuous time: what
happens when the state changes smoothly instead of in separate steps?

Start from one sentence: \emph{the rate of change of a quantity is proportional
to the quantity itself.} An equation that ties a quantity to its own rate of
change is called a \textbf{differential equation}. This one describes the
temperature gap between a cup of coffee and the room as the coffee cools, the
size of a growing population, and the amount left in a decaying radioactive
sample. Its solution is the exponential function.

Linear algebra enters because real systems usually track more than one quantity.
A predator population depends on a prey population, which depends back on the
predator. A mixing tank's salt concentration depends on its neighbor's. A
circuit's voltages and currents push on each other. The position and velocity of
a vibrating mass feed each other. In every one of these, you have a \emph{vector}
of quantities $\vec{u}(t)$, and the rule for how it changes is a \emph{matrix}:
\[
  \frac{d\vec{u}}{dt} = \mat{A}\,\vec{u}.
\]
Here $\vec{u}(t)$ is the list of quantities at time $t$, $d\vec{u}/dt$ is the
list of their rates of change, and $\mat{A}$ is a fixed square matrix that says
how each rate depends on the current values.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors solve this equation. Each eigenvector gives a
\textbf{mode} of the system: a motion in which the state stays on one fixed
line through the origin and only its size changes. The matching eigenvalue is
that mode's growth rate (and, when it is complex, also its oscillation
frequency). Whether the system settles to rest, blows up, or oscillates
forever is called its \textbf{stability}, and it can be read from the signs
of the eigenvalues.

All of this is packaged into one matrix, the \textbf{matrix exponential}
$e^{\mat{A}t}$. It is the matrix version of the scalar $e^{at}$: multiply any
starting state by $e^{\mat{A}t}$ and you get the state at time $t$.

\section{The One-Dimensional Story First}
\label{ch25:scalar}

We start with a single quantity, because every later formula is built from
this case.

Suppose a single number $u(t)$ changes at a rate proportional to its current
size:
\[
  \frac{du}{dt} = a\,u, \qquad u(0) = u_0.
\]
Here $a$ is a fixed number and $u_0$ is the starting value. In words: the
bigger $u$ is, the faster it grows (if $a > 0$) or the faster it shrinks (if
$a < 0$). The function with this property is the exponential, and we can
derive it instead of guessing. \textbf{Separate the variables} (move every $u$
to one side and every $t$ to the other), then integrate both sides:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{du}{u} &= a\,dt,\\
  \int \frac{du}{u} &= \int a\,dt,\\
  \ln|u| &= a t + C,
\end{align*}
where $C$ is the constant of integration. Exponentiating gives
$|u| = e^{C}e^{at}$, so $u = K e^{at}$ for some constant $K$. At $t=0$ this
reads $u(0) = K$, so $K = u_0$:
\[
  \boxed{\,u(t) = u_0\, e^{a t}.\,}
\]

\begin{intuitionbox}
The number $a$ is a \textbf{growth rate}. If $a > 0$, $u$ runs away to infinity.
If $a < 0$, $u$ decays to zero. If $a = 0$, $u$ stays at $u_0$ forever. The
sign of $a$ decides the long-term behavior, and the size $|a|$ sets how fast it
happens (Figure~\ref{ch25:fig-scalar}).
In the matrix case, the sign of the real part of each eigenvalue decides
whether its mode grows or decays.
\end{intuitionbox}

There is a second way to write $e^{at}$, and it is the one that carries over to
matrices. A \textbf{power series} is an ``infinite polynomial''
$c_0 + c_1 t + c_2 t^2 + \cdots$. Ask: which power series, when you
differentiate it, gives back itself times $a$? (For a refresher on Taylor series
and term-by-term differentiation, see Section~\ref{ch39:calculus} in the
Chapter~39 appendix.) Try
\[
  e^{at} = 1 + at + \frac{(at)^2}{2!} + \frac{(at)^3}{3!} + \cdots
         = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(at)^k}{k!}.
\]
Differentiate term by term:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{d}{dt}\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{a^k t^k}{k!}\right]
  &= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{a^k\, k\, t^{k-1}}{k!}
     && \text{(the $k=0$ term is constant)}\\
  &= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{a^k t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}
     && \text{(since $k/k! = 1/(k-1)!$)}\\
  &= a\sum_{j=0}^\infty \frac{a^j t^{j}}{j!}
     && \text{(set $j=k-1$)}\\
  &= a\, e^{at}.
\end{align*}
So the power series satisfies $\frac{d}{dt}e^{at} = a\,e^{at}$ with
$e^{a\cdot 0} = 1$: it is the solution. When $a$ becomes a matrix $\mat{A}$, we
cannot separate variables anymore (we cannot divide by a vector), but we can
still write down the power series, and it will still differentiate to itself
times $\mat{A}$. That series is the matrix exponential.

\begin{focusbox}
Two faces of the same function:
\[
  \frac{du}{dt} = a u,\ u(0)=u_0
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  u(t) = u_0 e^{at} = u_0\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(at)^k}{k!}.
\]
The differential equation states the rule; the exponential is the function
that obeys it. The rest of the chapter replaces $a$ by a matrix and keeps both
forms.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think (mental check):} If a quantity decays according to
$\frac{du}{dt} = -3u$ with initial size $u(0) = 8$, what is $u(t)$? At what time
does the quantity drop to $1$?
\emph{Check:} $u(t) = 8e^{-3t}$. Setting $8e^{-3t} = 1$ gives $e^{3t} = 8$, so
$t = \frac{\ln 8}{3} = \ln 2 \approx 0.693$.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Coupled Equations: Why a Matrix Appears}
\label{ch25:coupled}

Now let two quantities interact. Here is the smallest example.

\begin{example}[Two tanks trading salt]
\label{ch25:tanks}
Two tanks hold brine (salt water). Salt flows between them: tank~1 loses salt to tank~2 and
gains some back. Suppose the amounts $u_1(t)$ and $u_2(t)$ obey
\[
  \frac{du_1}{dt} = -2u_1 + u_2, \qquad
  \frac{du_2}{dt} = \phantom{-}u_1 - 2u_2.
\]
You cannot solve the first equation alone, because $u_2$ is in it; you cannot
solve the second alone, because $u_1$ is in it. They are \textbf{coupled} --
each variable's rate of change depends on the current value of the other, so
neither can be determined in isolation. But stack them into a vector
$\vec{u} = \colvec{u_1\\u_2}$ and the coupling becomes a single matrix:
\[
  \frac{d}{dt}\colvec{u_1\\u_2}
  = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\colvec{u_1\\u_2},
  \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
  \frac{d\vec{u}}{dt} = \mat{A}\vec{u},
  \quad \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The derivative of a vector means ``differentiate each component.'' The
matrix $\mat{A}$ encodes \emph{who pushes on whom}: row $i$ tells you how the
rate of $u_i$ depends on every $u_j$.
\end{example}

This is the general setup, called a \textbf{linear system of first-order
ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with constant coefficients}. Each word
has a job:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{ordinary}: there is only one variable we differentiate with
        respect to, the time $t$;
  \item \emph{first-order}: only first derivatives $d\vec{u}/dt$ (rates of
        change) appear, no second derivatives (accelerations);
  \item \emph{linear}: each rate is a linear combination of the current values;
  \item \emph{constant coefficients}: the matrix $\mat{A}$ does not change
        with time.
\end{itemize}
In symbols:
\[
  \frac{d\vec{u}}{dt} = \mat{A}\,\vec{u},
  \qquad \vec{u}(0) = \vec{u}_0,
  \qquad \vec{u}(t)\in\R^n,\ \mat{A}\in\R^{n\times n}.
\]
The starting state $\vec{u}_0$ is called the \textbf{initial condition}. We
want a formula for $\vec{u}(t)$: where is the state at every future (and past)
time, given where it starts?

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of $\mat{A}\vec{u}$ as a \textbf{velocity field}. At every point $\vec{u}$
of the plane (or of $\R^n$), the matrix hands you an arrow $\mat{A}\vec{u}$ that
says ``if you are here, move \emph{this} way, \emph{this} fast.'' A solution
$\vec{u}(t)$ is a path that, at every instant, moves along the arrow at the
point where it is. Solving the system means: drop a particle at $\vec{u}_0$ and
let the arrows carry it, like a leaf carried by a river's current.
Figure~\ref{ch25:fig-field} draws these arrows for the two-tank matrix.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{One Eigenvector at a Time: Decoupling by Modes}
\label{ch25:onemode}

We do not yet know how to solve
$\frac{d\vec{u}}{dt} = \mat{A}\vec{u}$ for a general starting vector. But suppose
the system starts \emph{exactly along an eigenvector}. Then the
matrix has nothing to do but stretch, and the vector equation collapses back into
the scalar equation we already solved.

Let $\mat{A}\vec{x} = \lambda\vec{x}$, with $\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$. Guess a solution
that stays pointed along $\vec{x}$ for all time, only changing its length:
\[
  \vec{u}(t) = e^{\lambda t}\,\vec{x}.
\]
Test it by computing both sides of $\frac{d\vec{u}}{dt} = \mat{A}\vec{u}$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{d\vec{u}}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(e^{\lambda t}\right)\vec{x}
  = \lambda\, e^{\lambda t}\,\vec{x},\\
  \mat{A}\vec{u} &= \mat{A}\!\left(e^{\lambda t}\vec{x}\right)
  = e^{\lambda t}\,(\mat{A}\vec{x})
  = e^{\lambda t}\,\lambda\vec{x}
  = \lambda\,e^{\lambda t}\,\vec{x}.
\end{align*}
They match. So $\vec{u}(t) = e^{\lambda t}\vec{x}$ is a solution. Along an
eigendirection, the matrix system behaves \emph{exactly} like the one-dimensional
$\frac{du}{dt} = \lambda u$: the eigenvalue $\lambda$ is the growth rate, the
eigenvector $\vec{x}$ is the fixed direction.

\begin{focusbox}
Each eigenpair $(\lambda,\vec{x})$ of $\mat{A}$ gives one pure solution -- a
\textbf{mode} -- of $\frac{d\vec{u}}{dt}=\mat{A}\vec{u}$:
\[
  \vec{u}(t) = e^{\lambda t}\,\vec{x}.
\]
It marches along the fixed line $\spn\{\vec{x}\}$, growing like $e^{\lambda t}$.
The eigenvector sets the \emph{direction}; the eigenvalue sets the \emph{rate}.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Eigenvectors are the directions in which the system does not mix its
components; it only scales them. A general state is a combination of these pure
modes, each evolving at its own rate $e^{\lambda t}$. To solve the system, you
\emph{decompose into modes, let each evolve, and add
them back up.}
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Adding modes: the general solution by superposition}
\label{ch25:superposition}

A single mode describes trajectories (solution paths) that start directly on an eigenvector line.
To solve from an arbitrary initial state, we use the fact that the system is \emph{linear}:
if $\vec{u}(t)$ and $\vec{w}(t)$ are both solutions, then any linear combination
$c_1\vec{u}(t) + c_2\vec{w}(t)$ is also a solution. This fact is called the
principle of \textbf{superposition}.
It holds because differentiation and matrix multiplication both distribute over sums:
\[
  \frac{d}{dt}(c_1\vec{u}+c_2\vec{w}) = c_1\mat{A}\vec{u} + c_2\mat{A}\vec{w} = \mat{A}(c_1\vec{u}+c_2\vec{w}).
\]

So if $\mat{A}$ ($n\times n$) has $n$ independent eigenvectors $\vec{x}_1,\dots,
\vec{x}_n$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$, the general solution is
the superposition of all $n$ modes:
\[
  \boxed{\,
  \vec{u}(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t}\vec{x}_1
             + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t}\vec{x}_2
             + \cdots
             + c_n e^{\lambda_n t}\vec{x}_n.
  \,}
\]
The constants $c_1,\dots,c_n$ are fixed by the initial condition. Set $t=0$
(every exponential becomes $1$):
\[
  \vec{u}(0) = c_1\vec{x}_1 + \cdots + c_n\vec{x}_n = \vec{u}_0.
\]
That is ``write $\vec{u}_0$ in the eigenvector basis.'' In matrix form, with
$\mat{S} = [\,\vec{x}_1\ \cdots\ \vec{x}_n\,]$ (the eigenvector matrix of Chapter~24) and $\vec{c}=\colvec{c_1\\\vdots\\c_n}$,
\[
  \mat{S}\vec{c} = \vec{u}_0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \vec{c} = \mat{S}\inv\vec{u}_0.
\]

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Recipe to solve $\dfrac{d\vec{u}}{dt}=\mat{A}\vec{u},\ \vec{u}(0)=\vec{u}_0$
(diagonalizable case):}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Find eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ and eigenvectors $\vec{x}_i$ of $\mat{A}$.
  \item Expand the start in that basis: solve $\mat{S}\vec{c}=\vec{u}_0$ for the
        coefficients $c_i$.
  \item Let each coefficient ride its own exponential:
        $\;\vec{u}(t)=\sum_i c_i e^{\lambda_i t}\vec{x}_i.$
\end{enumerate}
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{A fully worked example}
\label{ch25:tanks-solved}

We now solve the two-tank system of Example~\ref{ch25:tanks},
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \vec{u}(0) = \colvec{3\\1}.
\]

\emph{Step 1: eigenvalues.} The characteristic polynomial:
\begin{align*}
  \det(\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I})
  &= \det\!\begin{bmatrix} -2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & -2-\lambda \end{bmatrix}
   = (-2-\lambda)^2 - 1\\
  &= \lambda^2 + 4\lambda + 3
   = (\lambda+1)(\lambda+3).
\end{align*}
So $\lambda_1 = -1$ and $\lambda_2 = -3$. Both eigenvalues are negative: because
each mode scales by $e^{\lambda_i t}$, negative rates mean that every component
shrinks toward zero over time. The system is stable, exactly as the inward-pointing
arrows in Figure~\ref{ch25:fig-field} suggested.

\emph{Step 2: eigenvectors.} For $\lambda_1=-1$, solve $(\mat{A}+\mat{I})\vec{x}=0$:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} = \vec{0}
  \;\Longrightarrow\; x_1 = x_2
  \;\Longrightarrow\; \vec{x}_1 = \colvec{1\\1}.
\]
For $\lambda_2=-3$, solve $(\mat{A}+3\mat{I})\vec{x}=0$:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x} = \vec{0}
  \;\Longrightarrow\; x_1 = -x_2
  \;\Longrightarrow\; \vec{x}_2 = \colvec{1\\-1}.
\]
(These eigenvectors are perpendicular because $\mat{A}$ is symmetric ($\mat{A}=\mat{A}\T$) ---
a preview of the Spectral Theorem in the next chapter.)

\emph{Step 3: match the start.} We need $c_1\colvec{1\\1} + c_2\colvec{1\\-1} =
\colvec{3\\1}$, i.e.
\[
  c_1 + c_2 = 3, \qquad c_1 - c_2 = 1
  \;\Longrightarrow\; c_1 = 2,\ c_2 = 1.
\]

\emph{Answer.} Letting each coefficient ride its exponential,
\[
  \vec{u}(t)
  = 2\,e^{-t}\colvec{1\\1} + 1\,e^{-3t}\colvec{1\\-1}
  = \colvec{2e^{-t} + e^{-3t}\\[2pt] 2e^{-t} - e^{-3t}}.
\]
Sanity checks: at $t=0$ this is $\colvec{2+1\\2-1}=\colvec{3\\1}$, correct. As
$t\to\infty$, both exponentials vanish, so $\vec{u}\to\vec{0}$: the salt
evens out between the tanks and drains away, as the arrows of
Figure~\ref{ch25:fig-field} suggested. The
$e^{-3t}$ part (the imbalance between the tanks) dies fast; the $e^{-t}$ part (the
total amount, along $\colvec{1\\1}$) lingers longest. The \emph{slowest} mode --
the eigenvalue closest to zero -- dominates the late-time behavior.
Figure~\ref{ch25:fig-tank-time} plots the two amounts against time, and
Figure~\ref{ch25:fig-modal-decomp} shows the same solution as a path in the
$(u_1,u_2)$ plane.

\begin{labbox}
Take $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$ and $\vec{u}(0)=\colvec{2\\1}$.
Its eigenvalues are $1$ and $2$ with eigenvectors $\colvec{1\\0}$ and
$\colvec{1\\1}$. Find $c_1,c_2$ (you should get $c_1=1,\ c_2=1$, so both modes are
present), write $\vec{u}(t)=e^{t}\colvec{1\\0}+e^{2t}\colvec{1\\1}$, and
confirm both components blow up like $e^{2t}$ for large $t$. Which mode wins the
race here -- and why is it the \emph{largest} eigenvalue this time, not the
smallest? (Answer: when eigenvalues are positive, the biggest one dominates
growth; when negative, the one nearest zero dominates the slow decay. Either way
it is the \emph{rightmost} eigenvalue -- largest real part -- that sets the
late-time behavior.)
\end{labbox}

\begin{strangbox}
Strang teaches this as ``$\vec{u}(t) = \sum c_i e^{\lambda_i t}\vec{x}_i$,'' highlighting
how eigenvalues govern dynamical behavior. \emph{Before} Strang: a system of linear
differential equations is solved by finding the directions the matrix only stretches
(eigenvectors) and their growth or decay rates (eigenvalues). \emph{After} Strang:
this is diagonalization applied to time evolution, and it leads directly to the
matrix exponential $e^{\mat{A}t}$.
\end{strangbox}

\section{Stability: Reading the Future from the Eigenvalues}
\label{ch25:stability}

The superposition formula shows the long-term fate of the system.
Each mode carries a factor $e^{\lambda_i t}$. As $t\to\infty$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item if $\lambda_i < 0$, that mode \textbf{decays} to zero;
  \item if $\lambda_i > 0$, that mode \textbf{blows up};
  \item if $\lambda_i = 0$, that mode \textbf{persists} at constant size.
\end{itemize}
The whole solution is a sum of such terms, so its fate is decided by the
\emph{worst} mode -- the one with the largest real part. (Eigenvalues may be
complex, as we see below, and then it is the real part $\operatorname{Re}\lambda$
that sets growth or decay.)

\begin{formalbox}
Consider $\dfrac{d\vec{u}}{dt}=\mat{A}\vec{u}$ with $\mat{A}$ having eigenvalues
$\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The system is \textbf{stable} (every solution $\to\vec{0}$) if and only if
        $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i < 0$ for \emph{all} $i$.
  \item It is \textbf{neutrally stable} (solutions stay bounded) if and only if
        $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i \le 0$ for all $i$, with the
        zero-real-part eigenvalues not defective (an eigenvalue is \textbf{defective}
        if it has fewer independent eigenvectors than its algebraic multiplicity,
        Chapter~24).
  \item It is \textbf{unstable} (some solution $\to\infty$) if and only if
        $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i > 0$ for some $i$, or some
        eigenvalue with $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i = 0$ is defective.
\end{itemize}
\end{formalbox}

Why ``real part''? Because eigenvalues can be complex, and that is where
oscillation hides. Recall Euler's formula $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$
from Chapter~23. If $\lambda = \sigma + i\omega$ with real numbers $\sigma$ and
$\omega$, then
\[
  e^{\lambda t} = e^{(\sigma + i\omega)t}
  = e^{\sigma t}\,\bigl(\cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t\bigr).
\]
The \emph{real part} $\sigma$ controls the envelope $e^{\sigma t}$ -- growth or
decay -- and the \emph{imaginary part} $\omega$ controls the spinning
$\cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t$ -- oscillation. Figure~\ref{ch25:fig-envelope}
plots the real part $e^{\sigma t}\cos\omega t$ for a decaying case.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Split each eigenvalue $\lambda = \sigma + i\omega$ into two jobs:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}

$\omega = \operatorname{Im}\lambda$ & sets the \textbf{frequency}: how fast the solution rotates.
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
A pure imaginary eigenvalue ($\sigma=0$) means \emph{undamped oscillation}, like a
frictionless pendulum swinging forever. A complex eigenvalue with $\sigma<0$ means
a \emph{damped spiral} into the origin. With $\sigma>0$, the spiral moves outward.
\end{intuitionbox}

it. Reversing all the arrows of a sink gives a \textbf{source}, where every path
flows out.

\begin{example}[Planar rotation: complex eigenvalues]
\label{ch25:complex}
Take
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad \frac{d\vec{u}}{dt}=\mat{A}\vec{u}.
\]
Written out (a prime means $d/dt$), $u_1' = -u_2$ and $u_2' = u_1$. The eigenvalues satisfy
$\lambda^2 + 1 = 0$, so $\lambda = \pm i$: pure imaginary, $\sigma=0$. The theory
predicts a \emph{center} -- closed circular orbits, no growth, no decay. Indeed,
the solution starting at $\colvec{1\\0}$ is
\[
  \vec{u}(t) = \colvec{\cos t\\ \sin t},
\]
which you can verify directly: $\vec{u}'=\colvec{-\sin t\\\cos t}
=\colvec{-u_2\\u_1}=\mat{A}\vec{u}$. The state runs around the unit circle
forever. The arrow $\mat{A}\vec{u}=(-u_2,u_1)$ is always perpendicular to
$\vec{u}$, so the flow turns without moving inward or outward. In
Section~\ref{ch25:diag} we find that $e^{\mat{A}t}$ is the rotation matrix
through angle $t$.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Sign confusion.} The condition for \emph{stable} continuous dynamics is
$\operatorname{Re}\lambda < 0$, eigenvalues in the \emph{left} half-plane. This is
\emph{not} the same as the rule for discrete iterations $\vec{u}_{k+1} =
\mat{A}\vec{u}_k$, where stability means $|\lambda| < 1$ (eigenvalues inside the
unit \emph{circle}). Continuous time cares about left/right; discrete time cares
about inside/outside the circle. Mixing them up is a classic exam mistake. The
two rules are linked: if you look at a continuous system only every $t$ time
units, each step multiplies by a matrix with eigenvalues $z = e^{\lambda t}$, and
$|e^{\lambda t}| = e^{t\operatorname{Re}\lambda}$ is less than $1$ exactly when
$\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0$. So the left half-plane maps onto
the inside of the unit circle (Figure~\ref{ch25:fig-eigenplane}).
\end{trapbox}

\begin{walkbox}
A system has eigenvalues $-5$ and $-0.1$. Both decay, so it is stable -- but if
you watched it, what would you \emph{see}? One piece vanishes almost instantly;
the other lingers fifty times longer. The long-term shape of \emph{every}
trajectory is therefore nearly the eigenvector of $-0.1$. Think on your walk: in a
stable system, does the smallest or the largest $|\lambda|$ shape what you
observe after a while? (This is exactly why the ``slow mode'' governs the
relaxation time of springs, circuits, and chemical reactions.)
\end{walkbox}

\section{The Matrix Exponential: One Object for the Whole Flow}
\label{ch25:matexp}

We solved the system mode by mode. That works, but there is a more compact way
to say it. In one dimension we never said ``decompose and add'' -- we
wrote $u(t) = e^{at}u_0$. We want the same compact statement for vectors:
\[
  \vec{u}(t) = \,?\, \cdot \vec{u}_0,
\]
where the ``?'' is a single matrix that takes \emph{any} start $\vec{u}_0$ and
slides it forward to time $t$. That matrix is the \textbf{matrix exponential}
$e^{\mat{A}t}$, and we define it by the same power series we used in
Section~\ref{ch25:scalar}, with the scalar $a$ replaced by the matrix $\mat{A}$.

\begin{definition}[Matrix exponential]
\label{ch25:def-matexp}
For a square matrix $\mat{M}\in\R^{n\times n}$, define
\[
  e^{\mat{M}}
  \;=\;
  \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mat{M}^k}{k!}
  \;=\;
  \mat{I} + \mat{M} + \frac{\mat{M}^2}{2!} + \frac{\mat{M}^3}{3!} + \cdots .
\]
Here $\mat{M}^0 = \mat{I}$. Just as an infinite series of numbers converges
when its partial sums settle to a definite limit (Section~\ref{ch39:calculus}),
an infinite series of matrices converges when the number series in each of its
$n^2$ entries converges. This always happens here. If $m$ is the largest entry of
$\mat{M}$ in absolute value, then every entry of $\mat{M}^k$ is at most $(nm)^k$
in absolute value, so each entry's series is bounded term by term by the
convergent series $\sum_k (nm)^k/k! = e^{nm}$
(Section~\ref{ch39:integration} develops the calculus of
these matrix series and their term-by-term derivatives). Thus $e^{\mat{M}}$ is
always a well-defined $n\times n$ matrix. In particular,
$e^{\mat{A}t} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mat{A}^k t^k}{k!}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{focusbox}
$e^{\mat{A}t}$ is a \emph{matrix}, not a number. It is the ``time-$t$ flow
map'': multiply it by where you are now, and it returns where you will be after
time $t$. The
solution of $\frac{d\vec{u}}{dt}=\mat{A}\vec{u}$, $\vec{u}(0)=\vec{u}_0$, is
\[
  \boxed{\ \vec{u}(t) = e^{\mat{A}t}\,\vec{u}_0.\ }
\]
\end{focusbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think (mental check):} If $\mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -5 \end{bmatrix}$ is already diagonal, what is $e^{\mat{A}t}$?
\emph{Check:} Because powers of a diagonal matrix are diagonal ($\mat{A}^k = \diag(2^k, (-5)^k)$), each entry computes its own scalar series:
$e^{\mat{A}t} = \diag(e^{2t}, e^{-5t}) = \begin{bmatrix} e^{2t} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-5t} \end{bmatrix}$.
\end{recoverybox}

We now prove the boxed claim the same way we did for scalars:
differentiate the series term by term and watch it reproduce $\mat{A}$ times
itself.

\begin{theorem}[The matrix exponential solves the system]
\label{ch25:thm-solves}
The matrix-valued function $\Phi(t) = e^{\mat{A}t}$ satisfies
\[
  \frac{d}{dt}\,e^{\mat{A}t} = \mat{A}\,e^{\mat{A}t} = e^{\mat{A}t}\,\mat{A},
  \qquad e^{\mat{A}\cdot 0} = \mat{I}.
\]
Consequently $\vec{u}(t) = e^{\mat{A}t}\vec{u}_0$ solves
$\frac{d\vec{u}}{dt}=\mat{A}\vec{u}$ with $\vec{u}(0)=\vec{u}_0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
At $t=0$ every term but the first vanishes, leaving $e^{\mat{A}\cdot 0}=\mat{I}$.
Differentiate the series term by term (this is allowed for a power series that
converges for every $t$, as this one does; see Section~\ref{ch39:integration}).
Then factor one $\mat{A}$ out of the front (it commutes with every
$\mat{A}^{k-1}$) and reindex with $j=k-1$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{d}{dt}\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{\mat{A}^k t^k}{k!}
  &= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\mat{A}^k\, k\, t^{k-1}}{k!}
   = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\mat{A}^k t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\\
  &= \mat{A}\sum_{j=0}^\infty \frac{\mat{A}^j t^j}{j!}
   = \mat{A}\,e^{\mat{A}t}.
\end{align*}
Pulling the $\mat{A}$ out on the right instead gives $e^{\mat{A}t}\mat{A}$; the
two agree because $\mat{A}$ commutes with its own powers. Finally, with
$\vec{u}(t) = e^{\mat{A}t}\vec{u}_0$,
\[
  \frac{d\vec{u}}{dt} = \Bigl(\tfrac{d}{dt}e^{\mat{A}t}\Bigr)\vec{u}_0
  = \mat{A}e^{\mat{A}t}\vec{u}_0 = \mat{A}\vec{u}(t),
\]
and $\vec{u}(0)=\mat{I}\vec{u}_0=\vec{u}_0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{connectionbox}
This is the same proof, line for line, as the calculus proof that
$\frac{d}{dt}e^{at} = a\,e^{at}$. We replaced the scalar $a$ by the
matrix $\mat{A}$ and changed nothing else.
Calculus supplies the power series and term-by-term differentiation
(Section~\ref{ch39:integration}); linear algebra supplies the algebra of
matrix powers $\mat{A}^k$.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{Properties, and the one trap}
\label{ch25:props}

The matrix exponential keeps most of the algebra you expect from $e^x$, with
one exception.

\begin{proposition}[Algebra of $e^{\mat{A}}$]
\label{ch25:prop-algebra}
For square $\mat{A},\mat{B}$ of the same size:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $e^{\mat{0}} = \mat{I}$.
  \item $\bigl(e^{\mat{A}t}\bigr)\inv = e^{-\mat{A}t}$; in particular
        $e^{\mat{A}t}$ is \emph{always invertible}.
  \item $e^{\mat{A}(s+t)} = e^{\mat{A}s}\,e^{\mat{A}t}$ (the flow composes:
        evolving $s$ then $t$ equals evolving $s+t$).
  \item \textbf{If $\mat{A}\mat{B}=\mat{B}\mat{A}$,} then
        $e^{\mat{A}+\mat{B}} = e^{\mat{A}}e^{\mat{B}}$.
        If they do \emph{not} commute, this is generally \emph{false}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Property~(2) deserves a closer look. If $\mat{A}$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$,
the eigenvalues of $e^{\mat{A}t}$ are $e^{\lambda_1 t},\dots,e^{\lambda_n t}$
(for diagonalizable $\mat{A}$ this follows from the formula in
Section~\ref{ch25:diag}).
Because the determinant of any matrix equals the product of its eigenvalues,
\[
  \det(e^{\mat{A}t}) = \prod_{i=1}^n e^{\lambda_i t} = e^{t\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i} = e^{t\,\tr\mat{A}},
\]
where $\tr\mat{A}$, the \textbf{trace}, is the sum of the entries on the main
diagonal (which always equals the sum of all eigenvalues, Chapter~23). Because the scalar
exponential $e^{t\,\tr\mat{A}}$ is never zero, $\det(e^{\mat{A}t}) \neq 0$ for all
$t$. The flow map is always invertible: it can never flatten space into a
lower-dimensional slice, and two distinct initial states can never arrive at
the same point. This is the matrix form of a general fact about differential
equations: each starting state has exactly one solution, so two different
solution paths never cross.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{$e^{\mat{A}+\mat{B}} \neq e^{\mat{A}}e^{\mat{B}}$ when $\mat{A}$ and
$\mat{B}$ do not commute.} The familiar law $e^{x+y}=e^xe^y$ relies on
$xy=yx$, which is automatic for numbers but not for matrices. To see the
failure, expand both sides up to the second-order terms (those with two
factors). In $e^{\mat{A}+\mat{B}}$ they are
\[
  \tfrac12(\mat{A}+\mat{B})^2 = \tfrac12\mat{A}^2 + \tfrac12\mat{A}\mat{B}
  + \tfrac12\mat{B}\mat{A} + \tfrac12\mat{B}^2,
\]
while in $e^{\mat{A}}e^{\mat{B}} = (\mat{I}+\mat{A}+\tfrac12\mat{A}^2+\cdots)
(\mat{I}+\mat{B}+\tfrac12\mat{B}^2+\cdots)$ they are
\[
  \tfrac12\mat{A}^2 + \mat{A}\mat{B} + \tfrac12\mat{B}^2.
\]
They differ by $\tfrac12(\mat{A}\mat{B}-\mat{B}\mat{A})$. The matrix
$\mat{A}\mat{B}-\mat{B}\mat{A}$ is called the \textbf{commutator} of $\mat{A}$
and $\mat{B}$; it is zero exactly when the two matrices commute. Unless it
is zero, the exponential of a sum does not factor into a product of
exponentials.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Computing $e^{\mat{A}t}$ by Diagonalization}
\label{ch25:diag}

The power-series definition is the foundation, but it is hard to evaluate directly.
Diagonalization gives a practical way to compute it, and shows that the matrix
exponential packs the mode expansion from Section~\ref{ch25:superposition} into a single
matrix.

Suppose $\mat{A}$ is diagonalizable: $\mat{A} = \mat{S}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{S}\inv$,
where $\mat{\Lambda} = \diag(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$ holds the eigenvalues and
$\mat{S}$ holds the eigenvectors as columns. Powers of $\mat{A}$ simplify:
\[
  \mat{A}^k
  = (\mat{S}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{S}\inv)(\mat{S}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{S}\inv)\cdots(\mat{S}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{S}\inv)
  = \mat{S}\mat{\Lambda}^k\mat{S}\inv,
\]
because each inner pair $\mat{S}\inv\mat{S}$ cancels to $\mat{I}$. Feed this into the series:
\begin{align*}
  e^{\mat{A}t}
  &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{\mat{A}^k t^k}{k!}
   = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{\mat{S}\mat{\Lambda}^k\mat{S}\inv t^k}{k!}\\
  &= \mat{S}\!\left(\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{\mat{\Lambda}^k t^k}{k!}\right)\!\mat{S}\inv
   = \mat{S}\, e^{\mat{\Lambda}t}\,\mat{S}\inv.
\end{align*}
A diagonal matrix exponentiates entry by entry, because the powers of a diagonal
matrix are the powers of its diagonal entries:
\[
  e^{\mat{\Lambda}t}
  = \diag\!\bigl(e^{\lambda_1 t}, e^{\lambda_2 t}, \dots, e^{\lambda_n t}\bigr).
\]

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The master formula.} If $\mat{A}=\mat{S}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{S}\inv$, then
\[
  e^{\mat{A}t}
  = \mat{S}
    \begin{bmatrix}
      e^{\lambda_1 t} & & \\
      & \ddots & \\
      & & e^{\lambda_n t}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \mat{S}\inv.
\]
Read it as a sentence: \emph{change to eigen-coordinates ($\mat{S}\inv$),
exponentiate each one-dimensional flow ($e^{\lambda_i t}$), change back
($\mat{S}$).} The hard matrix problem became $n$ trivial scalar problems.
\end{focusbox}

($e^{\mat{A}t}$) means solving a coupled system. Instead drop down into

independent scalar flows, scale each by its own $e^{\lambda_i t}$, then ride
$\mat{S}$ back up. The two routes give the same $\vec{u}(t)$ -- that equality

\begin{intuitionbox}
Figure~\ref{ch25:fig-detour} draws the master formula as a detour.
In the eigenbasis, the coupled system uncouples completely: each eigen-coordinate
$w_i = (\mat{S}\inv\vec{u})_i$ obeys its own scalar differential equation $w_i' = \lambda_i w_i$, so
$w_i(t) = e^{\lambda_i t} w_i(0)$. The matrices $\mat{S}\inv$ and $\mat{S}$ convert
vectors between the standard coordinates and the eigenvector coordinates.
\end{intuitionbox}

The master formula also gives back the mode formula. Multiply it by
$\vec{u}_0$ and write $\mat{S}\inv\vec{u}_0 = \vec{c}$:
\[
  \vec{u}(t) = e^{\mat{A}t}\vec{u}_0
  = \mat{S}\,e^{\mat{\Lambda}t}\,\vec{c}
  = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i\, e^{\lambda_i t}\,\vec{x}_i.
\]
This is the superposition formula of
Section~\ref{ch25:superposition}.

\begin{example}[Exponentiating the two-tank matrix]
\label{ch25:tanks-exp}
For $\mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix}-2&1\\1&-2\end{bmatrix}$ we found
$\lambda_1=-1,\lambda_2=-3$ with $\vec{x}_1=\colvec{1\\1},\vec{x}_2=\colvec{1\\-1}$.
So
\[
  \mat{S} = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{S}\inv = \frac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Then
\[
  e^{\mat{A}t}
  = \mat{S}
    \begin{bmatrix}e^{-t}&0\\0&e^{-3t}\end{bmatrix}
    \mat{S}\inv
  = \frac12
    \begin{bmatrix}
      e^{-t}+e^{-3t} & e^{-t}-e^{-3t}\\[2pt]
      e^{-t}-e^{-3t} & e^{-t}+e^{-3t}
    \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Apply it to $\vec{u}_0=\colvec{3\\1}$:
\[
  e^{\mat{A}t}\colvec{3\\1}
  = \frac12\colvec{3(e^{-t}+e^{-3t}) + (e^{-t}-e^{-3t})\\[2pt]
                   3(e^{-t}-e^{-3t}) + (e^{-t}+e^{-3t})}
  = \colvec{2e^{-t}+e^{-3t}\\[2pt] 2e^{-t}-e^{-3t}},
\]
matching Section~\ref{ch25:tanks-solved} exactly. The single matrix $e^{\mat{A}t}$
carries the whole solution for \emph{every} starting vector at once.
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Compute $e^{\mat{A}t}$ by hand for the rotation generator
$\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ of Example~\ref{ch25:complex}, but
\emph{without} diagonalizing -- use the series. Show $\mat{A}^2=-\mat{I}$, hence
$\mat{A}^3=-\mat{A}$, $\mat{A}^4=\mat{I}$, and the powers cycle. Group the series
into even and odd terms and recognize the Taylor series of cosine and sine to get
\[
  e^{\mat{A}t} = \begin{bmatrix}\cos t & -\sin t\\ \sin t & \cos t\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The matrix exponential of the ``turning'' matrix is the rotation by angle $t$.
Compare it with Euler's formula $e^{i t} = \cos t + i\sin t$.
\end{labbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
The scalar identity $e^{it}=\cos t + i\sin t$
(Euler's formula) is the
counterpart of the matrix statement ``the exponential of the rotation generator is the
rotation matrix.'' The imaginary unit $i$ behaves algebraically like the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$; squaring it gives $-\mat{I}$,
just as $i^2=-1$. This is why complex eigenvalues signal rotation in
$\mat{A}\vec{x}$ and oscillation in $e^{\mat{A}t}\vec{x}$.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{When You Cannot Diagonalize}
\label{ch25:defective}

The master formula assumed $n$ independent eigenvectors. What if $\mat{A}$ is
\textbf{defective} -- lacking a complete set of $n$ independent eigenvectors, like
$\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}$? There is no
eigenvector basis, but the matrix exponential is still defined: its
power series converges for \emph{every} square matrix. You can evaluate it directly by
splitting $\mat{A}$ into a scalar multiple of $\mat{I}$ plus a \textbf{nilpotent} remainder,
meaning a matrix that becomes the zero matrix when raised to a high enough power.

Write $\mat{A} = \lambda\mat{I} + \mat{N}$ with $\mat{N}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0
\end{bmatrix}$. These commute (every matrix commutes with scalar multiples of the
identity $\lambda\mat{I}$), so by Proposition~\ref{ch25:prop-algebra}(4),
\[
  e^{\mat{A}t} = e^{\lambda t\mat{I}}\, e^{\mat{N}t}
  = e^{\lambda t}\, e^{\mat{N}t}.
\]
Now $\mat{N}^2 = \mat{0}$ (so $\mat{N}$ is nilpotent of degree 2), so its series
truncates cleanly after two terms:
\[
  e^{\mat{N}t} = \mat{I} + \mat{N}t = \begin{bmatrix}1 & t\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Therefore
\[
  e^{\mat{A}t} = e^{\lambda t}\begin{bmatrix}1 & t\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix}e^{\lambda t} & t\,e^{\lambda t}\\ 0 & e^{\lambda t}\end{bmatrix}.
\]

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think (mental check):} If $\mat{N} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, what is $\mat{N}^2$, and what is $e^{\mat{N}t}$?
\emph{Check:} $\mat{N}^2 = \begin{bmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix} = \mat{0}$, so only two terms survive the power series:
$e^{\mat{N}t} = \mat{I} + \mat{N}t = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3t \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
\end{recoverybox}

The new ingredient is a factor of $t$ multiplying an exponential. A
defective eigenvalue produces solutions involving $t\,e^{\lambda t}$ alongside
$e^{\lambda t}$: the missing eigenvector direction adds growth like $t$ on top
of the exponential. When $\lambda<0$ the exponential still wins in the end, so
$t\,e^{\lambda t}\to 0$, but the extra factor $t$ makes it rise first and decay
later (Figure~\ref{ch25:fig-defective}).

\begin{remark}
The general structure behind this is the \textbf{Jordan canonical form}
(introduced in Chapter~28, Section~\ref{ch28:jordan}) -- every matrix can be expressed as
$\mat{S}(\mat{\Lambda}+\mat{N})\mat{S}\inv$ with $\mat{\Lambda}$ diagonal,
$\mat{N}$ nilpotent, and $\mat{\Lambda}\mat{N}=\mat{N}\mat{\Lambda}$ (the
entries of $\mat{S}$ and $\mat{\Lambda}$ may be complex). Because
the pieces commute, $e^{\mat{A}t}$ always decomposes into pure exponentials
multiplied by polynomials in $t$. For now, keep the headline: the flow map
$e^{\mat{A}t}$ \emph{exists and is computable for every matrix $\mat{A}$},
diagonalizable or not.
\end{remark}

\begin{connectionbox}
A higher-order equation in one unknown (one with second or higher derivatives)
can be rewritten as a first-order matrix system, and this is where the
$t\,e^{\lambda t}$ terms you may have seen in calculus come from. The equation
$y'' + 2y' + y = 0$ has the repeated root $\lambda=-1$ (of
$\lambda^2+2\lambda+1=0$), and its solutions are
$e^{-t}$ \emph{and} $t\,e^{-t}$ -- exactly the defective pattern above. Setting
$\vec{u}=\colvec{y\\y'}$ turns the second-order equation into
$\vec{u}'=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-1&-2\end{bmatrix}\vec{u}$, a defective $2\times2$
system. So the calculus rule ``for a repeated root, also try $t\,e^{\lambda t}$''
comes from the nilpotent part of this matrix.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{A Modern Application}
\label{ch25:application}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Continuous-time Markov chains in epidemics, chemistry, and queues.}
A \emph{continuous-time Markov chain} is a system that jumps at random between a
finite list of states, where the rate of each jump depends only on the current
state. Examples: whether a person is Susceptible / Infected / Recovered, which
shape a protein molecule is folded into, how many customers wait in a queue.
The \emph{probability distribution} $\vec{p}(t)$ (the list of probabilities of
being in each state at time $t$; its entries add up to $1$) evolves by a linear
differential equation,
\[
  \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} = \mat{Q}\T\vec{p},
\]
where $\mat{Q}$ is the \emph{generator} (rate) matrix: off-diagonal $Q_{ij}\ge0$
is the rate of jumping $i\to j$, and each row sums to zero so probability is
conserved. The solution is exactly the matrix exponential,
\[
  \vec{p}(t) = e^{\mat{Q}\T t}\,\vec{p}(0).
\]
The eigenvalue $\lambda=0$ (every generator has one, because rows sum to zero)
carries the \emph{steady-state distribution}: the mode that never decays, the
long-run behavior of the chain. If every state can be reached from every other,
all other eigenvalues have negative real part, and
the one closest to zero sets the \emph{mixing time}: how long until the chain
forgets where it started. Disease forecasting, queueing theory,
and molecular kinetics all compute with $e^{\mat{Q}\T t}$.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{The exponential of a network.} In graph theory and neuroscience, if
$\mat{A}$ is the adjacency matrix of a network (Chapter~10), a \emph{walk} of
length $k$ from node $i$ to node $j$ is a route along $k$ edges, possibly
revisiting nodes, and $(\mat{A}^k)_{ij}$ counts these walks. Because
$e^{\mat{A}}=\sum \mat{A}^k/k!$, the entry $(e^{\mat{A}})_{ij}$ is a weighted count of
\emph{all} walks from node $i$ to node $j$, with walks of length $k$ down-weighted
by $1/k!$. This number, called the \emph{communicability} of $i$ and $j$,
scores how easily influence (signals, rumors, current) spreads between the two
nodes. The diagonal entry $(e^{\mat{A}})_{ii}$, which counts walks that start
and end at node $i$, is used as a \emph{centrality} score: a measure of how
important node $i$ is to the network.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{The Geometry of the Flow}
\label{ch25:geometry}

This section collects the pictures in one place. The
matrix $\mat{A}$ defines a velocity field; the family $\{e^{\mat{A}t}\}_{t\in\R}$,
one matrix for each time $t$, is the \emph{flow} it generates. It starts
at $\mat{I}$ when $t=0$ and slides every point of space along the arrows as $t$
increases. Figure~\ref{ch25:fig-node-saddle} compares a system with two negative
eigenvalues to one with eigenvalues of opposite sign.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Every $2\times2$ system with nonzero eigenvalues falls into one of these types,
decided by the two eigenvalues:
\begin{itemize}
  \item both real, same sign $\to$ \textbf{node} (in if negative, out if positive);
  \item real, opposite signs $\to$ \textbf{saddle} (always unstable);
  \item complex, nonzero real part $\to$ \textbf{spiral} (in if $\sigma<0$);
  \item pure imaginary $\to$ \textbf{center} (closed orbits, neutrally stable).
\end{itemize}
From two numbers you know the shape of every
trajectory before computing a single point.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{strangbox}
Strang sums this up as: \emph{the eigenvalues of $\mat{A}$ control
$e^{\mat{A}t}$}, and the four basic questions (does it grow, decay,
oscillate, blow up?) are answered by the locations of the $\lambda$'s. \emph{After}
Strang, the next step is to ask what happens when $\mat{A}$ is symmetric
(real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors, no spirals -- the spectral theorem,
next chapter) and what $e^{\mat{A}t}$ means when $\mat{A}$ is skew-symmetric,
$\mat{A}\T=-\mat{A}$ (pure rotation; lengths are preserved).
\end{strangbox}

\begin{historybox}
For two centuries after Newton, mathematical physics looked for explicit formulas for the paths of planets and other moving bodies. In the 1880s the French mathematician Henri Poincar\'e studied the \textbf{three-body problem}: predicting the motion of three bodies that attract each other by gravity, such as the Sun, Earth, and Moon. He showed that no general formula of the familiar kind exists. So he changed the question. Instead of looking for a formula for $\vec{u}(t)$, he asked what all the trajectories look like together, and drew what we now call phase portraits. For a linear system $\frac{d\vec{u}}{dt} = \mat{A}\vec{u}$, these are exactly the nodes, saddles, spirals, and centers of this chapter. Around 1960, Rudolf Kalman made linear \textbf{state-space models} standard in aerospace engineering: a vector $\vec{u}(t)$ of positions and velocities obeying a first-order system $\frac{d\vec{u}}{dt} = \mat{A}\vec{u}$ (plus control inputs). The matrix exponential $e^{\mat{A}t}$ became a basic tool for planning spacecraft trajectories and controlling robots.

Computing $e^{\mat{A}t}$ on a computer is harder than it looks. In their 1978 paper ``Nineteen Dubious Ways to Compute the Exponential of a Matrix,'' Cleve Moler and Charles Van Loan showed that adding up the power series $\sum \frac{(\mat{A}t)^k}{k!}$ term by term in floating-point arithmetic can be badly inaccurate when $\mat{A}t$ has large entries. The terms grow huge with alternating signs before they shrink, and subtracting huge, nearly equal numbers wipes out the correct digits. This loss of accuracy is called \textbf{catastrophic cancellation}. The factor $t\,e^{\lambda t}$ of defective matrices also has a physical relative: \textbf{resonance}, where pushing an undamped oscillator at its natural frequency gives a swing that grows like $t\sin\omega t$.

Real physical systems are usually nonlinear, but the eigenvalue test of this chapter still applies near an equilibrium point (a state where nothing changes). There the system is approximated by a linear one whose matrix $\mat{A}$ is the \textbf{Jacobian} (Chapter~5), the matrix of first partial derivatives. In 1892 the Russian mathematician Aleksandr Lyapunov showed how to prove stability without solving the differential equation at all: find an energy-like quantity (a \textbf{Lyapunov function}) that decreases along every trajectory.

Two problems are still active research. First, for huge sparse systems with millions of variables (power grids, quantum systems, chemical reaction networks), the $n\times n$ matrix $e^{\mat{A}t}$ is far too large to form or store. Instead one computes the single vector $e^{\mat{A}t}\vec{v}$ directly, using Krylov methods (Chapter~33) that only need the products $\mat{A}\vec{v}, \mat{A}^2\vec{v}, \dots$. Second, \textbf{non-normal matrices} (those with $\mat{A}\mat{A}\T \neq \mat{A}\T\mat{A}$; their eigenvectors cannot be chosen perpendicular) can show \textbf{transient growth}: trajectories can grow large for a while before they decay, even when every eigenvalue has negative real part. In fluid flow this temporary growth can trigger turbulence. Studying it uses \textbf{pseudospectra}, which track how far the eigenvalues move when the matrix is changed slightly. In Chapter~26 (Section~\ref{ch26:what-is-symmetric}), we see how symmetric matrices rule out spirals and give pure exponential decay along perpendicular axes, and in Chapter~27 (Definition~\ref{ch27:def-pd}), positive definite matrices provide the energy functions that certify stability.
\end{historybox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Cost and intuition recap.} What did solving
$\frac{d\vec{u}}{dt}=\mat{A}\vec{u}$ cost, and what is the one idea to
keep?
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Intuition:} eigenvectors are the directions the flow does not
        mix; along each, the system is the scalar law $w'=\lambda w$ with
        solution $e^{\lambda t}$. Solve, then superpose.
  \item \textbf{Cost (diagonalizable case):} finding all eigenvalues and
        eigenvectors of $\mat{A}$ takes about $O(n^3)$ work. After that,
        \emph{each} value $\vec{u}(t)$ is one change of basis, a diagonal
        scaling, and a change back -- $O(n^2)$ per time, for as many times $t$
        as you like. You pay the $O(n^3)$ once and reuse it.
  \item \textbf{Cost (general $\mat{A}$):} the matrix exponential $e^{\mat{A}t}$
        still exists and is computable without eigenvectors. A standard method,
        \emph{scaling and squaring}, sums a short series for
        $e^{\mat{A}t/2^s}$ (a small time step, where the series is accurate)
        and then squares the result $s$ times, since
        $\bigl(e^{\mat{A}t/2^s}\bigr)^{2^s} = e^{\mat{A}t}$.
  \item \textbf{The headline:} the signs of $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i$ decide
        the fate of \emph{every} trajectory before you compute a single point.
\end{itemize}
\end{recoverybox}

\section*{Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A linear system $\frac{d\vec{u}}{dt}=\mat{A}\vec{u}$ is a velocity field;
        its solution is a path flowing along the arrows.
  \item Each eigenpair gives a pure mode $\vec{u}(t)=e^{\lambda t}\vec{x}$. By
        superposition the general (diagonalizable) solution is
        $\vec{u}(t)=\sum_i c_i e^{\lambda_i t}\vec{x}_i$, with $\vec{c}=
        \mat{S}\inv\vec{u}_0$.
  \item \textbf{Stability} is read from the eigenvalues: stable exactly when every
        $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i < 0$. The real part is growth/decay; the
        imaginary part is oscillation frequency.
  \item The \textbf{matrix exponential} $e^{\mat{A}t}=\sum_k \mat{A}^kt^k/k!$ is
        the time-$t$ flow map; $\vec{u}(t)=e^{\mat{A}t}\vec{u}_0$ solves the
        system, proved by the same term-by-term differentiation as the scalar
        case.
  \item When $\mat{A}=\mat{S}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{S}\inv$,
        $e^{\mat{A}t}=\mat{S}\,e^{\mat{\Lambda}t}\,\mat{S}\inv$ with
        $e^{\mat{\Lambda}t}=\diag(e^{\lambda_i t})$ -- diagonalization applied to
        continuous time evolution. Defective matrices add polynomial-in-$t$ factors.
  \item Beware: $e^{\mat{A}+\mat{B}}=e^{\mat{A}}e^{\mat{B}}$ only when
        $\mat{A}\mat{B}=\mat{B}\mat{A}$; and continuous stability ($\operatorname{Re}
        \lambda<0$) is not discrete stability ($|\lambda|<1$).
  \item \textbf{Looking forward:} In this chapter, eigenvectors could lean at
        skewed angles and eigenvalues could wander into the complex plane,
        generating spirals. In Chapter~26 (Section~\ref{ch26:what-is-symmetric}), we see
        that when a matrix is symmetric ($\mat{A} = \mat{A}\T$), no spirals can
        occur: every eigenvalue is real and the eigenvectors can always be
        chosen perpendicular, which is the content of the Spectral Theorem.
\end{itemize}

Let $\mat{N}=\begin{bmatrix}0&2\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$. Compute $\mat{N}^2$, then sum
the series to find $e^{\mat{N}t}$ exactly. Verify by checking
$\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mat{N}t}=\mat{N}e^{\mat{N}t}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Diagonalize, then exponentiate]
\label{ch25:ex-diag}
For $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-2&-3\end{bmatrix}$, find $\mat{S}$ and
$\mat{\Lambda}$, then assemble $e^{\mat{A}t}=\mat{S}e^{\mat{\Lambda}t}\mat{S}\inv$
as an explicit $2\times2$ matrix of exponentials. (Eigenvalues are $-1$ and $-2$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Rotation generator]
\label{ch25:ex-rotation}
Let $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\omega\\\omega&0\end{bmatrix}$. Using
$\mat{A}^2=-\omega^2\mat{I}$, sum the even and odd parts of the series to show
$e^{\mat{A}t}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\omega t&-\sin\omega t\\\sin\omega t&\cos\omega t
\end{bmatrix}$. What is the period of the resulting motion?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Higher-order ODE as a system]
\label{ch25:ex-secondorder}
The damped oscillator is $y''+3y'+2y=0$. Set $\vec{u}=\colvec{y\\y'}$ and write
the equivalent first-order system $\vec{u}'=\mat{A}\vec{u}$. Find the eigenvalues
of $\mat{A}$ and confirm they are the roots of $\lambda^2+3\lambda+2=0$. Conclude
the general solution $y(t)=c_1 e^{-t}+c_2 e^{-2t}$, and explain why the system is
stable.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The commutator obstruction]
\label{ch25:ex-commutator}
Let $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ and
$\mat{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$. Show $\mat{A}\mat{B}\neq
\mat{B}\mat{A}$. Compute $e^{\mat{A}}$ and $e^{\mat{B}}$ (each series is short),
form $e^{\mat{A}}e^{\mat{B}}$, and compare with $e^{\mat{A}+\mat{B}}$ where
$\mat{A}+\mat{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ (this one has eigenvalues
$\pm1$). Confirm they are different, and identify the source of the discrepancy.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trace and determinant of the flow]
\label{ch25:ex-trace}
Prove that $\det\!\bigl(e^{\mat{A}t}\bigr)=e^{t\,\tr\mat{A}}$ for diagonalizable
$\mat{A}$. (Hint: use $e^{\mat{A}t}=\mat{S}e^{\mat{\Lambda}t}\mat{S}\inv$ and that
$\det$ is multiplicative.) Interpret: when is the flow volume-preserving?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: stability]
\label{ch25:ex-stability}
Suppose $\mat{A}$ is diagonalizable with all eigenvalues satisfying
$\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i<0$. Using $\vec{u}(t)=\sum_i c_i e^{\lambda_i t}
\vec{x}_i$, prove that $\norm{\vec{u}(t)}\to0$ as $t\to\infty$ for \emph{every}
initial condition. Where exactly does the negativity of the real parts enter?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Challenge: the slow mode]
\label{ch25:ex-slowmode}
A stable system has eigenvalues $\lambda_1=-0.2$ and $\lambda_2=-5$ with
eigenvectors $\vec{x}_1,\vec{x}_2$. Argue that for large $t$, \emph{every}
nonzero trajectory $\vec{u}(t)$ becomes nearly parallel to $\vec{x}_1$, regardless
of the start (assuming $c_1\neq0$). Quantify ``large'': roughly how big must $t$
be before the $\vec{x}_2$ contribution is under $1\%$ of the $\vec{x}_1$
contribution, if they start with equal coefficients? (Use $e^{-5t}/e^{-0.2t}=
e^{-4.8t}<0.01$.)
\end{exercise}
