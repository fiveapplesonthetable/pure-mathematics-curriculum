\chapter{Dot Products, Length, and Angle}

In Chapter~3 we combined vectors by scaling them and adding them tip-to-tail, and
we described their span: the lines, planes, and hyperplanes through the origin that
those combinations fill out. Those tools can tell whether an arrow lies in a plane,
but they cannot tell how \emph{long} an arrow is or at what \emph{angle} two arrows
meet. Questions about length, distance, and angle are called \emph{metric}
questions (from the Greek word for ``measure''), and addition and scaling alone
cannot answer them.

This chapter introduces one new operation that answers both: the
\textbf{dot product}. You multiply matching coordinates and add the results. From
that single number we get the length of an arrow, the angle between two arrows,
and a one-line test for perpendicular arrows: $\vec u \cdot \vec v = 0$. Later
chapters use the same operation to find the point of a line or plane closest to a
given point, to fit a line to noisy data (least squares), and to split a signal
into sine and cosine waves (Fourier series). Search engines use it to judge whether
two documents are about the same thing.

We start with the geometry (arrows, lengths, and the law of cosines from
trigonometry), then make it precise, and finish with two inequalities about dot
products and lengths that hold in \emph{every} dimension. Take this chapter slowly:
most of the rest of the book relies on it.

\chapterline

\section{The dot product, two ways}
\label{ch04:dot-def}

\subsection{The algebraic definition}

Here is the whole operation in one line. Given two vectors in $\R^n$ with the same
number of entries,
\[
  \vec{u} = \colvec{u_1 \\ u_2 \\ \vdots \\ u_n}, \qquad
  \vec{v} = \colvec{v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n},
\]
their \textbf{dot product} is the single number
\[
  \vec{u}\cdot\vec{v} \;=\; u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + \cdots + u_n v_n
  \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i.
\]
Multiply matching entries, add up the results. The inputs are two vectors; the
output is one \emph{scalar} (a single real number, as opposed to a list of numbers).
For that reason the dot product is sometimes called the \emph{scalar product}.
(You will also see it written $\inner{\vec u}{\vec v}$, called the \emph{inner product};
later, in Chapter 10, when we meet row vectors and matrix multiplication, we will also
write this exact calculation as a row multiplied into a column. All these notations
mean the same arithmetic.)

\begin{example}\label{ch04:first-dot}
Let $\vec u = \colvec{3 \\ 4}$ and $\vec v = \colvec{2 \\ -1}$. Then
\[
  \vec u \cdot \vec v = (3)(2) + (4)(-1) = 6 - 4 = 2.
\]
And in three dimensions, with $\vec a = \colvec{1\\2\\2}$ and
$\vec b = \colvec{2\\0\\-1}$,
\[
  \vec a\cdot\vec b = (1)(2) + (2)(0) + (2)(-1) = 2 + 0 - 2 = 0.
\]
That zero has a geometric meaning: later in this chapter you will read it as
``$\vec a$ and $\vec b$ are perpendicular.''
\end{example}

\textbf{Mental check.} Work out $\colvec{2\\5}\cdot\colvec{-5\\2}$ in your head. You get
$(2)(-5) + (5)(2) = -10 + 10 = 0$. In general, swapping the two coordinates of
$\colvec{a\\b}$ and flipping one sign gives $\colvec{-b\\a}$, and
$(a)(-b) + (b)(a) = 0$. So this trick always produces a vector perpendicular to the
original one in $\R^2$ (Section~\ref{ch04:orth-sec} explains why a zero dot product
means perpendicular).

\begin{focusbox}
The dot product takes two vectors of the \emph{same dimension} and returns one number.
You can only dot $\vec u\cdot\vec v$ when $\vec u$ and $\vec v$ have the same
number of components. Dotting a vector in $\R^2$ with a vector in $\R^3$ is
meaningless -- there is no rule that pairs up their entries.
\end{focusbox}

Three properties follow directly from the definition, and the rest of the chapter
uses all of them.

\begin{formalbox}
For all vectors $\vec u, \vec v, \vec w \in \R^n$ and every scalar $c\in\R$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Symmetry:} $\vec u\cdot\vec v = \vec v\cdot\vec u$.
  \item \textbf{Linearity in each slot:}
        $(\vec u + \vec w)\cdot\vec v = \vec u\cdot\vec v + \vec w\cdot\vec v$
        and $(c\,\vec u)\cdot\vec v = c\,(\vec u\cdot\vec v)$.
  \item \textbf{Positivity:} $\vec u\cdot\vec u = u_1^2 + \cdots + u_n^2 \ge 0$,
        with equality if and only if $\vec u = \vec 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{formalbox}

A ``slot'' is one of the two positions in $\vec u\cdot\vec v$. Property~2 is
stated for the first slot; by symmetry the same rules hold in the second slot.
``If and only if'' means ``exactly when'': $\vec u\cdot\vec u=0$ happens for
$\vec u=\vec 0$ and for no other vector.

\begin{proof}
Each is one line of algebra. Symmetry: $u_iv_i = v_iu_i$ term by term.
Linearity: $(u_i+w_i)v_i = u_iv_i + w_iv_i$, then sum over $i$; and
$(cu_i)v_i = c(u_iv_i)$. Positivity: $\vec u\cdot\vec u = \sum_i u_i^2$ is a sum of
squares, hence $\ge 0$, and a sum of squares is zero exactly when every term is
zero, i.e. every $u_i=0$.
\end{proof}

Property~3 says that $\vec u\cdot\vec u$ is never negative and is zero only for the
zero vector. That is what allows us to treat $\vec u\cdot\vec u$ as a length
squared, the next topic.

\subsection{Length lives inside the dot product}

Look again at $\vec u\cdot\vec u = u_1^2 + \cdots + u_n^2$. In the plane, with
$\vec u = \colvec{u_1\\u_2}$, this is exactly $u_1^2 + u_2^2$ -- the inside of the
Pythagorean theorem. The hypotenuse of the right triangle with legs $u_1$ and
$u_2$ is the length of the arrow $\vec u$ (Figure~\ref{ch04:pythagoras}). So:
\[
  \norm{\vec u} \;=\; \sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2} \;=\; \sqrt{\vec u\cdot\vec u}.
\]
As in Chapter~2, $\norm{\vec u}$ (the norm) denotes the length of $\vec u$.
In three dimensions, Pythagoras used twice gives
$\norm{\vec u}=\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2}$: once across the floor, then once up to the
tip (Figure~\ref{ch04:pythagoras3d}). In higher dimensions we can no longer draw the
triangles, so we \emph{define} length in $\R^n$ by the same formula. It is the
definition that keeps Pythagoras true in every dimension.

\begin{definition}\label{ch04:norm}
The \textbf{length} (or \textbf{norm}, or \textbf{magnitude}) of
$\vec u\in\R^n$ is
\[
  \norm{\vec u} \;=\; \sqrt{\vec u\cdot\vec u}
  \;=\; \sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2 + \cdots + u_n^2}.
\]
A vector with $\norm{\vec u}=1$ is called a \textbf{unit vector}.
\end{definition}

Because $\vec u\cdot\vec u \ge 0$, that square root is always of a nonnegative
number, so $\norm{\vec u}$ is a well-defined real number $\ge 0$, and it equals $0$
only for $\vec u = \vec 0$.

\begin{example}
$\norm{\colvec{3\\4}} = \sqrt{9+16} = 5$. \quad
$\norm{\colvec{1\\2\\2}} = \sqrt{1+4+4} = 3$. \quad
$\norm{\colvec{1\\1\\1\\1}} = \sqrt{4} = 2$.
The last one is a useful sanity check: the all-ones vector in $\R^n$ has length
$\sqrt n$, which grows without bound as the dimension does.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
The dot product $\vec u\cdot\vec u$ is length \emph{squared}, not length. A common
mistake is to forget the square root, or to take the square root of each squared
coordinate separately. There is exactly one square root, and it goes
\emph{around the whole sum}: $\norm{\vec u}=\sqrt{u_1^2+\cdots+u_n^2}$, never
$\abs{u_1}+\cdots+\abs{u_n}$. (That sum of absolute values is a different measure of
size, sometimes called the ``taxicab'' length because it adds up distances along the
grid; it is not the length we get from the dot product.)
\end{trapbox}

Scaling a vector scales its length by the
\emph{absolute value} of the scalar. For any $c\in\R$,
\[
  \norm{c\,\vec u} = \sqrt{(c\vec u)\cdot(c\vec u)}
                   = \sqrt{c^2\,(\vec u\cdot\vec u)}
                   = \abs{c}\,\norm{\vec u}.
\]
Doubling an arrow doubles its length; negating it ($c=-1$) flips its direction but
leaves its length unchanged.

\section{The geometric meaning: length times length times cosine}

We now have an \emph{algebraic} number, $\vec u\cdot\vec v$, and we have used the
special case $\vec u\cdot\vec u$ to recover length. But what does the general dot
product of two \emph{different} vectors mean geometrically? The answer is
\[
  \boxed{\;\vec u\cdot\vec v \;=\; \norm{\vec u}\,\norm{\vec v}\,\cos\theta\;}
\]
where $\theta$ is the angle between the two arrows when both start at the same
point, measured between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$. The next subsection shows that
this formula is the law of cosines from trigonometry, rewritten with dot products.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The dot product measures \emph{how much two arrows point the same way}, weighted by
how long they are. If they point in nearly the same direction, $\cos\theta$ is near
$+1$ and the dot product is large and positive. If they are perpendicular,
$\cos\theta = 0$ and the dot product is $0$. If they point in opposite directions,
$\cos\theta$ is near $-1$ and the dot product is large and negative. The sign of
$\vec u\cdot\vec v$ alone tells you whether the angle between the arrows is acute
(less than $90^\circ$), right (exactly $90^\circ$), or obtuse (more than $90^\circ$);
see Figure~\ref{ch04:sign}.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Deriving the cosine formula from the law of cosines}

Place both arrows tail-to-tail at the origin. The vector $\vec u - \vec v$ is the
arrow that runs from the tip of $\vec v$ to the tip of $\vec u$ -- it closes the
triangle whose other two sides are $\vec u$ and $\vec v$ (Figure~\ref{ch04:loc}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.3,>=Latex]
  \coordinate (O) at (0,0);
  \coordinate (U) at (3,1.6);
  \coordinate (V) at (3.2,-0.4);
  \draw[->,very thick,vecblue] (O) -- (U) node[above left]{$\vec u$};
  \draw[->,very thick,vecred]  (O) -- (V) node[below right]{$\vec v$};
  \draw[->,thick,structurepurple] (V) -- (U)
        node[midway,right]{$\vec u-\vec v$};
  \pic[draw,->,"$\theta$",angle radius=0.9cm,angle eccentricity=1.35]
      {angle=V--O--U};
  \draw[fill=black] (O) circle (1pt);
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Two arrows tail-to-tail at the origin enclose an angle $\theta$. The
third side of the triangle is $\vec u-\vec v$, and the law of cosines relates its
length to $\norm{\vec u}$, $\norm{\vec v}$, and $\cos\theta$.}
\label{ch04:loc}
\end{figure}

The law of cosines from trigonometry says that in a triangle with side lengths
$a$, $b$, $c$ and angle $\theta$ opposite the side of length $c$,
\[
  c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\theta.
\]
Apply it to our triangle, with $a = \norm{\vec u}$, $b = \norm{\vec v}$, and the
opposite side $c = \norm{\vec u - \vec v}$:
\[
  \norm{\vec u - \vec v}^2
    = \norm{\vec u}^2 + \norm{\vec v}^2 - 2\,\norm{\vec u}\,\norm{\vec v}\cos\theta.
\]
Now compute that left side with the dot product, using linearity and symmetry:
\begin{align*}
  \norm{\vec u - \vec v}^2
    &= (\vec u - \vec v)\cdot(\vec u - \vec v) \\
    &= \vec u\cdot\vec u - \vec u\cdot\vec v - \vec v\cdot\vec u + \vec v\cdot\vec v \\
    &= \norm{\vec u}^2 - 2\,(\vec u\cdot\vec v) + \norm{\vec v}^2.
\end{align*}
Set the two expressions for $\norm{\vec u-\vec v}^2$ equal. The
$\norm{\vec u}^2$ and $\norm{\vec v}^2$ on both sides cancel, leaving
\[
  -2\,(\vec u\cdot\vec v) = -2\,\norm{\vec u}\,\norm{\vec v}\cos\theta,
\]
and dividing by $-2$ gives precisely
\[
  \vec u\cdot\vec v = \norm{\vec u}\,\norm{\vec v}\,\cos\theta.
\]
So the algebraic dot product and the geometric ``length times length times cosine''
are the same number, and the law of cosines is what connects them.

\begin{aestheticbox}
The left-hand side, $\sum_i u_iv_i$, is arithmetic and never mentions angles. The
right-hand side is geometry: two lengths and a cosine. The identity says they always
agree. This is why a computer, which only stores lists of numbers, can compute angles
and test whether directions are perpendicular in spaces it cannot draw.
\end{aestheticbox}

\subsection{Solving for the angle}

Turn the boxed formula around to get the angle from the coordinates. As long as
neither vector is zero,
\[
  \cos\theta = \frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\norm{\vec u}\,\norm{\vec v}},
  \qquad
  \theta = \arccos\!\left(\frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}
                                {\norm{\vec u}\,\norm{\vec v}}\right).
\]
Here $\arccos$ (also written $\cos^{-1}$, the \emph{inverse cosine}) undoes the
cosine: $\arccos(x)$ is the angle between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ whose cosine is
$x$. For example, $\arccos(1)=0^\circ$, $\arccos(0)=90^\circ$, and
$\arccos(-1)=180^\circ$. Figure~\ref{ch04:cos-curve} shows why this works: between
$0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ the cosine takes each value from $-1$ to $1$ exactly once,
so the cosine pins down the angle.

\begin{example}\label{ch04:angle-ex}
Find the angle between $\vec u = \colvec{3\\4}$ and $\vec v = \colvec{2\\-1}$
(the vectors from Example~\ref{ch04:first-dot}). We already found
$\vec u\cdot\vec v = 2$. The lengths are
\[
  \norm{\vec u} = \sqrt{9+16} = 5, \qquad
  \norm{\vec v} = \sqrt{4+1} = \sqrt5 \approx 2.236.
\]
So
\[
  \cos\theta = \frac{2}{5\sqrt5} \approx 0.1789,
  \qquad
  \theta \approx \arccos(0.1789) \approx 79.7^\circ.
\]
The dot product was positive, so we expected an acute angle. $79.7^\circ$ is acute
but close to $90^\circ$: the arrows are nearly perpendicular.
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Compute by hand, then sketch each pair as arrows and check that the angle is acute,
right, or obtuse as the sign of the dot product predicts.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\colvec{1\\0}\cdot\colvec{1\\1}$ and the angle between them.
        (Answer: dot $=1$, angle $=45^\circ$.)
  \item $\colvec{2\\1}\cdot\colvec{-1\\2}$. What is the angle? What does the
        value of the dot product tell you before you compute the angle?
  \item $\colvec{1\\1\\0}\cdot\colvec{1\\1\\1}$, then $\cos\theta$, then $\theta$.
        (This is the angle between a face diagonal and the space diagonal of a
        cube.)
\end{enumerate}
\end{labbox}

\section{Orthogonality: perpendicular means dot product zero}
\label{ch04:orth-sec}

The cosine formula gives a simple test for perpendicular arrows. Two nonzero
arrows are perpendicular exactly when the angle between them is $90^\circ$, i.e.
$\cos\theta = 0$. Since $\vec u\cdot\vec v = \norm{\vec u}\norm{\vec v}\cos\theta$
and the lengths are nonzero, the dot product is zero precisely when $\cos\theta = 0$:
\[
  \vec u \perp \vec v \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \vec u\cdot\vec v = 0.
\]
Here $\vec u\perp\vec v$ reads ``$\vec u$ is perpendicular to $\vec v$,'' and the
double arrow $\Longleftrightarrow$ means ``if and only if.''

\begin{definition}\label{ch04:orthogonal}
Two vectors $\vec u, \vec v\in\R^n$ are \textbf{orthogonal} (another word for
perpendicular, from the Greek for ``right-angled'') when $\vec u\cdot\vec v = 0$.
By this definition the zero vector is
orthogonal to \emph{every} vector, since $\vec 0\cdot\vec v = 0$ always.
\end{definition}

The algebraic test ``dot product $=0$'' has one big advantage over the picture: it
works in any number of dimensions, where we can no longer draw the right angle but
the equation still makes sense. Two vectors in $\R^{100}$ can be orthogonal; you
will never picture it, but you can always check it.

\begin{example}
Back in Example~\ref{ch04:first-dot} we found
$\colvec{1\\2\\2}\cdot\colvec{2\\0\\-1}=0$. Those two three-dimensional arrows are
orthogonal: they meet at a right angle in space. A flat sketch on paper would not
show that angle clearly; the dot product confirms it in one line.
\end{example}

\begin{strangbox}
\emph{Before Strang:} when he opens his lectures by saying ``perpendicular means
the dot product is zero,'' you will already own that sentence -- you will have
\emph{derived} it from the cosine formula rather than been told to memorize it.
\emph{After Strang:} this one idea, ``$\vec u\cdot\vec v=0$,'' is the starting
point for several later topics: orthogonal subspaces (lines or planes through the
origin that meet at right angles), the four fundamental subspaces of a matrix
meeting at right angles, orthogonal matrices (whose columns are perpendicular unit
vectors), projections, and the Gram--Schmidt process (a method that turns a set of
vectors into perpendicular ones). Whenever Strang draws two subspaces crossing at
$90^\circ$, the test underneath is the dot product of this chapter.
\end{strangbox}

\subsection{The Pythagorean theorem, reborn}

For orthogonal vectors the dot product gives the Pythagorean theorem directly. If
$\vec u\cdot\vec v = 0$, expand the squared length of the sum:
\begin{align*}
  \norm{\vec u + \vec v}^2
    &= (\vec u+\vec v)\cdot(\vec u+\vec v) \\
    &= \norm{\vec u}^2 + 2\,(\vec u\cdot\vec v) + \norm{\vec v}^2.
\end{align*}
The middle term $2(\vec u\cdot\vec v)$ vanishes precisely when the vectors are
orthogonal, leaving
\[
  \norm{\vec u + \vec v}^2 = \norm{\vec u}^2 + \norm{\vec v}^2
  \qquad\text{exactly when } \vec u\perp\vec v.
\]
This is the Pythagorean theorem, stated for vectors, derived in two lines, and
valid in every dimension: in the left panel of Figure~\ref{ch04:diagonals},
$\vec u+\vec v$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $\vec u$ and
$\vec v$. The converse (the statement run backwards) also holds: if
$\norm{\vec u+\vec v}^2 = \norm{\vec u}^2+\norm{\vec v}^2$, then the cross term must
be zero, so $\vec u\cdot\vec v = 0$ and the vectors are orthogonal. Pythagoras and
orthogonality are the \emph{same} statement.

\textbf{Mental check.} If $\vec u$ and $\vec v$ are orthogonal, how does the length
of $\vec u+\vec v$ compare to the length of $\vec u-\vec v$? In your head:
\[
  \norm{\vec u - \vec v}^2 = \norm{\vec u}^2 - 2(\vec u\cdot\vec v) + \norm{\vec v}^2
  = \norm{\vec u}^2 + \norm{\vec v}^2.
\]
The cross term vanishes again, so $\norm{\vec u+\vec v} = \norm{\vec u-\vec v}$.
Geometrically, the two diagonals of a rectangle have the same length. When the
vectors are not perpendicular, the parallelogram tilts and one diagonal becomes longer
than the other (Figure~\ref{ch04:diagonals}).

\section{Unit vectors and direction}

In the formula $\vec u\cdot\vec v = \norm{\vec u}\,\norm{\vec v}\cos\theta$, the number
mixes two separate geometric ideas: how long the arrows are, and what angle they form.
Often we care only about the \emph{direction} an arrow points, not its length. To
strip out the length, divide a nonzero vector by its own norm:
\[
  \hat{\vec u} \;=\; \frac{\vec u}{\norm{\vec u}}.
\]
This is called \textbf{normalizing} $\vec u$, and the result $\hat{\vec u}$ (read
``$\vec u$ hat'') is a unit vector pointing the same way as $\vec u$, because
\[
  \norm{\hat{\vec u}} = \norm[\Big]{\frac{1}{\norm{\vec u}}\vec u}
  = \frac{1}{\norm{\vec u}}\,\norm{\vec u} = 1,
\]
using the scaling rule $\norm{c\vec u}=\abs c\,\norm{\vec u}$ with the positive
scalar $c = 1/\norm{\vec u}$.

\begin{example}
Normalize $\vec u = \colvec{3\\4}$. Its length is $5$, so
$\hat{\vec u} = \tfrac15\colvec{3\\4} = \colvec{3/5\\4/5} = \colvec{0.6\\0.8}$,
and indeed $0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 0.36+0.64 = 1$. \checkmark
\end{example}

\textbf{Mental check.} If $\vec u$ has length $10$, what is the length of $\frac{1}{10}\vec u$?
It is $1$. What is the length of $-3\hat{\vec u}$? By the scaling rule, $\abs{-3}\norm{\hat{\vec u}} = 3$.
If you double $\vec u$, what happens to its normalized version $\hat{\vec u}$? Nothing:
doubling the arrow doubles both the numerator and the length in the denominator, so the
quotient is the same unit arrow.

For unit vectors the cosine formula gets simpler. If $\hat{\vec u}$ and
$\hat{\vec v}$ are both unit vectors, then
$\norm{\hat{\vec u}}=\norm{\hat{\vec v}}=1$ and the formula collapses to
\[
  \hat{\vec u}\cdot\hat{\vec v} = \cos\theta.
\]
For unit vectors, \emph{the dot product is the cosine of the angle between
them} (Figure~\ref{ch04:unit-circle}). This is the idea behind cosine similarity,
the application at the end of the chapter.

Two complementary readings of the dot product:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\vec u\cdot\vec u = \norm{\vec u}^2$ (length squared), and
  \item $\hat{\vec u}\cdot\hat{\vec v} = \cos\theta$ (cosine of the angle), once you
        have normalized.
\end{itemize}
Length and angle both come from this one operation.
\end{focusbox}

\section{A first look at projection}

This section previews \emph{projection}, which a later chapter treats in full. Ask:
\emph{how much of $\vec u$ points along the direction of $\vec v$?}

Picture shining a light perpendicular to the line through $\vec v$ and reading off
the shadow $\vec u$ casts on that line (Figure~\ref{ch04:proj}). That shadow is the
\textbf{projection} of $\vec u$ onto $\vec v$, written $\proj_{\vec v}\vec u$.

The \emph{signed} length of the shadow is its length with a sign attached: positive
when the shadow points the same way as $\vec v$, negative when it points the opposite
way (which happens when $\theta$ is obtuse). From the right triangle in the picture,
the signed length is the side next to the angle $\theta$, namely
$\norm{\vec u}\cos\theta$. Dividing both sides of the cosine formula
$\vec u\cdot\vec v=\norm{\vec u}\norm{\vec v}\cos\theta$ by $\norm{\vec v}$ turns this
into a dot product:
\[
  \norm{\vec u}\cos\theta = \frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\norm{\vec v}}.
\]
The shadow as an actual vector is this signed length times the unit vector
$\hat{\vec v} = \vec v/\norm{\vec v}$ along the line:
\begin{align*}
  \proj_{\vec v}\vec u
   &= \left(\frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\norm{\vec v}}\right)\hat{\vec v}
    = \left(\frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\norm{\vec v}}\right)\frac{\vec v}{\norm{\vec v}}\\
   &= \frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\norm{\vec v}^2}\,\vec v
    = \frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\vec v\cdot\vec v}\,\vec v.
\end{align*}

\begin{example}
Project $\vec u = \colvec{1\\3}$ onto $\vec v = \colvec{2\\0}$. Here
$\vec u\cdot\vec v = 2$ and $\vec v\cdot\vec v = 4$, so
\[
  \proj_{\vec v}\vec u = \tfrac24\colvec{2\\0} = \tfrac12\colvec{2\\0}
                       = \colvec{1\\0}.
\]
This matches the picture: the shadow of $\colvec{1\\3}$ on the horizontal axis is
$\colvec{1\\0}$. It keeps the horizontal part and drops the vertical part.
\end{example}

\textbf{Mental check.} What is the projection of an arrow $\vec u$ onto itself?
Using the formula, $\proj_{\vec u}\vec u = \frac{\vec u\cdot\vec u}{\vec u\cdot\vec u}\vec u = 1\,\vec u = \vec u$.
The shadow of an arrow along its own line is the entire arrow.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Every appearance of the fraction $\dfrac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\vec v\cdot\vec v}$ later
in this book -- in least squares (the best approximate solution of a system that has
no exact one), in regression (fitting a line to data), in the Gram--Schmidt process --
is this same shadow. The dot product on top measures the overlap of $\vec u$ with
$\vec v$; the dot product on the bottom rescales by the length of $\vec v$, so the
answer does not change if you replace $\vec v$ by a longer or shorter arrow on the
same line.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Two great inequalities}

We have been writing
$\cos\theta = \dfrac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\norm{\vec u}\norm{\vec v}}$ as if it
always made sense. But a cosine must lie between $-1$ and $1$, so the fraction on
the right must never leave $[-1,1]$. That is, we need
$\abs{\vec u\cdot\vec v} \le \norm{\vec u}\norm{\vec v}$. This is the
\textbf{Cauchy--Schwarz inequality}. Below we prove it in every dimension using
algebra alone, with no pictures.

\subsection{Cauchy--Schwarz}

First the picture. Look back at the right triangle in Figure~\ref{ch04:proj}.
The hypotenuse has length $\norm{\vec u}$, and the shadow along the line of $\vec v$ has
length $\norm{\proj_{\vec v}\vec u} = \frac{\abs{\vec u\cdot\vec v}}{\norm{\vec v}}$.
In any right triangle, a leg is never longer than the hypotenuse:
\[
  \frac{\abs{\vec u\cdot\vec v}}{\norm{\vec v}} \;\le\; \norm{\vec u}.
\]
Multiplying both sides by $\norm{\vec v}$ gives $\abs{\vec u\cdot\vec v} \le \norm{\vec u}\,\norm{\vec v}$
at once. A shadow can never be longer than the stick that cast it. When can the shadow equal the stick?
Only when the arrow lies completely flat along the line, so that $\vec u$ and $\vec v$ are parallel.

That picture works in two and three dimensions, where the triangle can be drawn. The
algebraic proof below shows that the same bound holds in 100 dimensions, or in
abstract vector spaces where triangles cannot be drawn.

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy--Schwarz]\label{ch04:cs}
For all $\vec u,\vec v\in\R^n$,
\[
  \abs{\vec u\cdot\vec v} \;\le\; \norm{\vec u}\,\norm{\vec v}.
\]
Equality holds if and only if $\vec u$ and $\vec v$ are parallel (one is a scalar
multiple of the other, including the case where one is $\vec 0$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $\vec v = \vec 0$ both sides are zero and there is nothing to prove, so assume
$\vec v\ne\vec 0$. The idea is to use positivity (property 3): the squared length of
\emph{any} vector is $\ge 0$. Consider, for a real number $t$, the vector
$\vec u - t\,\vec v$, and expand its squared length:
\begin{align*}
  0 \;\le\; \norm{\vec u - t\vec v}^2
    &= (\vec u - t\vec v)\cdot(\vec u - t\vec v)\\
    &= \norm{\vec u}^2 - 2t\,(\vec u\cdot\vec v) + t^2\,\norm{\vec v}^2.
\end{align*}
Read the right-hand side as a quadratic function of $t$:
\[
  q(t) = \underbrace{\norm{\vec v}^2}_{a}\, t^2
         \;\underbrace{-\,2(\vec u\cdot\vec v)}_{b}\, t
         + \underbrace{\norm{\vec u}^2}_{c}.
\]
Because a squared length is never negative, $q(t)\ge 0$ for \emph{every} real number
$t$. So the graph of $q$ is an upward-opening parabola that either touches the
horizontal axis once or stays entirely above it; it can never cross below
(Figure~\ref{ch04:cs-parabola}). Recall that for a quadratic $at^2+bt+c$ the
\emph{discriminant} is $\Delta=b^2-4ac$, and the quadratic formula
$t=\bigl(-b\pm\sqrt{\Delta}\bigr)/(2a)$ shows that there are two distinct real roots
exactly when $\Delta>0$. A parabola that never dips below the axis cannot have two
distinct roots, so $\Delta\le 0$:
\[
  \bigl(-2(\vec u\cdot\vec v)\bigr)^2 - 4\,\norm{\vec v}^2\,\norm{\vec u}^2 \le 0.
\]
That is $4(\vec u\cdot\vec v)^2 \le 4\,\norm{\vec u}^2\norm{\vec v}^2$. Divide by $4$
and take square roots:
\[
  \abs{\vec u\cdot\vec v} \le \norm{\vec u}\,\norm{\vec v}.
\]
Equality means the discriminant is exactly zero, so the parabola touches the axis at a single root
$t_0$, giving $\norm{\vec u - t_0\vec v}^2 = 0$. By positivity, this forces
$\vec u - t_0\vec v = \vec 0$, so $\vec u = t_0\vec v$: the vectors are parallel.
Conversely, if $\vec u = c\,\vec v$, both sides equal $\abs c\,\norm{\vec v}^2$, so
equality holds.
\end{proof}

\begin{aestheticbox}
That proof used \emph{only} the three properties in our Formal box -- symmetry,
linearity, positivity. It never looked at coordinates or drew an angle. So the
inequality holds not just in $\R^n$ but in \emph{any} setting with a
dot-product-like operation obeying those three rules, such as the spaces of
functions from Chapter~1. The same argument shows that the correlation coefficient
in statistics (a number that measures how closely two quantities rise and fall
together) always lies between $-1$ and $1$.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{marginnotebox}
\textbf{A theorem with three names.} Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the algebraic
version for finite lists of numbers in 1821; Viktor Bunyakovsky extended it to
integrals in 1859; and Hermann Schwarz rediscovered the integral version in 1888
while studying minimal surfaces (soap-film shapes). In many textbooks it is
called the Cauchy--Bunyakovsky--Schwarz (CBS) inequality.
\end{marginnotebox}

With Cauchy--Schwarz in hand, the angle formula makes sense in every dimension: the
fraction $\vec u\cdot\vec v/(\norm{\vec u}\,\norm{\vec v})$ lies in $[-1,1]$, so
there is exactly one angle $\theta$ between $0$ and $\pi$ radians (that is, between
$0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$) with that cosine. \emph{This is why we are allowed to
define angle by the dot product even in dimensions we cannot picture.}

\subsection{The triangle inequality}

The second inequality says that a straight path is never longer than a detour:
walking along $\vec u$ and then along $\vec v$ covers at least as much distance as
going directly along $\vec u+\vec v$. In symbols,
$\norm{\vec u + \vec v} \le \norm{\vec u} + \norm{\vec v}$
(Figure~\ref{ch04:triangle}).

\caption{The direct trip $\vec u+\vec v$ is never longer than the detour

\label{ch04:triangle}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[Triangle inequality]\label{ch04:tri}
For all $\vec u,\vec v\in\R^n$,
\[
  \norm{\vec u + \vec v} \;\le\; \norm{\vec u} + \norm{\vec v}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Square the left side and expand:
\[
  \norm{\vec u+\vec v}^2
   = \norm{\vec u}^2 + 2\,(\vec u\cdot\vec v) + \norm{\vec v}^2.
\]
Now bound the cross term. Since $\vec u\cdot\vec v \le \abs{\vec u\cdot\vec v}$, and
by Cauchy--Schwarz $\abs{\vec u\cdot\vec v}\le\norm{\vec u}\norm{\vec v}$,
\begin{align*}
  \norm{\vec u+\vec v}^2
   &\le \norm{\vec u}^2 + 2\,\norm{\vec u}\norm{\vec v} + \norm{\vec v}^2\\
   &= \bigl(\norm{\vec u} + \norm{\vec v}\bigr)^2.
\end{align*}
Both sides are nonnegative, so taking square roots preserves the inequality:
$\norm{\vec u+\vec v}\le\norm{\vec u}+\norm{\vec v}$.
\end{proof}

The triangle inequality follows from Cauchy--Schwarz: the only term in the expansion
that we do not control is the dot product, and Cauchy--Schwarz bounds it. In
mathematical language, the triangle inequality is a \emph{corollary} of
Cauchy--Schwarz (a result that follows quickly from another one).

\begin{walkbox}
Take a walk and turn this over: equality in the triangle inequality,
$\norm{\vec u+\vec v}=\norm{\vec u}+\norm{\vec v}$, means the detour is no longer than
the direct route -- the ``corner'' has flattened out. Geometrically, when must that
happen? Trace the proof: equality forces equality in Cauchy--Schwarz, which forces
$\vec u$ and $\vec v$ parallel, \emph{and} forces $\vec u\cdot\vec v\ge 0$, so they
point the \emph{same} way, not opposite ways. Does that match your picture of when
two arrows lie perfectly end to end along a single line?
\end{walkbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The two properties $\norm{\vec u}\ge 0$ (with equality only at $\vec 0$) and
$\norm{\vec u+\vec v}\le\norm{\vec u}+\norm{\vec v}$, together with
$\norm{c\vec u}=\abs c\norm{\vec u}$, are exactly the rules mathematicians require of
any ``length.'' Anything that obeys them is called a \emph{norm}. A norm gives a
\emph{distance} between two vectors, $d(\vec u,\vec v) = \norm{\vec u-\vec v}$ (the
length of the arrow from one tip to the other), and this distance satisfies its own
triangle inequality, $d(\vec u,\vec w)\le d(\vec u,\vec v)+d(\vec v,\vec w)$. A set
with such a distance is called a \emph{metric space}. Metric spaces are the starting
point of \emph{analysis} and \emph{topology}, the branches of mathematics that make
limits, continuity, and convergence precise.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Modern application: cosine similarity}

Why does any of this matter to a search engine, a recommender system (software that
suggests products, films, or songs), or a large language model (an AI system that
generates text)? Because those systems turn things -- documents, images, words --
into vectors, and then must decide which vectors are \emph{similar}. The normalized
dot product is the standard measure of that similarity, for the reason we just
proved: by Cauchy--Schwarz it is a cosine, so it lies in $[-1,1]$ and says how much
two vectors point the same way.

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Cosine similarity.} Represent a document as a vector of word counts. Suppose
our vocabulary is only \{\texttt{linear}, \texttt{algebra}, \texttt{cooking}\} and
\[
  \vec d_1 = \colvec{4\\3\\0}\;(\text{a math note}),\quad
  \vec d_2 = \colvec{8\\6\\0}\;(\text{a longer math note}),\quad
  \vec d_3 = \colvec{0\\1\\5}\;(\text{a recipe}).
\]
The \textbf{cosine similarity} of two documents is the cosine of the angle between
their vectors,
\[
  \cos\theta = \frac{\vec d_i\cdot\vec d_j}{\norm{\vec d_i}\,\norm{\vec d_j}}.
\]
Compare the two math notes:
\[
  \vec d_1\cdot\vec d_2 = 32 + 18 + 0 = 50, \quad
  \norm{\vec d_1} = 5,\quad \norm{\vec d_2}=10,\quad
  \cos\theta = \frac{50}{50} = 1.
\]
A perfect score of $1$: even though $\vec d_2$ has twice the word counts (it is
a longer document), it points in exactly the same direction, so cosine
similarity rates the two notes identical in \emph{topic}. We want this: a document
should not look different only because it is longer. Now compare the math note to
the recipe:
\[
  \vec d_1\cdot\vec d_3 = 0 + 3 + 0 = 3,\quad
  \norm{\vec d_3} = \sqrt{26}\approx 5.10,\quad
  \cos\theta = \frac{3}{5\cdot 5.10}\approx 0.118.
\]
The vectors are nearly orthogonal, with a similarity near zero, which is what we
want: the recipe is about something else. The same arithmetic, run over vectors with
hundreds or thousands of coordinates (one per word, or one per coordinate of a word
embedding, the lists of numbers that represent meaning, introduced in Chapter~2), is
how modern search, recommendation, and retrieval systems find the items closest in
meaning to your query. In every case the computation is $\sum_i u_iv_i$ divided by
two lengths.
\end{applicationbox}

To see why length-insensitivity is the right behaviour, drop the (here empty)
\texttt{cooking} axis and plot the three document vectors in the
\texttt{linear}--\texttt{algebra} plane (Figure~\ref{ch04:cosine}). The two math
notes $\vec d_1$ and $\vec d_2$ lie on the \emph{same ray} from the origin --
$\vec d_2$ is $\vec d_1$ stretched -- so the angle between them is $0$ and the
cosine is $1$. The recipe $\vec d_3$ has almost all of its weight on the
\texttt{cooking} axis (out of the page), so once that axis is restored the full
angle to either math note is close to $90^\circ$ and the cosine is close to $0$.

\begin{trapbox}
Cosine similarity ignores length \emph{on purpose}. Sometimes that is wrong for your
problem -- if magnitude carries real information (total sales, signal energy, how
many times a user clicked), then normalizing it away throws out the thing you
cared about. Choose deliberately: cosine similarity (angle only) versus a raw dot
product or the Euclidean distance $\norm{\vec u-\vec v}$ (the ordinary straight-line
distance between the tips; both of these depend on length). The dot product gives you both
ingredients; \emph{you} decide which to keep.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Worked summary example}

This example applies every idea in the chapter to one pair of vectors. Take
\[
  \vec u = \colvec{2\\2\\1}, \qquad \vec v = \colvec{0\\3\\4}.
\]

\textbf{Dot product.}
$\vec u\cdot\vec v = (2)(0)+(2)(3)+(1)(4) = 0+6+4 = 10.$

\textbf{Lengths.}
$\norm{\vec u} = \sqrt{4+4+1} = \sqrt9 = 3$, \quad
$\norm{\vec v} = \sqrt{0+9+16} = \sqrt{25} = 5.$

\textbf{Angle.}
$\cos\theta = \dfrac{10}{3\cdot5} = \dfrac{10}{15} = \dfrac23 \approx 0.667$,
so $\theta = \arccos(0.667)\approx 48.2^\circ$. Positive dot product, acute angle:
consistent.

\textbf{Cauchy--Schwarz check.}
$\abs{\vec u\cdot\vec v} = 10$ and $\norm{\vec u}\norm{\vec v} = 15$, and indeed
$10 \le 15$. \checkmark

\textbf{Triangle inequality check.}
$\vec u+\vec v = \colvec{2\\5\\5}$, so $\norm{\vec u+\vec v} = \sqrt{4+25+25}
= \sqrt{54}\approx 7.35$, while $\norm{\vec u}+\norm{\vec v} = 3+5 = 8$, and
$7.35 \le 8$. \checkmark

\textbf{Unit vector.}
$\hat{\vec u} = \tfrac13\colvec{2\\2\\1} = \colvec{2/3\\2/3\\1/3}$, and
$(2/3)^2+(2/3)^2+(1/3)^2 = \tfrac{4+4+1}{9} = 1.$ \checkmark

\textbf{Projection of $\vec u$ onto $\vec v$.}
\[
  \proj_{\vec v}\vec u
   = \frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\vec v\cdot\vec v}\,\vec v
   = \frac{10}{25}\colvec{0\\3\\4}
   = \tfrac25\colvec{0\\3\\4}
   = \colvec{0\\6/5\\8/5}.
\]
Sanity check: the shadow should be shorter than $\vec u$. Its length is
$\tfrac25\norm{\vec v} = \tfrac25\cdot 5 = 2$, which is less than
$\norm{\vec u}=3$. \checkmark

\begin{recoverybox}
That example used every tool at once. The thing to remember is \textbf{one operation},
$\vec u\cdot\vec v = \sum_i u_iv_i$. From it, length is $\sqrt{\vec u\cdot\vec u}$,
angle is $\arccos\!\frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\norm{\vec u}\norm{\vec v}}$,
perpendicular is $\vec u\cdot\vec v = 0$, and projection is
$\frac{\vec u\cdot\vec v}{\vec v\cdot\vec v}\vec v$.

\textbf{Cost.} A dot product in $\R^n$ is $n$ multiplications and $n-1$ additions,
so the work grows in proportion to the dimension $n$; computer scientists write this
as $O(n)$ (read ``order $n$''). This shorthand is called \emph{big-O notation}:
$O(n^k)$ means ``at most a fixed constant times $n^k$ once $n$ is large'', so it
records how fast the work grows and ignores the constant in front. Length costs one dot product plus a square
root; an angle costs two more lengths and an $\arccos$; a projection costs two dot
products and a scaling. All of these are cheap, which is why
cosine similarity scales to vectors with thousands of coordinates and to millions of
documents.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{historybox}
For centuries, length and angle were
considered the exclusive domain of Euclidean synthetic geometry---proving theorems with compass,
straightedge, and classical trigonometry without coordinates. In 1821, Augustin-Louis Cauchy published
his landmark textbook \emph{Cours d'Analyse}, where he proved the algebraic inequality for finite sums:
$(\sum a_i b_i)^2 \le (\sum a_i^2)(\sum b_i^2)$. Cauchy viewed this strictly as an arithmetic fact
about lists of numbers. Bunyakovsky (1859) and Schwarz (1888) later extended it from sums to
integrals, as the reading note beside Theorem~\ref{ch04:cs} describes.

Meanwhile, the geometric meaning of the dot product was hidden inside Sir William Rowan Hamilton's
1843 quaternions---a four-dimensional number system extending complex numbers by combining a real
(scalar) part and a three-dimensional vector part into one number. Hamilton's product of two spatial
vectors (quaternions with zero scalar part) is
\[
  \vec{u}\vec{v} \;=\; -\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v} \,+\, \vec{u}\times\vec{v},
\]
where $\vec{u}\times\vec{v}$ is the cross product (an arrow perpendicular to both $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$).
In Hamilton's system the scalar piece carries a minus sign, which made
computing geometric lengths awkward and required confusing sign corrections throughout mechanics.

In 1881, Josiah Willard Gibbs at Yale split Hamilton's product
in two, defining the dot product $\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}$ as a positive-definite operation
($\vec u\cdot\vec u$ is strictly positive whenever $\vec{u}\ne\vec{0}$) equal to $\norm{\vec{u}}\norm{\vec{v}}\cos\theta$, while treating orthogonal
projection $\proj_{\vec{v}}\vec{u} = \frac{\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}}{\vec{v}\cdot\vec{v}}\vec{v}$ as the
geometric shadow cast by $\vec{u}$ onto the line through $\vec{v}$.

Gibbs' notation sparked the ``Quaternion War'' of the 1890s in the pages of \emph{Nature}. Peter Guthrie Tait, Hamilton's disciple,
attacked Gibbs' separate products as a ``monstrosity,'' arguing that discarding the unified quaternion
product destroyed a precious division algebra (a rare number system where every nonzero element can be
divided by). Gibbs and the British physicist Oliver Heaviside argued that mathematical tools should
serve physics and geometry rather than rigid algebraic conventions. Their side won: Gibbs--Heaviside
vector notation became the standard language of electromagnetism and of physics generally. It also
generalizes better. Quaternion multiplication is tied to four dimensions, while the dot product works
in $\R^n$ for every $n$ and extends to infinite-dimensional spaces of functions.

In high dimensions, geometry behaves unexpectedly. As the dimension $n$ climbs into the hundreds
or thousands, almost every pair of randomly chosen vectors is nearly perpendicular. This is one
form of the \emph{concentration of measure} (the fact that nearly all the volume of a high-dimensional
sphere gathers in a thin strip around its equator). How to arrange $k$ unit vectors in $\R^n$ so that the
largest dot product between any pair is as small as possible---the \emph{spherical code} problem---remains
unsolved in general dimensions. And in modern computing, searching high-dimensional
vector embeddings by cosine similarity without comparing every pair is an active research problem.

The dot product measures lengths and angles between given
vectors, but it does not move space itself. To rotate, shear, stretch, or project an entire space at
once, we need functions that take in vectors and return new ones---the subject of
Chapter~5 (Section~\ref{ch05:matrix-is-function}), where matrices act as linear transformations.
\end{historybox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch04:ex-warmup}
Compute each dot product and say whether the angle between the vectors is acute,
right, or obtuse.
\[
  \text{(a) } \colvec{1\\2}\cdot\colvec{3\\1},\qquad
  \text{(b) } \colvec{2\\-3}\cdot\colvec{3\\2},\qquad
  \text{(c) } \colvec{1\\1\\1}\cdot\colvec{1\\1\\-3}.
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Find the length of each vector and then normalize it to a unit vector.
\[
  \text{(a) } \colvec{6\\8}, \qquad
  \text{(b) } \colvec{1\\-2\\2}, \qquad
  \text{(c) } \colvec{1\\1\\1\\1}.
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Find the angle (in degrees, to one decimal place) between
$\vec u=\colvec{1\\0\\1}$ and $\vec v=\colvec{1\\1\\0}$. These are two face diagonals
of a cube meeting at a corner; does your answer match the $60^\circ$ you might guess
from the geometry?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
For what value(s) of $k$ are $\colvec{2\\k}$ and $\colvec{3\\-1}$ orthogonal? For
what value(s) of $k$ are they parallel?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Project $\vec u=\colvec{3\\4}$ onto $\vec v=\colvec{1\\0}$, and then onto
$\vec w=\colvec{0\\1}$. Add your two projection vectors. What do you get, and why
does it make sense?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Let $\vec u=\colvec{2\\1}$ and $\vec v=\colvec{1\\3}$. Verify the Cauchy--Schwarz
inequality and the triangle inequality numerically for this pair. Are either of them
close to equality? Explain in one sentence what would have to be true for equality.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that for any vectors $\vec u,\vec v\in\R^n$,
\[
  \norm{\vec u+\vec v}^2 + \norm{\vec u-\vec v}^2 = 2\norm{\vec u}^2 + 2\norm{\vec v}^2.
\]
(This is the \emph{parallelogram law}.) Then draw a parallelogram with sides
$\vec u$ and $\vec v$ and explain which lengths the four terms represent.
\emph{Hint:} expand both squared norms using the dot product and watch the cross
terms.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Prove that if $\vec u$ is orthogonal to both $\vec v$ and $\vec w$, then $\vec u$ is
orthogonal to every linear combination $a\vec v + b\vec w$. \emph{Hint:} use
linearity of the dot product. (This fact is used later, when we study vectors that
are orthogonal to a whole subspace.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
A unit vector in the plane can be written
$\hat{\vec u}=\colvec{\cos\alpha\\\sin\alpha}$ and another as
$\hat{\vec v}=\colvec{\cos\beta\\\sin\beta}$. Compute
$\hat{\vec u}\cdot\hat{\vec v}$ directly from the coordinates, and use the fact that
this equals $\cos\theta$ (with $\theta$ the angle between them) to re-derive the
angle-subtraction formula
$\cos(\alpha-\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta$. So a trigonometric
identity follows from the dot product.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Three documents over the vocabulary \{\texttt{cat}, \texttt{dog}, \texttt{stock},
\texttt{market}\} are
\[
  \vec a=\colvec{3\\2\\0\\0},\quad
  \vec b=\colvec{6\\4\\0\\0},\quad
  \vec c=\colvec{0\\0\\4\\3}.
\]
Compute the cosine similarity of each pair. Which two documents are most similar, and
why does $\vec a$ versus $\vec b$ score $1$ even though their word counts differ?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Challenge]
Use Cauchy--Schwarz to prove that for any real numbers $a_1,\dots,a_n$,
\[
  (a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n)^2 \;\le\; n\,(a_1^2 + a_2^2 + \cdots + a_n^2).
\]
\emph{Hint:} apply the inequality to $\vec a = \colvec{a_1\\\vdots\\a_n}$ and a
well-chosen second vector. When is it an equality? (Dividing both sides by $n^2$ shows that the average of the squares is at least the
square of the average.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Challenge]
The angle formula lets us define angle in any dimension. Let $\vec u$, $\vec v$,
$\vec w$ be three unit vectors and let
$\theta_{uv},\theta_{vw},\theta_{uw}$ be the pairwise angles. Argue, using the
triangle inequality applied to arcs on the unit sphere (or the cosine formula and a
little trig), that $\theta_{uw}\le\theta_{uv}+\theta_{vw}$. Why is this the
``triangle inequality for angles,'' and where have you seen its flat-space cousin in
this chapter?
\end{exercise}
