\chapter{The Four Fundamental Subspaces}

In Chapter~14 we learned that every subspace has a size, its \textbf{dimension}: the
number of vectors in any basis. Gaussian elimination splits the $n$ columns of an
$m \times n$ matrix into $r$ pivot columns and $n - r$ free columns. That gave us bases
and dimensions for two subspaces: the column space $\col(\mat{A}) \subseteq \R^m$, of
dimension $r$, and the nullspace $\nullsp(\mat{A}) \subseteq \R^n$, of dimension $n - r$.

A matrix has rows as well as columns. Doing the same with the rows (that is, with the
columns of the transpose $\mat{A}\T$, an $n \times m$ matrix) gives two more subspaces.
Together the four are called the \emph{four fundamental subspaces} of $\mat{A}$: two
live among the inputs, in $\R^n$, and two among the outputs, in $\R^m$. This chapter
builds each one, shows how to read all four off a single elimination, and puts them
together into one picture of what any matrix does to its inputs.

\section{Two spaces for the inputs, two for the outputs}

Let $\mat{A}$ be an $m \times n$ matrix. It takes an input $\vec{x}\in\R^n$ and returns
an output $\mat{A}\vec{x}\in\R^m$. So there are two spaces in play: the input space
$\R^n$, also called the \textbf{domain}, and the output space $\R^m$, also called the
\textbf{codomain}. In this chapter we often call them the \emph{input room} and the
\emph{output room}. Each room contains exactly two of the fundamental subspaces, and in
each room the two are \emph{orthogonal complements} of each other. The
\textbf{orthogonal complement} of a subspace $V$, written $V^\perp$ (``$V$ perp''), is the
set of all vectors that are perpendicular to every vector in $V$. For our pairs this means
two things: every vector in one subspace meets every vector in the other at a right
angle, and between them the two subspaces account for the whole room, so their
dimensions add up to the dimension of the room.

\begin{focusbox}
The four fundamental subspaces of an $m \times n$ matrix $\mat{A}$ are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \textbf{column space} $\col(\mat{A}) \subseteq \R^m$ -- all reachable outputs $\mat{A}\vec{x}$;
  \item the \textbf{nullspace} $\nullsp(\mat{A}) \subseteq \R^n$ -- all inputs mapped to zero by $\mat{A}$;
  \item the \textbf{row space} $\row(\mat{A}) = \col(\mat{A}\T) \subseteq \R^n$ -- the span of the rows;
  \item the \textbf{left nullspace} $\nullsp(\mat{A}\T) \subseteq \R^m$ -- all vectors $\vec{y}$ with $\mat{A}\T\vec{y}=\vec{0}$; these turn out to be exactly the vectors perpendicular to $\col(\mat{A})$.
\end{itemize}
Two live in the input room $\R^n$: the row space and the nullspace.
Two live in the output room $\R^m$: the column space and the left nullspace.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Here is the picture this chapter builds. Inside $\R^n$, every input vector splits in
exactly one way into two perpendicular pieces: one in the row space and one in the
nullspace. $\mat{A}$ sends the nullspace piece to $\vec{0}$. It carries the row space
onto the column space \emph{one-to-one} (different inputs give different outputs) and
\emph{onto} (every vector of the column space is reached). In $\R^m$, every vector splits
into a piece in the column space and a piece in the left nullspace; a target with a
nonzero left-nullspace piece cannot be reached by $\mat{A}$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{The column space: where the outputs live}

Recall the definition. The column space is everything you can build out of the columns of
$\mat{A}$:
\[
  \col(\mat{A}) \;=\; \set{\,\mat{A}\vec{x} : \vec{x}\in\R^n\,}
  \;=\; \spn(\text{columns of }\mat{A}) \;\subseteq\; \R^m.
\]
The equation $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ has a solution exactly when $\vec{b}$ lies in this space.
The column space is the set of \emph{achievable right-hand sides}.

\begin{example}\label{ch15:colspace-rank2}
Take
\[
  \mat{A} \;=\;
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3\\ 2 & 4 & 8\\ 1 & 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Column~2 is exactly $2\times$ column~1, so it adds nothing new. Columns~1 and~3 point in
different directions. The column space is therefore the plane through the origin in $\R^3$ spanned by
$\colvec{1\\2\\1}$ and $\colvec{3\\8\\5}$. It is two-dimensional: a flat sheet inside three-space.
The vector $\colvec{0\\0\\1}$, say, is \emph{not} on that sheet, so $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\colvec{0\\0\\1}$
has no solution.
\end{example}

How did we know columns~1 and~3 were the ``real'' ones? Elimination tells us. The pivot columns
of $\mat{A}$ -- the columns that end up holding a pivot after row reduction -- are a basis for the
column space.

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Basis for the column space.} Row-reduce $\mat{A}$ to find which columns contain pivots.
The corresponding columns \emph{of the original $\mat{A}$} form a basis for $\col(\mat{A})$.
Therefore $\dimn \col(\mat{A}) = r$, the number of pivots, which we call the \textbf{rank}.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{trapbox}
A common mistake: after row reduction you must take the pivot columns of the
\emph{original} matrix, not the pivot columns of the reduced matrix. Row operations change the
column space, because mixing the rows moves the columns around in $\R^m$. What row operations
\emph{do} preserve is \emph{which} columns are dependent on which -- so the \emph{positions} of the
pivots are trustworthy, but the actual basis vectors must come from $\mat{A}$ itself.
\end{trapbox}

Here is the trap in miniature.

\begin{labbox}
Reduce $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$. You get
$\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$. The pivot is in column~1. The column space of the reduced
matrix is the span of $\colvec{1\\0}$ (the $x$-axis). But the column space of $\mat{A}$ is the
span of $\colvec{1\\1}$ (the diagonal line). Those are different lines: they share the same dimension
($r=1$) and the same pivot positions, but you must read the basis vector $\colvec{1\\1}$ off the
original $\mat{A}$. Figure~\ref{ch15:colspace-trap} shows the two lines.
\end{labbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose $\mat{A}$ is a $4 \times 3$ matrix. What is the largest possible
dimension its column space can have? Could it ever fill the output room $\R^4$?
\vspace{0.3em}

The column space is spanned by $3$ columns living in $\R^4$. By Corollary~\ref{ch14:cor-count}(b),
fewer than $4$ vectors cannot span a $4$-dimensional space, so $\dimn \col(\mat{A}) \le 3$. So $\col(\mat{A})$ is
at most a 3D subspace inside $\R^4$. There is \emph{always} at least one dimension of unreachable
space left over in the output room.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{The nullspace: inputs mapped to zero}

The nullspace collects every input that $\mat{A}$ sends to zero:
\[
  \nullsp(\mat{A}) \;=\; \set{\,\vec{x}\in\R^n : \mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{0}\,} \;\subseteq\; \R^n.
\]
This is a subspace because if $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{0}$ and $\mat{A}\vec{y}=\vec{0}$ then
$\mat{A}(c\vec{x}+d\vec{y})=\vec{0}$ too. Geometrically, moving along a nullspace
direction does not change the output: $\mat{A}(\vec{x} + \vec{n}) = \mat{A}\vec{x}$
for any $\vec{n} \in \nullsp(\mat{A})$.

We find a basis by solving $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{0}$ with Gaussian elimination.
Setting each free variable to $1$ while setting all other free variables to $0$
produces one \emph{special solution}, and the special solutions form a basis for
$\nullsp(\mat{A})$.

\begin{example}\label{ch15:nullspace-special}
Continue with $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&8\\1&2&5\end{bmatrix}$ from
Example~\ref{ch15:colspace-rank2}. Eliminate:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&8\\1&2&5\end{bmatrix}
  \;\xrightarrow{R_2-2R_1,\,R_3-R_1}\;
  \begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\0&0&2\\0&0&2\end{bmatrix}
  \;\xrightarrow{R_3-R_2}\;
  \begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\0&0&2\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Pivots sit in columns~1 and~3; column~2 is free. Set the free variable $x_2 = 1$. Back-substitute:
the second row gives $2x_3=0$, so $x_3=0$; the first row gives $x_1+2x_2+3x_3=0$, so $x_1=-2$. The
special solution is
\[
  \vec{s} = \colvec{-2\\1\\0},
  \qquad
  \nullsp(\mat{A}) = \spn\set[\big]{\colvec{-2\\1\\0}}.
\]
Check:
\[
  \mat{A}\vec{s}=\colvec{1\cdot(-2)+2\cdot1+3\cdot0\\ 2\cdot(-2)+4\cdot1+8\cdot0\\
  1\cdot(-2)+2\cdot1+5\cdot0}=\colvec{0\\0\\0}.
\]
One free variable, so the nullspace is a single
line in $\R^3$: $\dimn\nullsp(\mat{A})=1$.
\end{example}

Count what happened. There were $n=3$ columns. Two held pivots ($r=2$), one
was free ($n-r=1$), and that free variable became the one dimension of the nullspace. The same
count works for every matrix.

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Dimension of the nullspace.} For an $m\times n$ matrix of rank $r$, the number of free
variables is $n-r$, and each gives a special solution. Chapter~12 showed that the special
solutions span the nullspace. They are also independent: each special solution has a $1$ in its
own free-variable coordinate and a $0$ in all other free-variable coordinates, so none is a
combination of the others. So the $n-r$ special solutions form a basis, and
\[
  \dimn \nullsp(\mat{A}) \;=\; n - r.
\]
This number is called the \textbf{nullity} of $\mat{A}$, and the identity
$\text{rank} + \text{nullity} = n$ is the \textbf{rank--nullity theorem}.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{walkbox}
Suppose $\mat{A}$ is a $3\times 5$ matrix. On a walk, picture its $5$-dimensional input
space: if $\mat{A}$ crushes a $2$-dimensional plane to zero ($\dimn\nullsp(\mat{A})=2$),
how many independent input directions survive into the output? Could $\nullsp(\mat{A})$
ever be only $1$-dimensional for a $3\times 5$ matrix, or do $3$ rows force at least $2$
free variables?
\end{walkbox}

\section{The row space: inputs that govern the output}

The rows of $\mat{A}$ are vectors with $n$ components, so they live in the domain
$\R^n$. Their span is the row space:
\[
  \row(\mat{A}) \;=\; \spn(\text{rows of }\mat{A}) \;=\; \col(\mat{A}\T) \;\subseteq\; \R^n.
\]
Each component of the output is given by a dot product with a row of $\mat{A}$:
$(\mat{A}\vec{x})_i = (\text{row }i)\cdot \vec{x}$. If $\vec{x}$ is orthogonal to
every row, every one of these dot products is zero and the output is $\vec{0}$ --
that is exactly the nullspace condition. So the row space and the nullspace are orthogonal
complements inside $\R^n$, a claim we prove in a later section.

How big is the row space, and what is a basis? Here, unlike the column space, the reduced
matrix can be used directly.

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Basis for the row space.} Row operations are reversible combinations of rows, so they
never change the span of the rows. The nonzero rows of the row-reduced matrix $\mat{R}$ span
$\row(\mat{A})$. Why are they linearly independent? Because each nonzero row has a leading pivot
with only zeros below it in that column---the staircase echelon pattern prevents any row from being
canceled by the rows beneath it. Thus the $r$ nonzero rows of $\mat{R}$ form an explicit basis for
$\row(\mat{A})=\row(\mat{R})$, and
\[
  \dimn \row(\mat{A}) \;=\; r \;=\; \dimn\col(\mat{A}).
\]
Unlike the column space, you \emph{may} use the rows of the reduced matrix directly.
\end{formalbox}

That last equation says the number of independent rows equals the number of
independent columns. The next chapter treats it head-on, but you have already
proved it -- both counts equal $r$, the number of pivots.

\begin{example}\label{ch15:rowspace}
Same $\mat{A}$. Its reduced rows (from Example~\ref{ch15:nullspace-special}, after scaling the
pivot to $1$) are $(1,2,3)$ and $(0,0,1)$, written as columns:
\[
  \row(\mat{A}) = \spn\set[\Big]{\colvec{1\\2\\3},\ \colvec{0\\0\\1}}.
\]
This is a two-dimensional plane in $\R^3$. The nullspace direction $\colvec{-2\\1\\0}$ is
orthogonal to both row vectors. Dot with $\colvec{1\\2\\3}$: $-2+2+0=0$. Dot with
$\colvec{0\\0\\1}$: $0+0+0=0$. The plane (row space) and the line (nullspace) meet at a
right angle, and their dimensions $2+1=3$ add up to the dimension of $\R^3$.
\end{example}

Figure~\ref{ch15:rowspace-null} shows this split of the input room: the row space is a plane, the
nullspace is the line through it at a right angle, and every input vector decomposes uniquely into a
piece in each.

\begin{strangbox}
Strang writes the row space as $C(\mat{A}\T)$ rather than $\row(\mat{A})$ so that all
four subspaces use just two symbols, $C$ and $N$: $C(\mat{A}\T)$ and $N(\mat{A})$ in
$\R^n$, paired with $C(\mat{A})$ and $N(\mat{A}\T)$ in $\R^m$. That notation makes it
immediately visible why transposing $\mat{A}$ swaps the input picture with the output
picture.
\end{strangbox}

\section{The left nullspace: the unreachable output directions}

The fourth space is the one students forget, because naming it requires a transpose. The
\emph{left nullspace} is the nullspace of $\mat{A}\T$:
\[
  \nullsp(\mat{A}\T) \;=\; \set{\,\vec{y}\in\R^m : \mat{A}\T\vec{y}=\vec{0}\,} \;\subseteq\; \R^m.
\]
Why ``left''? Because $\mat{A}\T\vec{y}=\vec{0}$ is the same as $\vec{y}\T\mat{A}=\vec{0}\T$ -- a
row vector $\vec{y}\T$ multiplying $\mat{A}$ from the \emph{left} and producing the zero row. So
$\vec{y}$ records a linear combination of the \emph{rows} of $\mat{A}$ that cancels to zero. These
are exactly the redundancies among the rows.

Geometrically, the left nullspace lives in the output room $\R^m$ and consists of the directions
the column space cannot reach. If $\vec{y}\in\nullsp(\mat{A}\T)$, then for every input $\vec{x}$,
\[
  \vec{y}\cdot(\mat{A}\vec{x}) = \vec{y}\T\mat{A}\vec{x} = (\mat{A}\T\vec{y})\T\vec{x}
  = \vec{0}\T\vec{x}=0.
\]
So $\vec{y}$ is perpendicular to every possible output. The left nullspace is the orthogonal
complement of the column space inside $\R^m$.

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Dimension and basis of the left nullspace.} The transpose $\mat{A}\T$ is $n\times m$, and
its rank is also $r$: its column space is $\row(\mat{A})$, which we just saw has dimension $r$.
Applying the rank--nullity count to $\mat{A}\T$ (which has $m$ columns) gives
\[
  \dimn \nullsp(\mat{A}\T) \;=\; m - r.
\]
A direct way to get a basis: write the elimination as $\mat{E}\mat{A}=\mat{R}$, where $\mat{E}$
is the product of all the elimination matrices used (Chapters~7 and~8). Row $i$ of $\mat{E}$ lists
the multiples of the rows of $\mat{A}$ that were combined to make row $i$ of $\mat{R}$. The rows
of $\mat{E}$ that produce the \emph{zero rows} of $\mat{R}$ are a basis for the left nullspace.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{example}\label{ch15:leftnull}
For our running $\mat{A}$, the reduction $R_2-2R_1$, $R_3-R_1$, then $R_3-R_2$ combined the rows
so that the final third row was all zeros. Tracking the combination, the third row of $\mat{R}$
came from
\[
  (R_3 - R_1) - (R_2 - 2R_1) = R_1 - R_2 + R_3,
\]
i.e.\ row~1 minus row~2 plus row~3 of $\mat{A}$ gives the zero row. So
$\vec{y}=(1,-1,1)$, written as a column, satisfies $\vec{y}\T\mat{A}=\vec{0}\T$. Check it
entry by entry:
\[
  \vec{y}\T\mat{A}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1\cdot1 - 1\cdot2 + 1\cdot1 & 1\cdot2-1\cdot4+1\cdot2 & 1\cdot3-1\cdot8+1\cdot5 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Here $m=3$ and $r=2$, so the left nullspace is the single line through $\vec{y}$ in $\R^3$,
of dimension $m-r=1$. It is orthogonal to the column space plane: $\vec{y}$ dotted with
column~1, $(1,2,1)$, gives $1-2+1=0$, and dotted with column~3, $(3,8,5)$, gives $3-8+5=0$.
Figure~\ref{ch15:colspace-leftnull} shows the plane and the perpendicular line.
\end{example}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If a target output $\vec{b} \in \R^m$ has a nonzero component in the left
nullspace $\nullsp(\mat{A}\T)$, can the linear system $\mat{A}\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ ever have an exact
solution?
\vspace{0.3em}

Never. Every achievable output $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ lives in $\col(\mat{A})$. Because the left nullspace
meets the column space at a right angle, any vector with a piece in $\nullsp(\mat{A}\T)$ sticks out
of the reachable plane. No choice of input $\vec{x}$ can ever reach it.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
The matrix and its transpose have the same rank $r$, so all four dimensions are fixed by just
three whole numbers, $m$, $n$ and $r$:
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    &\text{in }\R^m: && \dimn\col(\mat{A})=r, && \dimn\nullsp(\mat{A}\T)=m-r,\\
    &\text{in }\R^n: && \dimn\row(\mat{A})=r, && \dimn\nullsp(\mat{A})=n-r.
  \end{aligned}
\]
In each room the two dimensions add up to the dimension of the room: $r+(m-r)=m$ and
$r+(n-r)=n$. Each room splits into a part $\mat{A}$ uses (row space, column space) and a part it
does not (nullspace, left nullspace). Figure~\ref{ch15:dim-bars} draws this count.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Orthogonality: the two spaces in each room are perpendicular}

We have checked the right angles in one example. Now we prove them for every matrix, since the
big picture rests on them.

\begin{theorem}[Orthogonality of the fundamental subspaces]\label{ch15:ortho-thm}
For any $m\times n$ matrix $\mat{A}$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item In $\R^n$: every nullspace vector is orthogonal to every row-space vector. In symbols,
  $\nullsp(\mat{A}) \perp \row(\mat{A})$, and together they fill $\R^n$.
  \item In $\R^m$: every left-nullspace vector is orthogonal to every column-space vector. In
  symbols, $\nullsp(\mat{A}\T) \perp \col(\mat{A})$, and together they fill $\R^m$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Part 1.} Let $\vec{x}\in\nullsp(\mat{A})$, so $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{0}$. Reading $\mat{A}\vec{x}$
row by row, the $i$-th entry is $(\text{row}_i)\cdot\vec{x}=0$. So $\vec{x}$ is orthogonal to every
row, hence to every linear combination of rows, hence to the entire row space. That gives
$\nullsp(\mat{A})\perp\row(\mat{A})$.

For the dimension count: $\dimn\row=r$ and $\dimn\nullsp=n-r$. If a vector $\vec{v}$ belongs to
both $\row(\mat{A})$ and $\nullsp(\mat{A})$, it must be orthogonal to itself:
$\vec{v}\cdot\vec{v}=\norm{\vec{v}}^2=0$, forcing $\vec{v}=\vec{0}$. Thus the two subspaces meet only
at the origin. Choose a basis of $r$ vectors for $\row(\mat{A})$ and a basis of $n-r$ vectors for
$\nullsp(\mat{A})$. Together they give $r+(n-r)=n$ vectors in $\R^n$, and if a combination
$\vec{u}+\vec{w}=\vec{0}$ ($\vec{u}\in\row(\mat{A})$, $\vec{w}\in\nullsp(\mat{A})$), then
$\vec{u}=-\vec{w}$ lies in both subspaces and hence equals $\vec{0}$, forcing every basis
coefficient to vanish. By Corollary~\ref{ch14:cor-count}(c), these $n$ independent vectors form a
basis for $\R^n$. Thus $\row(\mat{A})$ and $\nullsp(\mat{A})$ are orthogonal complements: every
vector in $\R^n$ decomposes uniquely into a row-space piece plus a nullspace piece.

\emph{Part 2} is Part 1 applied to $\mat{A}\T$: its row space is $\col(\mat{A})$ and its nullspace
is $\nullsp(\mat{A}\T)$, both living in $\R^m$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
To remember which pairs are perpendicular: the nullspace is defined by
``dot with every row gives zero,'' so the nullspace is whatever is left after you remove the row
space -- they are complements. Likewise the left nullspace is ``dot with every column gives zero,''
so it is the complement of the column space. Rows pair with the nullspace; columns pair with the
left nullspace. The output ($\col$) and the unreachable directions ($\nullsp(\mat{A}\T)$) carve up
$\R^m$; the listened-to inputs ($\row$) and the ignored inputs ($\nullsp$) carve up $\R^n$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The statement ``the two spaces are orthogonal complements and together fill the room'' is
sometimes called the \emph{Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, Part 2}. It has a counterpart in
analysis. In functional analysis---the branch of mathematics that studies calculus on
infinite-dimensional function spaces---the same statement is the \emph{Fredholm alternative}: the
equation $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ is solvable precisely when $\vec{b}$ is orthogonal to every
solution of the adjoint homogeneous problem $\mat{A}\T\vec{y}=\vec{0}$ (the \emph{adjoint} is the
general version of the transpose, Chapter~10, and \emph{homogeneous} means the right-hand side is
zero). For matrices that condition reads ``$\vec{b}\perp$ left nullspace, i.e.\
$\vec{b}\in\col(\mat{A})$.'' The same four-subspace reasoning carries over to differential
equations, where it tells you when a differential equation with given data has a solution.
\end{connectionbox}

Two facts drive everything in Figure~\ref{ch15:bigpicture}. First, \textbf{the nullspace maps to zero}
by definition. Second, \textbf{the row space maps invertibly onto the column space}. Take any input $\vec{x}\in\R^n$ and split it (uniquely, by
Theorem~\ref{ch15:ortho-thm}) as
\[
  \vec{x} = \vec{x}_{\text{row}} + \vec{x}_{\text{null}},
  \qquad \vec{x}_{\text{row}}\in\row(\mat{A}),\ \ \vec{x}_{\text{null}}\in\nullsp(\mat{A}).
\]
Then
\[
  \mat{A}\vec{x} = \mat{A}\vec{x}_{\text{row}} + \mat{A}\vec{x}_{\text{null}}
  = \mat{A}\vec{x}_{\text{row}} + \vec{0} = \mat{A}\vec{x}_{\text{row}}.
\]
So the output is entirely determined by the row-space component of the input: the nullspace
component does not affect the output. The map ``$\vec{x}_{\text{row}}\mapsto\mat{A}\vec{x}_{\text{row}}$''
goes from the row space (dimension $r$) to the column space (dimension $r$).

An \textbf{isomorphism} is a linear map that is both one-to-one (different inputs never collide)
and onto (every vector in the target space is reached). Such a map can be undone: each output
comes from exactly one input, so there is an inverse map.

\begin{theorem}[Row space to column space is an isomorphism]\label{ch15:iso-thm}
Restricted to the row space (that is, fed only inputs from $\row(\mat{A})$), $\mat{A}$ is an
isomorphism from $\row(\mat{A})$ onto
$\col(\mat{A})$: every output in $\col(\mat{A})$ comes from exactly one vector in $\row(\mat{A})$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Onto:} every output $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ equals $\mat{A}\vec{x}_{\text{row}}$ by the splitting
above, so every element of the column space is hit by some row-space vector.

\emph{One-to-one:} suppose two row-space vectors $\vec{u},\vec{v}\in\row(\mat{A})$ give the same
output, $\mat{A}\vec{u}=\mat{A}\vec{v}$. Then $\mat{A}(\vec{u}-\vec{v})=\vec{0}$, so
$\vec{u}-\vec{v}\in\nullsp(\mat{A})$. But $\vec{u}-\vec{v}$ is also in the row space (a difference
of row-space vectors). A vector in both the row space and the nullspace is orthogonal to itself,
so $\vec{u}-\vec{v}=\vec{0}$, i.e. $\vec{u}=\vec{v}$. Distinct row-space vectors give distinct
outputs.
\end{proof}

\begin{focusbox}
Every matrix, square or not, becomes invertible once you cut away the parts it does not use:
feed it only inputs from the row space and look only at outputs in the column space. On those
two subspaces $\mat{A}$ is one-to-one and onto from $\row(\mat{A})$ to $\col(\mat{A})$, and the
rank $r$ is the dimension of both.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}\label{ch15:splitting}
Watch the splitting in action with our $\mat{A}$. Take the input $\vec{x}=\colvec{0\\1\\0}$
(just turning on the free variable, plus nothing else). Decompose it. The nullspace direction is
$\vec{n}=\colvec{-2\\1\\0}$ with $\norm{\vec{n}}^2=5$. Project $\vec{x}$ onto it (using Chapter~4's
line-projection formula):
\[
  \vec{x}_{\text{null}} = \frac{\vec{x}\cdot\vec{n}}{\vec{n}\cdot\vec{n}}\,\vec{n}
  = \frac{(0)(-2)+(1)(1)+0}{5}\colvec{-2\\1\\0}
  = \frac{1}{5}\colvec{-2\\1\\0}.
\]
Then
\[
  \vec{x}_{\text{row}} = \vec{x}-\vec{x}_{\text{null}}
  = \colvec{0\\1\\0}-\tfrac{1}{5}\colvec{-2\\1\\0} = \tfrac{1}{5}\colvec{2\\4\\0}.
\]
This vector lies in the row space: it is $\tfrac{2}{5}\colvec{1\\2\\0}$, and
$\colvec{1\\2\\0}$ is the first reduced row minus $3$ times the second. It is also orthogonal to
$\vec{n}$: $(2)(-2)+(4)(1)+0=0$. Now apply $\mat{A}$:
\[
  \mat{A}\vec{x} = \mat{A}\colvec{0\\1\\0}=\colvec{2\\4\\2},\qquad
  \mat{A}\vec{x}_{\text{row}} = \mat{A}\cdot\tfrac15\colvec{2\\4\\0}
  =\tfrac15\colvec{2\cdot1+4\cdot2\\2\cdot2+4\cdot4\\2\cdot1+4\cdot2}=\tfrac15\colvec{10\\20\\10}
  =\colvec{2\\4\\2}.
\]
The two outputs are equal. The nullspace part contributed nothing; the row-space part did all the
work, as Theorem~\ref{ch15:iso-thm} and the splitting above say.
\end{example}

\section{Reading all four bases off one elimination}

One row reduction gives bases for all four subspaces. Here is the recipe in one place.

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{The one-elimination recipe.} Reduce $\mat{A}$ (tracking operations in $\mat{E}$ so that
$\mat{E}\mat{A}=\mat{R}$). Let the pivots sit in $r$ columns.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Column space} ($\dim r$, in $\R^m$): the pivot columns of the \emph{original}
  $\mat{A}$.
  \item \textbf{Row space} ($\dim r$, in $\R^n$): the nonzero rows of $\mat{R}$.
  \item \textbf{Nullspace} ($\dim n-r$, in $\R^n$): the special solutions, one per free column.
  \item \textbf{Left nullspace} ($\dim m-r$, in $\R^m$): the rows of $\mat{E}$ that produced the
  zero rows of $\mat{R}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{formalbox}

\begin{labbox}
Try the full recipe on
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 1\\ 2 & 4 & 3 & 4\\ 1 & 2 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
  \quad (m=3,\ n=4).
\]
Eliminate to $\mat{R}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&0&-1\\0&0&1&2\\0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}$ (pivots in columns 1
and 3, so $r=2$). Now read off all four:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Column space}: columns 1 and 3 of $\mat{A}$, namely $\colvec{1\\2\\1}$ and
  $\colvec{1\\3\\2}$ (dimension $2$, in $\R^3$).
  \item \textbf{Row space}: $\colvec{1\\2\\0\\-1}$ and $\colvec{0\\0\\1\\2}$ (dimension $2$, in
  $\R^4$).
  \item \textbf{Nullspace}: free columns are 2 and 4. Setting $x_2=1$ (and the other free variable $x_4=0$) gives
  $\colvec{-2\\1\\0\\0}$; setting $x_4=1$ (and $x_2=0$) gives $\colvec{1\\0\\-2\\1}$ (dimension $2$, in $\R^4$).
  \item \textbf{Left nullspace}: one zero row, so $\dim 1$ in $\R^3$. The combination
  $R_1-R_2+R_3$ of the original rows is zero, giving $\colvec{1\\-1\\1}$. This is the third row
  of $\mat{E}$.
\end{itemize}
Check the dimensions: $r+(n-r)=2+2=4$ fills $\R^4$, and $r+(m-r)=2+1=3$ fills $\R^3$.
Figure~\ref{ch15:recipe-fig} shows where in $\mat{E}\mat{A}=\mat{R}$ each basis was read off.
\end{labbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Watch your rooms. The row space and nullspace are sets of $4$-component vectors (they live in the
\emph{input} space $\R^4$); the column space and left nullspace are $3$-component vectors (they live
in the \emph{output} space $\R^3$). A frequent error is to try to ``check orthogonality'' between
the column space and the nullspace -- but those two live in different rooms and cannot even be
dotted together. Orthogonality is always \emph{within} a room: row $\perp$ null in $\R^n$, column
$\perp$ left-null in $\R^m$.
\end{trapbox}

\section{A modern application: where data lives and what is unreachable}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Recommender systems and the column space.} Imagine a big matrix $\mat{A}$ whose rows are
movies and whose columns are users, with entry $A_{ij}$ the rating user $j$ gave movie $i$. Most
entries are missing; a recommender system's job is to fill them in. The working assumption behind
systems like the ones that won the Netflix Prize (a public competition, run from 2006 to 2009, to
predict movie ratings) is that tastes are \emph{low rank}: a handful of hidden factors (how much
you like comedy, action, slow-paced films) explain most of the ratings. ``Low rank'' means the
rank $r$ of the filled-in matrix is small compared with $m$ and $n$.

The four subspaces of that low-rank matrix say what such a model can and cannot do.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{column space} (in $\R^m$, one coordinate per movie) is the set of rating
  patterns the model can produce. If a real user's true ratings lie outside this $r$-dimensional
  column space, no choice of factors reproduces them exactly. The part of their ratings that sticks
  out lies in the \textbf{left nullspace}, perpendicular to everything the model can represent;
  that part is what the model gives up on.
  \item The \textbf{nullspace} (in $\R^n$, one coordinate per user) holds the weightings of users
  whose rating columns cancel out exactly. It has dimension $n-r$, almost all of $\R^n$ when $r$ is
  small: most users' columns are combinations of a few others. That redundancy is what lets the
  system fill in a user's missing ratings from other users.
\end{itemize}
Image compression and many data-analysis methods rely on the same idea: keep the $r$ directions
that carry most of the information (a column space of small dimension) and discard the rest.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Least squares and the left nullspace, previewed.} A system with more equations than
unknowns is called \emph{overdetermined}; fitting a line or a model to many data points
(\emph{regression}) gives such a system. It usually has no solution, precisely because
$\vec{b}$ has a piece sticking out of the column space, a piece lying in the left nullspace. The
least-squares method (Part~IV) throws that unreachable piece away by projecting
$\vec{b}$ onto the column space and solving for the part that \emph{is} achievable. The size of
the left-nullspace piece measures how badly the data fails to fit the model.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Worked summary example, all four at once}

\begin{example}\label{ch15:full-worked}
Let
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 2\\ 2 & 6 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
  \qquad (m=2,\ n=3).
\]
Row~2 is twice row~1, so we expect $r=1$. Eliminate: $R_2-2R_1$ gives
$\mat{R}=\begin{bmatrix}1&3&2\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}$. One pivot, $r=1$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Column space} (in $\R^2$, $\dim 1$): pivot column~1 of $\mat{A}$ is
  $\colvec{1\\2}$. The column space is the line through $\colvec{1\\2}$.
  \item \textbf{Left nullspace} (in $\R^2$, $\dim m-r=1$): the operation $R_2-2R_1$ zeroed a row,
  so the combination $-2(\text{row}_1)+1(\text{row}_2)$ vanishes, i.e. $\vec{y}=\colvec{-2\\1}$
  gives $\vec{y}\T\mat{A}=\vec{0}\T$. Check entry by entry: $-2(1)+1(2)=0$, $-2(3)+1(6)=0$, and
  $-2(2)+1(4)=0$. The left nullspace is the line through $\colvec{-2\\1}$, perpendicular to
  the column-space direction $\colvec{1\\2}$ (since $(-2)(1)+(1)(2)=0$). Together they fill
  $\R^2$ (Figure~\ref{ch15:rank1-room}).
  \item \textbf{Row space} (in $\R^3$, $\dim 1$): the line through the nonzero reduced row
  $\colvec{1\\3\\2}$.
  \item \textbf{Nullspace} (in $\R^3$, $\dim n-r=2$): free columns 2, 3. Special solutions
  $\colvec{-3\\1\\0}$ (set $x_2=1$) and $\colvec{-2\\0\\1}$ (set $x_3=1$) span a plane. Both are
  orthogonal to the row space direction $\colvec{1\\3\\2}$: $-3+3+0=0$ and $-2+0+2=0$. The line
  (row space) plus the plane (nullspace) fill $\R^3$.
  \end{itemize}
Every dimension checks: $1+1=2$ in $\R^2$ and $1+2=3$ in $\R^3$. This is a rank-one matrix: all
outputs are multiples of a single column vector, and all rows are multiples of a single row.
\end{example}

\begin{historybox}
From Arthur Cayley and James Joseph Sylvester's work in the 1850s until well into the twentieth
century, linear algebra was taught largely through determinants (numbers computed from the
entries of a square matrix that test whether it is invertible), minors (determinants of smaller
square pieces of the matrix), and Cramer's rule. Rectangular $m \times n$ matrices fit poorly into
that approach, because a non-square matrix has no determinant. Much of the work was long
formula manipulation by hand, which said little about what a transformation does to space.

The geometric view came from physics. In 1903 the Swedish mathematician Ivar Fredholm was
studying integral equations---equations where the unknown is a whole function rather than a list
of numbers, used to describe things like heat flow and electric fields. These behave like linear
systems with infinitely many unknowns, where determinants no longer work. Fredholm proved what is
now called the \emph{Fredholm alternative}: a linear system $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ has an exact
solution if and only if the target $\vec{b}$ is perpendicular to every solution of the transposed
equation $\mat{A}\T\vec{y}=\vec{0}$. In the language of this chapter, the reachable outputs are
exactly the orthogonal complement of the left nullspace:
\[
  \col(\mat{A}) \;=\; \bigl(\nullsp(\mat{A}\T)\bigr)^\perp.
\]
Whether a system can be solved is a test of right angles.

Textbooks kept the determinant formulas for decades afterwards. One obstacle was that the
transpose was treated as bookkeeping---``flip rows into columns''---rather than as the
\emph{adjoint} map of Chapter~10, which carries measurements in the output room back to the input
room. Another was that row reduction keeps the row space and nullspace in $\R^n$ but changes the
column space in $\R^m$, which made the output side harder to see.

Gilbert Strang did much to put the geometry first. In a 1993 paper in the \emph{American
Mathematical Monthly} titled ``The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra,'' he argued for the
two-room diagram---two perpendicular subspaces in $\R^n$, two perpendicular subspaces in $\R^m$,
joined by an invertible map between the $r$-dimensional row and column spaces---as the
organizing picture of the subject.

The four-subspace picture organizes several central tools of applied mathematics: least-squares
data fitting (projecting an unreachable data vector onto the column space); the Moore--Penrose
pseudoinverse (a substitute for the inverse that works for every matrix: it undoes $\mat{A}$'s map
from the row space onto the column space and sends the left nullspace to $\vec{0}$); and the
singular value decomposition (SVD), which finds perpendicular bases
for all four subspaces at once. Research continues. When a data matrix has millions of noisy
entries, it is no longer clear-cut which directions belong to the row space and which to the
nullspace; researchers use randomized algorithms and error bounds (such as the Davis--Kahan
theorem, which bounds how far a subspace can tilt when the matrix is slightly perturbed) to
estimate the four subspaces reliably. Extending the picture to \emph{tensors} (grids of numbers
with three or more indices, beyond the two of a matrix) is hard: matrix rank is quickly computed
by elimination, but computing the rank of a three-index tensor is NP-hard, meaning no efficient
method is known or expected.
\end{historybox}

\section{What we now know}

\begin{focusbox}
The four fundamental subspaces of an $m\times n$ matrix $\mat{A}$ of rank $r$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Subspace & Lives in & Dimension \\
\midrule
Column space $\col(\mat{A})$ & $\R^m$ & $r$ \\
Left nullspace $\nullsp(\mat{A}\T)$ & $\R^m$ & $m-r$ \\
Row space $\row(\mat{A})$ & $\R^n$ & $r$ \\
Nullspace $\nullsp(\mat{A})$ & $\R^n$ & $n-r$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In $\R^n$: row space $\perp$ nullspace, dimensions add to $n$.
In $\R^m$: column space $\perp$ left nullspace, dimensions add to $m$.
And $\mat{A}$ maps the row space one-to-one onto the column space, collapsing the nullspace to
$\vec{0}$.
\end{focusbox}

The next chapter puts the rank itself at the center and states the
Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra in full; most of the work is already done.

\begin{connectionbox}
For any $m\times n$ matrix $\mat{A}$, both the input space $\R^n$ and the output space $\R^m$ split into two perpendicular subspaces. Every input is the sum of a part that $\mat{A}$ carries invertibly onto $\col(\mat{A})$ (its row-space part) and a part in $\nullsp(\mat{A})$ that $\mat{A}$ sends to $\vec{0}$.

The big picture tells us which subspaces exist, how big they are, and that they meet at right angles. It does not yet give a formula for the closest point of $\col(\mat{A})$ or $\row(\mat{A})$ to a given vector (a \emph{projection matrix}), and when $\vec{b} \notin \col(\mat{A})$, knowing that the leftover piece lies in $\nullsp(\mat{A}\T)$ does not yet tell us how to compute the best approximate solution. Chapter~16 proves that row rank equals column rank ($\dimn \row(\mat{A}) = \dimn \col(\mat{A})$) for every matrix, states the complete Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra, and shows how any matrix splits into a sum of rank-one matrices. Part~IV (Chapters 17--20) then builds orthogonal projections and least squares.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental warmup: room and dimension check]\label{ch15:ex-mental-dim}
A matrix $\mat{A}$ has size $4 \times 6$ and rank $r = 3$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item In your head, state the dimension of each of the four fundamental subspaces.
  \item Which two subspaces live in the input room $\R^6$, and what is the sum of their dimensions?
  \item Which two subspaces live in the output room $\R^4$, and what is the sum of their dimensions?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mental warmup: which room?]\label{ch15:ex-mental-room}
Let $\mat{A}$ be a $3 \times 5$ matrix. A student writes: ``We checked that $\col(\mat{A})$ is
orthogonal to $\nullsp(\mat{A})$ by computing the dot product of their basis vectors.''
Without doing any arithmetic, explain in one sentence why this calculation is impossible.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch15:ex-basic}
For $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$, find a basis and the dimension of each of
the four fundamental subspaces. State which room ($\R^2$ or $\R^3$) each lives in.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch15:ex-special}
Let $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&2&1\\2&2&4&3\\1&1&2&0\end{bmatrix}$. Reduce it, identify the
pivot and free columns, and write down all the special solutions (a basis for the nullspace).
What is the nullity?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch15:ex-colspace-trap}
Give a $2\times2$ matrix whose row-reduced form has a pivot in column~1, yet whose column space is
\emph{not} the $x$-axis. Use it to explain in one sentence why the column-space basis must be read
from the original matrix, not the reduced one.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch15:ex-leftnull}
For $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\\3&6\end{bmatrix}$, find the left nullspace directly by
solving $\mat{A}\T\vec{y}=\vec{0}$. Confirm its dimension equals $m-r$ and that every basis vector
is orthogonal to the column space.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch15:ex-dimcheck}
A matrix $\mat{A}$ is $5\times 7$ with rank $4$. State the dimension of each of the four
fundamental subspaces and the room each lives in. How many independent special solutions does
$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{0}$ have?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch15:ex-splitting}
For $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$ and input $\vec{x}=\colvec{3\\1}$, split
$\vec{x}$ into its row-space and nullspace pieces and verify that $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\mat{A}\vec{x}_
{\text{row}}$, the content of Theorem~\ref{ch15:iso-thm}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch15:ex-solvability}
Use the left nullspace to decide solvability. For
$\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, a vector $\vec{b}$ makes
$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ solvable if and only if $\vec{b}$ is orthogonal to the left nullspace. Find that
orthogonality condition explicitly and check it on $\vec{b}=\colvec{1\\2\\0}$ and
$\vec{b}=\colvec{1\\1\\1}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch15:ex-rowcol-iso}
Let $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&3\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$. Identify all four subspaces, then verify
by hand that $\mat{A}$ carries the row-space basis to the column-space basis in a one-to-one onto
fashion (Theorem~\ref{ch15:iso-thm}). What are the two stretch factors?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch15:ex-proof}
\emph{(Proof.)} Prove that for any $m\times n$ matrix $\mat{A}$, a vector in both the row space and
the nullspace must be the zero vector. Then use this to prove that the only vector in all four
subspaces simultaneously is $\vec{0}$ (after accounting for the rooms they live in).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch15:ex-transpose}
\emph{(Symmetry challenge.)} Explain why $\rank(\mat{A})=\rank(\mat{A}\T)$ guarantees that the
column space and row space always have the \emph{same} dimension, even when $m\ne n$ so they live
in rooms of different sizes. Illustrate with a $2\times 5$ matrix of rank $2$: what are the four
dimensions?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch15:ex-application}
\emph{(Modeling.)} A data matrix $\mat{A}$ is $1000\times 50$ (1000 samples, 50 features) and turns
out to have rank $r=8$. In plain language, what do the column space, nullspace, row space, and left
nullspace each mean for this dataset? Which subspace measures ``directions in feature space that do
not affect any output,'' and which measures ``output patterns the data can never produce''?
\end{exercise}
