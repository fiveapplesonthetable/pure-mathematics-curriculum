\chapter{Symmetric Matrices and the Spectral Theorem}

In Chapter~24 you learned to take a matrix apart with its eigenvectors, and in
Chapter~25 you used that diagonalization to solve coupled differential
equations $\vec{u}' = \mat{A}\vec{u}$ using the matrix exponential
$e^{\mat{A}t} = \mat{P}e^{\mat{\Lambda}t}\mat{P}\inv$ (writing $\mat{P}$ for the
matrix whose columns are the eigenvectors and $\mat{\Lambda}$ for the diagonal
matrix of eigenvalues). The central idea was
decoupling: find special directions $\vec{x}$ where the matrix acts as pure
scaling ($\mat{A}\vec{x} = \lambda\vec{x}$), change to those eigenvector
coordinates so each mode (each eigenvector's share of the solution) evolves
independently, and change back. In general this ran into three complications.
First, eigenvalues could be complex, turning plain growth and decay into
spirals and oscillations. Second, the eigenvectors could sit at
non-perpendicular angles, which distorts the geometry and makes $\mat{P}\inv$ a
real matrix inverse that must be computed. Third, defective matrices (too few
independent eigenvectors) could not be diagonalized at all, and produced terms
like $t e^{\lambda t}$ that mixed the modes together.

This chapter studies the matrices for which all three complications
disappear: the \textbf{symmetric matrices}, those equal to their own transpose,
$\mat{A} = \mat{A}\T$. Their eigenvalues are always real. Their eigenvectors can
always be chosen \emph{perpendicular} to one another. So the eigenvector matrix
$\mat{P}$ can be taken to be an \textbf{orthogonal matrix} $\mat{Q}$ (square,
with perpendicular unit-length columns; geometrically a rotation or a
reflection), whose inverse is its transpose, $\mat{Q}\inv = \mat{Q}\T$.
The diagonalization becomes
\[
  \boxed{\;\mat{A} = \mat{Q}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{Q}\T.\;}
\]
The list of eigenvalues of a matrix is called its \textbf{spectrum}, by
analogy with the spectrum of light, where a prism splits a beam into its pure
colors. The statement that every symmetric matrix factors this way is the
\textbf{Spectral Theorem}. It says a symmetric matrix does nothing but stretch
space by different amounts along perpendicular directions. Data analysis,
the study of vibrations, optimization and much of statistics rely on it, as
later chapters show. We start with the picture -- stretching along
perpendicular axes -- and then prove why symmetry forces real eigenvalues and
perpendicular eigenvectors.

\section{What Makes a Matrix Symmetric}
\label{ch26:what-is-symmetric}

A square matrix $\mat{A}$ is \textbf{symmetric} when it is unchanged by
transposition: $\mat{A}\T = \mat{A}$. Concretely, the entry in row $i$, column
$j$ equals the entry in row $j$, column $i$ -- the matrix is a mirror image of
itself across the main diagonal.
Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-mirror} shows the pairing.

\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 & -1 \\ 5 & 0 & 3 \\ -1 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
  \quad\text{is symmetric:}\quad a_{ij}=a_{ji}.
\]
Fold the matrix along the diagonal (the $2,0,4$) and the off-diagonal entries land
on their twins: the two $5$'s swap, the two $3$'s swap, the two $-1$'s swap. By
contrast
\[
  \mat{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\]
is \emph{not} symmetric, because the $2$ above the diagonal faces a $-2$ below it.

\begin{definition}[Symmetric matrix]
\label{ch26:def-symmetric}
A real $n\times n$ matrix $\mat{A}$ is \textbf{symmetric} if $\mat{A}\T = \mat{A}$,
equivalently if $a_{ij} = a_{ji}$ for all $i,j$.
\end{definition}

\begin{focusbox}
Symmetry is a statement about the \emph{matrix}, but its real meaning is a
statement about the \emph{dot product}. The single identity to carry through this
whole chapter is
\[
  \inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\vec{y}}
  \qquad\text{for all } \vec{x},\vec{y}.
\]
``You may move $\mat{A}$ from one side of the dot product to the other for free.''
Every theorem in this chapter follows from this identity.
\end{focusbox}

Why does symmetry mean this? Recall the dot product written with matrices:
$\inner{\vec{u}}{\vec{v}} = \vec{u}\T\vec{v}$. Then
\begin{align*}
  \inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}
  &= (\mat{A}\vec{x})\T\vec{y}
  = \vec{x}\T \mat{A}\T \vec{y} && \text{(transpose of a product reverses the order)}\\
  &= \vec{x}\T(\mat{A}\T\vec{y})
  = \inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\T\vec{y}}.
\end{align*}
So $\mat{A}$ can always be moved across the dot product if you transpose it on the
way. Symmetry, $\mat{A}\T=\mat{A}$, is exactly the condition that it crosses
\emph{without changing}. We will use this move four times.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A general matrix can rotate, stretch, and shear space. A symmetric matrix does
less: geometrically it \textbf{stretches space along a set of perpendicular
axes}, by a different factor along each axis (a negative factor also flips that
axis). There is no rotation or shear on top of the stretching. Think of pulling a
rubber sheet in two perpendicular directions with different strengths.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Where symmetric matrices come from}
\label{ch26:where-from}

You might worry that symmetric matrices are rare. They are common, and usually
for the same reason: a symmetric matrix records a \emph{relationship between
pairs} that does not care about order.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gram matrices.} For \emph{any} matrix $\mat{M}$, the product
        $\mat{M}\T\mat{M}$ is symmetric, because
        $(\mat{M}\T\mat{M})\T = \mat{M}\T(\mat{M}\T)\T = \mat{M}\T\mat{M}$.
        Its $(i,j)$ entry is the dot product of column $i$ with column $j$ of
        $\mat{M}$ -- and the dot product does not care about order. These appear
        in least squares as the normal equations $\mat{M}\T\mat{M}\vec{x} =
        \mat{M}\T\vec{b}$.
  \item \textbf{Covariance matrices.} A covariance matrix records, for each pair
        of measured features in a data set, how much the two vary together
        (Chapter~10). Feature $i$ varies with feature $j$ exactly as much as $j$
        varies with $i$, so every covariance matrix is symmetric. (It is the Gram
        matrix of the data after subtracting the mean, divided by a constant.)
  \item \textbf{Distances and similarities.} The matrix of pairwise distances or
        similarities between $n$ objects is symmetric: object $i$ is as far from
        $j$ as $j$ is from $i$.
  \item \textbf{Second derivatives.} For a function $f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ of several
        variables, the partial derivative $\partial f/\partial x_i$ (Chapter~19) is the rate
        of change of $f$ when only $x_i$ moves. The \textbf{Hessian matrix}
        collects the second partial derivatives,
        $H_{ij} = \partial^2 f / \partial x_i \partial x_j$ (differentiate with
        respect to $x_j$, then $x_i$); it describes how the graph of $f$ curves in
        every direction. It is symmetric because the order of differentiation
        does not matter for smooth functions (Clairaut's theorem:
        $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} =
        \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_j \partial x_i}$). Optimization methods
        use the Hessian to find minimum points.
  \item \textbf{Graphs and networks.} A graph is a set of nodes joined by edges;
        it is \emph{undirected} when the edges have no direction (like
        friendships rather than one-way links). The adjacency matrix of an
        undirected graph is symmetric: an edge between node $i$ and node $j$ is
        equally an edge between $j$ and $i$. So is the \textbf{graph Laplacian}
        $\mat{L} = \mat{D} - \mat{A}$, where $\mat{D}$ is the diagonal matrix
        whose $i$th entry counts the edges at node $i$ and $\mat{A}$ is the
        adjacency matrix; it describes how heat or information spreads across
        a network.
\end{itemize}

Whenever the underlying relationship has no preferred direction from one party
to the other, the matrix that records it is symmetric.

\section{The Picture: Stretching Along Perpendicular Axes}
\label{ch26:picture}

Start with an example. Take the symmetric matrix
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix},
\]
and watch what it does to the unit circle. The circle is the set of all arrows of
length $1$; its image under $\mat{A}$ tells you everything about how $\mat{A}$
stretches space. Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-ellipse} shows the result: the circle
becomes an \textbf{ellipse}. The long and short
axes of that ellipse are \emph{perpendicular}, and they line up with the diagonal
directions $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$.

Check the picture by hand. Along $(1,1)$:
\[
  \mat{A}\colvec{1\\1} = \colvec{2+1\\1+2} = \colvec{3\\3} = 3\colvec{1\\1}.
\]
So $(1,1)$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $3$ -- $\mat{A}$ stretches it by a
factor of $3$. Along $(1,-1)$:
\[
  \mat{A}\colvec{1\\-1} = \colvec{2-1\\1-2} = \colvec{1\\-1} = 1\cdot\colvec{1\\-1}.
\]
So $(1,-1)$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $1$ -- $\mat{A}$ leaves its length
alone. The two eigenvectors are perpendicular: $(1,1)\cdot(1,-1) = 1-1 = 0$.

That perpendicularity is not luck: symmetry forces it, as
Section~\ref{ch26:orthogonal-eigenvectors} proves. The long and short axes of the
ellipse -- the directions along which a symmetric matrix purely stretches -- are
called the \textbf{principal axes}, and they are always at right angles.

For contrast, Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-nonsym} puts a non-symmetric matrix next to
our symmetric one. The non-symmetric matrix also has two real eigenvalues ($2$
and $1$), but its eigenvectors meet at $45^\circ$, not $90^\circ$.

\begin{walkbox}
Consider the matrix $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$. We
found it stretches $(1,1)$ by $3$ and fixes $(1,-1)$. Now feed it a
non-eigenvector like $(1,0)$, which sits halfway between the two axes. Which way
does the output arrow tilt -- toward the axis stretched by $3$ or the one stretched
by $1$? Toward the one with the \emph{larger} eigenvalue, because that part of
the input grows more. Checking directly: $\mat{A}(1,0)=(2,1)$, which tilts toward
the $(1,1)$ axis.
\end{walkbox}

\section{Real Eigenvalues}
\label{ch26:real-eigenvalues}

A general matrix can have complex eigenvalues -- a rotation matrix has eigenvalues
$\cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta$, which are not real and correspond to no
real stretching direction at all. Symmetric matrices never do this.

\begin{theorem}[Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues]
\label{ch26:thm-real}
If $\mat{A}$ is a real symmetric matrix, then every eigenvalue of $\mat{A}$ is
real.
\end{theorem}

The shortest proof briefly uses complex numbers and then returns to real ones.
For a complex number $z = a + bi$, the \textbf{conjugate} (Chapter~23) is
$\overline{z} = a - bi$, and $\overline{z}z = a^2 + b^2 = |z|^2$ is a real number
that is never negative. A number equals its own conjugate exactly when $b = 0$,
that is, when it is real. For a complex vector or matrix we use the
\textbf{conjugate transpose} $\vec{z}^{*} = \overline{\vec{z}}\T$: conjugate every
entry and swap rows and columns. Like the ordinary transpose it reverses
products, $(\mat{M}\vec{z})^{*} = \vec{z}^{*}\mat{M}^{*}$. For real vectors,
$\vec{x}\T\vec{x} = \sum x_k^2$ is the squared length; for complex vectors the
conjugate makes $\vec{z}^{*}\vec{z} = \sum_k |z_k|^2$ a \emph{positive real
number} whenever $\vec{z}\neq\vec{0}$.

\begin{proof}
Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue, which for all we know so far might be complex,
with eigenvector $\vec{z}\neq\vec{0}$, so $\mat{A}\vec{z} = \lambda\vec{z}$. Form the
number $\vec{z}^{*}\mat{A}\vec{z}$ and compute it two ways.

\emph{First way.} Substitute $\mat{A}\vec{z}=\lambda\vec{z}$:
\[
  \vec{z}^{*}\mat{A}\vec{z} = \vec{z}^{*}(\lambda\vec{z}) = \lambda\,(\vec{z}^{*}\vec{z}).
\]

\emph{Second way.} Because $\mat{A}$ is \emph{real}, conjugating changes nothing,
and because it is \emph{symmetric}, transposing changes nothing:
$\mat{A}^{*} = \overline{\mat{A}}\T = \mat{A}\T = \mat{A}$. Therefore
\begin{align*}
  \vec{z}^{*}\mat{A}\vec{z}
  &= \vec{z}^{*}\mat{A}^{*}\vec{z} && \text{(since } \mat{A}^{*}=\mat{A})\\
  &= (\mat{A}\vec{z})^{*}\vec{z} && \text{(conjugate transpose reverses products)}\\
  &= (\lambda\vec{z})^{*}\vec{z}
  = \overline{\lambda}\,(\vec{z}^{*}\vec{z}).
\end{align*}

Setting the two equal: $\lambda\,(\vec{z}^{*}\vec{z}) = \overline{\lambda}\,(\vec{z}^{*}\vec{z})$.
Since $\vec{z}^{*}\vec{z} > 0$, we may cancel it to get $\lambda = \overline{\lambda}$.
A number equal to its own conjugate is real.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
A complex eigenvalue signals rotation combined with scaling. A symmetric
matrix satisfies $\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\vec{y}}$,
and this balance leaves no room for rotation: the matrix can only scale along
directions. Real eigenvalues are the algebraic sign of that pure scaling.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Orthogonal Eigenvectors}
\label{ch26:orthogonal-eigenvectors}

The second property of symmetric matrices is that
eigenvectors belonging to \emph{different} eigenvalues are automatically
perpendicular. The proof uses the symmetry identity
$\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\vec{y}}$.

\begin{theorem}[Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal]
\label{ch26:thm-orthogonal}
Let $\mat{A}$ be real symmetric. If $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\lambda\vec{x}$ and
$\mat{A}\vec{y}=\mu\vec{y}$ with $\lambda\neq\mu$, then $\inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}=0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Compute the single number $\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}$ two ways, sliding
$\mat{A}$ across the dot product using symmetry.

On one hand, $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\lambda\vec{x}$, so
\[
  \inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \inner{\lambda\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \lambda\inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}.
\]
On the other hand, by symmetry $\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}=\inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\vec{y}}$,
and $\mat{A}\vec{y}=\mu\vec{y}$, so
\[
  \inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\vec{y}} = \inner{\vec{x}}{\mu\vec{y}} = \mu\inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}.
\]
Subtracting, $(\lambda-\mu)\inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = 0$. Since $\lambda\neq\mu$,
the factor $\lambda-\mu\neq 0$, so $\inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}=0$.
\end{proof}

In words: move $\mat{A}$ onto the right-hand eigenvector and you get $\mu$; move
it onto the left-hand one and you get $\lambda$. The two answers can only agree
if the dot product is zero. This is why the axes of the ellipse in
Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-ellipse} are perpendicular.

\begin{example}[Checking orthogonality numerically]
\label{ch26:ex-ortho}
Return to $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ with eigenvectors
$\vec{x}=(1,1)$ for $\lambda=3$ and $\vec{y}=(1,-1)$ for $\mu=1$. Directly checking
the dot product: $\inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = (1)(1)+(1)(-1) = 0$. Now a $3\times 3$ example: take
\[
  \mat{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The bottom-right block already exposes the eigenvector $\vec{e}_3=(0,0,1)$ with
$\lambda=5$. The top-left $2\times 2$ block is $\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$,
whose eigenvectors are $(1,1,0)$ (eigenvalue $3$) and $(1,-1,0)$ (eigenvalue
$-1$). All three pairwise dot products vanish:
\[
  (1,1,0)\cdot(1,-1,0)=0,\quad (1,1,0)\cdot(0,0,1)=0,\quad (1,-1,0)\cdot(0,0,1)=0.
\]
These are three mutually perpendicular stretching axes.
\end{example}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: the third direction for free.} Suppose a $3\times 3$
symmetric matrix has three distinct eigenvalues, and you have already computed
eigenvectors $\vec{x}_1 = (1, 1, 0)$ and $\vec{x}_2 = (1, -1, 0)$ for the
first two. Do you need to set up and solve a third system of equations
$(\mat{A} - \lambda_3\mat{I})\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ to find the direction of the
third eigenvector?

No: because $\mat{A}$ is symmetric and the eigenvalues are distinct,
Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-orthogonal} guarantees that the third eigenvector
$\vec{x}_3$ must be perpendicular to both $\vec{x}_1$ and $\vec{x}_2$. In
$\R^3$, the only vectors perpendicular to the entire $xy$-plane point along the
$z$-axis, so $\vec{x}_3$ must be proportional to $(0, 0, 1)$ (or their cross
product $\vec{x}_1 \times \vec{x}_2$). Orthogonality hands you the final axis
with zero row reduction.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-orthogonal} only guarantees orthogonality across
\emph{distinct} eigenvalues. If an eigenvalue is repeated -- say a double
eigenvalue with a whole \emph{plane} of eigenvectors -- two eigenvectors from that
same plane need not start out perpendicular. The fix: within
a repeated eigenvalue's eigenspace you are free to \emph{choose} an orthonormal
basis (Gram--Schmidt does it), since every vector in that plane is an
eigenvector. So you can \emph{always} end up with a full
orthonormal set; you may have to choose carefully inside a shared eigenspace. The
full spectral theorem in the next section guarantees this always works.
\end{trapbox}

\section{The Spectral Theorem}
\label{ch26:spectral-theorem}

Combining real eigenvalues (Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-real}) with an
orthonormal basis of eigenvectors gives the main factorization
of symmetric matrices.

\begin{formalbox}
\begin{theorem}[Spectral Theorem for real symmetric matrices]
\label{ch26:thm-spectral}
Let $\mat{A}$ be a real $n\times n$ symmetric matrix. Then there is an
\textbf{orthogonal} matrix $\mat{Q}$ (its columns form an orthonormal basis of
$\R^n$, so $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$ and $\mat{Q}\inv=\mat{Q}\T$) and a
\textbf{real diagonal} matrix $\mat{\Lambda}=\diag(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$
such that
\[
  \mat{A} = \mat{Q}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{Q}\T.
\]
The columns $\vec{q}_1,\dots,\vec{q}_n$ of $\mat{Q}$ are orthonormal eigenvectors
of $\mat{A}$, and $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ are the corresponding (real)
eigenvalues. Equivalently, every real symmetric matrix is \textbf{orthogonally
diagonalizable}.
\end{theorem}
\end{formalbox}

The most important change from ordinary diagonalization
$\mat{A}=\mat{P}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{P}\inv$ is that $\mat{P}\inv$ has become
$\mat{Q}\T$. A costly inverse becomes a transpose, which needs no arithmetic at
all, because the eigenvector basis is orthonormal. That swap is what makes the
spectral theorem so useful in computation and in proofs.

\subsection{Reading the factorization as a sum of projections}
\label{ch26:sum-of-projections}

Multiply $\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T$ out column by column and a cleaner form
appears. Writing $\mat{Q}=[\vec{q}_1\ \cdots\ \vec{q}_n]$, the product expands into
\[
  \mat{A} = \mat{Q}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{Q}\T
  = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \lambda_k\,\vec{q}_k\vec{q}_k\T.
\]
(This is the column-times-row form of matrix multiplication: column $k$ of
$\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}$ is $\lambda_k\vec{q}_k$, and row $k$ of $\mat{Q}\T$ is
$\vec{q}_k\T$.) Each term $\vec{q}_k\vec{q}_k\T$ is an $n\times n$ matrix of rank one -- it is the
\textbf{projection} onto the line through $\vec{q}_k$ (recall the projection onto a
unit vector $\vec{q}$ is $\vec{q}\vec{q}\T$). So the spectral theorem reads:

\begin{focusbox}
\[
  \mat{A} = \lambda_1\,\vec{q}_1\vec{q}_1\T + \lambda_2\,\vec{q}_2\vec{q}_2\T + \cdots + \lambda_n\,\vec{q}_n\vec{q}_n\T.
\]
A symmetric matrix is a \textbf{weighted sum of perpendicular projections}. To
apply $\mat{A}$ to a vector: project onto each principal axis, scale that piece by
the axis's eigenvalue, and add the pieces back up.
\end{focusbox}

Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-projsum} makes this concrete for
$\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ acting on $\vec{x}=(1,0)$: split
$\vec{x}$ into its components along the two perpendicular axes, scale each by its
eigenvalue, and add the scaled pieces to land exactly on $\mat{A}\vec{x}=(2,1)$.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: testing the projection sum.} What happens if you
multiply the sum $\sum_{k=1}^n \lambda_k \vec{q}_k\vec{q}_k\T$ directly by
the first eigenvector $\vec{q}_1$?

Because the basis is orthonormal, $\vec{q}_1\T\vec{q}_1 = 1$ while
$\vec{q}_k\T\vec{q}_1 = 0$ for every $k \neq 1$. Every term in the sum vanishes
except the first, leaving $\lambda_1\vec{q}_1(1) = \lambda_1\vec{q}_1$. The
eigenvector equation is built into the sum of projections.
\end{recoverybox}

This decomposition is called the \textbf{spectral decomposition}. The singular
value decomposition (Chapter~29) extends it to all matrices. If you drop the
terms with the smallest $|\lambda_k|$, you get a simpler matrix that is close to
$\mat{A}$ (a \emph{low-rank approximation}, since it is a sum of fewer rank-one
pieces); principal component analysis, later in this chapter, does exactly this.

\subsection{The geometry: rotate, stretch, rotate back}
\label{ch26:rotate-stretch}

Read $\mat{A}\vec{x} = \mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T\vec{x}$ right to left as three
moves, as you read ordinary diagonalization -- but now the first and last
moves are rigid (they change no lengths or angles).
Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-rotate-stretch} draws the three moves.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\mat{Q}\T\vec{x}$: \textbf{rotate} (or reflect) space so the principal
        axes line up with the coordinate axes. This step measures how much of
        $\vec{x}$ points along each $\vec{q}_k$; its $k$-th entry is
        $\inner{\vec{q}_k}{\vec{x}}$. Because $\mat{Q}$ is orthogonal, lengths and
        angles are preserved -- nothing is distorted yet.
  \item $\mat{\Lambda}(\cdots)$: \textbf{stretch} each coordinate axis by its
        eigenvalue $\lambda_k$ (a negative $\lambda_k$ flips that axis).
  \item $\mat{Q}(\cdots)$: \textbf{rotate back} to the original orientation.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{strangbox}
Strang calls this $\mat{A} = \mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T$ and sometimes
``$\mat{S} = \mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T$,'' reserving $\mat{S}$ for symmetric
matrices. He summarizes it as \emph{real eigenvalues, orthogonal
eigenvectors}. \textbf{Before} Strang, the picture to hold onto is
Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-rotate-stretch}: symmetric means perpendicular stretching
axes. \textbf{After} Strang: the singular value decomposition (SVD, Chapter~29)
writes \emph{any} matrix, even a rectangular one, as
$\mat{A}=\mat{U}\mat{\Sigma}\mat{V}\T$, where $\mat{U}$ and $\mat{V}$ are
orthogonal and $\mat{\Sigma}$ is diagonal with entries $\ge 0$. It is the same
``rotate, stretch, rotate'' idea, except that the first rotation $\mat{V}\T$ and the
last rotation $\mat{U}$ may differ. When $\mat{A}$ is symmetric with all
eigenvalues $\ge 0$ (positive semidefinite), the two factorizations coincide:
$\mat{U}=\mat{V}=\mat{Q}$ and $\mat{\Sigma}=\mat{\Lambda}$. When some eigenvalues are
negative, $\mat{\Sigma}$ holds their absolute values and $\mat{U}$, $\mat{V}$
differ by a sign in those columns.
\end{strangbox}

\subsection{Why it is true: a proof sketch}
\label{ch26:proof-sketch}

We have already proved the two main ingredients: eigenvalues are real
(Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-real}) and eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are
orthogonal (Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-orthogonal}). The only gap is \emph{repeated}
eigenvalues -- we must be sure there are always enough orthonormal eigenvectors to
fill an entire orthonormal basis, even when an eigenvalue repeats. The argument
runs by induction on the matrix size $n$ (prove the $1\times 1$ case, then show
that the result for size $n-1$ implies it for size $n$). Its main step is
called \textbf{deflation}: take one eigenvector $\vec{q}_1$ out of the problem,
and show that what remains is a problem of the same kind on the subspace
perpendicular to $\vec{q}_1$, one dimension smaller.

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The case $n=1$ is immediate ($\mat{A}$ is a single real number, and
$\mat{Q}=[1]$). Suppose the theorem
holds for all symmetric matrices of size $n-1$. Given a symmetric $n\times n$
matrix $\mat{A}$, its characteristic polynomial has at least one root
(every polynomial has a complex root), and by Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-real} that
root $\lambda_1$ is real. Take a \emph{unit}
eigenvector $\vec{q}_1$ for it: $\mat{A}\vec{q}_1=\lambda_1\vec{q}_1$.

Now look at the \textbf{orthogonal complement} $W = \vec{q}_1^{\perp}$, the
$(n-1)$-dimensional subspace of all vectors perpendicular to $\vec{q}_1$. The key
claim is that $\mat{A}$ \emph{maps $W$ into itself}. To check it, take any
$\vec{w}\in W$, so $\inner{\vec{q}_1}{\vec{w}}=0$, and test whether $\mat{A}\vec{w}$
is still perpendicular to $\vec{q}_1$:
\begin{align*}
  \inner{\vec{q}_1}{\mat{A}\vec{w}}
  &= \inner{\mat{A}\vec{q}_1}{\vec{w}} && \text{(symmetry: move } \mat{A} \text{ across)}\\
  &= \inner{\lambda_1\vec{q}_1}{\vec{w}}
  = \lambda_1\inner{\vec{q}_1}{\vec{w}} = 0.
\end{align*}
So $\mat{A}\vec{w}\in W$. Now describe vectors of $W$ by their coordinates in an
orthonormal basis of $W$. In those coordinates, $\mat{A}$ acting on $W$ is an
$(n-1)\times(n-1)$ matrix, and it is again symmetric, because it still satisfies
$\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\vec{y}}$ for
$\vec{x},\vec{y}$ in $W$. By the induction assumption it has an
orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $\vec{q}_2,\dots,\vec{q}_n$ of $W$. Together with
$\vec{q}_1$, these form an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of all of $\R^n$.
Stacking them as columns of $\mat{Q}$ gives $\mat{A}\mat{Q}=\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}$,
so $\mat{A}=\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T$.
\end{proof}

In short: take out one eigenvector, and symmetry guarantees that $\mat{A}$ keeps
the perpendicular subspace to itself (Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-deflation}). A subspace
that a matrix maps into itself is called \emph{invariant}. Symmetry is exactly
what makes the orthogonal complement invariant, and that is what lets the
induction go through even with repeated eigenvalues.

\begin{aestheticbox}
The spectral theorem says that a web of pairwise couplings -- covariances,
energies, distances, physical links -- can always be separated into independent
actions along perpendicular directions, each a single stretch. The same fact
appears in many settings: a vibrating structure has independent patterns of
vibration (its normal modes), and a cloud of data has independent directions of
spread (its principal components).
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Computing the Spectral Decomposition by Hand}
\label{ch26:computing}

We now run the full procedure on a concrete matrix, so that $\mat{Q}$ and
$\mat{\Lambda}$ become actual numbers.

\begin{example}[Full spectral decomposition of a $2\times2$ matrix]
\label{ch26:ex-full-2x2}
Take
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]

\textbf{Step 1: eigenvalues.} Solve $\det(\mat{A}-\lambda\mat{I})=0$:
\[
  \det\begin{bmatrix} 4-\lambda & 2 \\ 2 & 1-\lambda \end{bmatrix}
  = (4-\lambda)(1-\lambda) - 4
  = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda = \lambda(\lambda - 5).
\]
So $\lambda_1 = 5$ and $\lambda_2 = 0$. Both real, as promised. (A zero
eigenvalue means $\mat{A}$ flattens one direction to nothing -- it is singular.)

\textbf{Step 2: eigenvectors.} For $\lambda_1 = 5$, solve $(\mat{A}-5\mat{I})\vec{x}=\vec{0}$:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -4 \end{bmatrix}\vec{x}=\vec{0}
  \;\Rightarrow\; -x_1 + 2x_2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \vec{x}=\colvec{2\\1}.
\]
For $\lambda_2 = 0$, solve $\mat{A}\vec{y}=\vec{0}$:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\vec{y}=\vec{0}
  \;\Rightarrow\; 2y_1 + y_2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \vec{y}=\colvec{1\\-2}.
\]
Check orthogonality: $(2,1)\cdot(1,-2) = 2 - 2 = 0$. Perpendicular, as
Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-orthogonal} guarantees.

\textbf{Step 3: normalize.} Both eigenvectors have length $\sqrt{5}$, so
\[
  \vec{q}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}\colvec{2\\1},\qquad
  \vec{q}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}\colvec{1\\-2}.
\]

\textbf{Step 4: assemble.}
\[
  \mat{Q} = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{\Lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Verify $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$ (here $\mat{Q}\T=\mat{Q}$, since this
particular $\mat{Q}$ happens to be symmetric):
\[
  \mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}
  = \frac15\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-2\end{bmatrix}
  = \frac15\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&5\end{bmatrix}=\mat{I}.
\]
So $\mat{Q}$ is orthogonal.

\textbf{Step 5: spectral form.} As a sum of projections,
\[
  \mat{A} = 5\,\vec{q}_1\vec{q}_1\T + 0\,\vec{q}_2\vec{q}_2\T
  = 5\cdot\frac15\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2&1\end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The whole matrix is recovered from a \emph{single} rank-one projection scaled by
$5$, because the other eigenvalue is $0$.
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Diagonalize $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}$ by hand, following the
five steps above. You should find $\lambda = 4$ and $\lambda = 2$, eigenvectors
$(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$, and
\[
  \mat{Q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix},\quad
  \mat{\Lambda}=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Then \emph{verify} by multiplying $\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T$ back out and
confirming you recover $\mat{A}$. Finally, write $\mat{A}$ as
$4\,\vec{q}_1\vec{q}_1\T + 2\,\vec{q}_2\vec{q}_2\T$ and check that too. This shows
the transpose doing the work of an inverse.
\end{labbox}

\section{The Spectral Theorem and Quadratic Forms}
\label{ch26:quadratic-forms}

Symmetric matrices are the natural way to write \textbf{quadratic forms} --
expressions like $3x^2 + 4xy + 3y^2$ in which every term is a number times a
product of exactly two variables ($x^2$, $xy$, $y^2$, and so on). Every
such form can be written as $q(\vec{x}) = \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}$ for exactly one
symmetric $\mat{A}$. For instance
\[
  3x^2 + 4xy + 3y^2
  = \begin{bmatrix}x&y\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\colvec{x\\y}.
\]
The off-diagonal $2$'s are each \emph{half} the cross-term coefficient $4$, split
evenly so the matrix comes out symmetric. (Any split of the $4$ gives the same
form; the even split is the only symmetric choice.)

The spectral theorem shows what such a form looks like in the right coordinates.
Substitute $\mat{A}=\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T$ and change to the rotated
coordinates $\vec{y}=\mat{Q}\T\vec{x}$ (here $y_k = \inner{\vec{q}_k}{\vec{x}}$ is
the coordinate of $\vec{x}$ along the $k$th principal axis):
\begin{align*}
  q(\vec{x}) &= \vec{x}\T\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T\vec{x}\\
  &= (\mat{Q}\T\vec{x})\T\mat{\Lambda}(\mat{Q}\T\vec{x}) && \text{(since } \vec{x}\T\mat{Q} = (\mat{Q}\T\vec{x})\T)\\
  &= \vec{y}\T\mat{\Lambda}\vec{y}\\
  &= \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n y_n^2.
\end{align*}
In the principal-axis coordinates the cross terms vanish
and the form is a plain weighted sum of squares. The eigenvalues are the weights.

One way to see a quadratic form in two variables is through a \textbf{level
set}: the curve of all points where $q(\vec{x})$ takes one fixed value, like a
contour line on a map. Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-quadform} shows the level set
$q(\vec{x})=1$ for our example; its axes are the eigenvectors.

optimization, which hunts for lowest points, cares about these matrices.

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{Calculus and the second derivative test.} A \emph{critical point} of a
function of several variables is a point where all its first partial derivatives
are zero, so the graph is flat there. Near a critical point, a smooth (repeatedly
differentiable) function is approximated by a quadratic form whose matrix is
half the symmetric \emph{Hessian} $H$ of second partial derivatives. The spectral
theorem splits that form into independent parabolas along the principal axes of
$H$. The critical
point is a \emph{minimum} when all eigenvalues of $H$ are positive (a bowl), a
\emph{maximum} when all are negative (a dome), and a \emph{saddle} when the signs
disagree -- up along one principal axis, down along a perpendicular one. The
familiar single-variable second-derivative test ($f''>0$ means minimum) is
the $1\times 1$ case, and the multivariable test is the spectral theorem applied
to $H$.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Why Symmetry Is Everywhere: A Modern Application}
\label{ch26:application}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Principal Component Analysis (PCA).} Suppose you have a cloud of data --
say $n$ samples, each a point in $\R^d$ (pixels of a face, genes of a cell,
features of a customer). Center it so the mean is at the origin and stack the
samples as rows of a matrix $\mat{X}$. The \textbf{covariance matrix}
\[
  \mat{C} = \frac{1}{n-1}\,\mat{X}\T\mat{X}
\]
is symmetric (it is a Gram matrix, Section~\ref{ch26:where-from}), so the spectral
theorem applies: $\mat{C} = \mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T$ with real eigenvalues
$\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge 0$ and orthonormal eigenvectors
$\vec{q}_k$.

Those eigenvectors are the \textbf{principal components}, the perpendicular
directions along which the data varies. The eigenvalue $\lambda_k$ is the
\emph{variance} (average squared distance from the mean) of the data along
direction $\vec{q}_k$. The eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue points along
the direction of greatest spread, the long axis of the data cloud in
Figure~\ref{ch26:fig-pca}.

\emph{The payoff:} keep only the top few $\vec{q}_k$ (largest variances) and you
have compressed high-dimensional data onto a few perpendicular axes while losing
as little of its spread as possible. This is the computation behind most
dimensionality reduction (shrinking data with many features down to a few
summary features): \emph{diagonalize a symmetric covariance matrix and read
its principal axes.} PCA's directions are perpendicular and its variances are
real because of the spectral theorem.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{remark}[Why symmetry reflects nature]
\label{ch26:rem-not-convenience}
Symmetry is not a hypothesis chosen to make the theorems clean. Energy, variance,
distance and mutual coupling are symmetric by their definitions, which is why
the spectral theorem applies to them.
\end{remark}

\section{A Gallery of Symmetric Behaviors}
\label{ch26:gallery}

A few small worked cases show the range of what symmetric matrices do.

\begin{example}[Diagonal matrices: the trivial spectral case]
\label{ch26:ex-diagonal}
A diagonal matrix $\mat{D}=\diag(d_1,\dots,d_n)$ is already symmetric and already
diagonalized: $\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$, $\mat{\Lambda}=\mat{D}$. Its principal axes are
the standard coordinate axes, and it stretches axis $k$ by $d_k$. The spectral
theorem says that every symmetric matrix is a diagonal matrix viewed in a rotated
coordinate system.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A reflection is symmetric]
\label{ch26:ex-reflection}
The reflection across the line $y=x$ is
\[
  \mat{R} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},\qquad \mat{R}\T=\mat{R}.
\]
It is symmetric, so it has perpendicular eigenvectors with real eigenvalues. Along
the mirror line $(1,1)$ nothing moves: $\mat{R}(1,1)=(1,1)$, eigenvalue $+1$.
Perpendicular to the mirror, $(1,-1)$ flips: $\mat{R}(1,-1)=(-1,1)=-(1,-1)$,
eigenvalue $-1$. So $\mat{\Lambda}=\diag(1,-1)$. A \emph{negative} eigenvalue
means ``this axis gets flipped.'' By contrast, a \emph{rotation} (other than by
$0$ or $\pi$) is \emph{not} symmetric -- consistent with its complex eigenvalues
and its lack of any real stretching axis.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Trace and determinant as quick checks]
\label{ch26:ex-trace-det}
Two checks cost almost nothing. The sum of eigenvalues equals the trace (the
sum of the diagonal entries), and the product equals the determinant. For
$\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$ from
Example~\ref{ch26:ex-full-2x2}:
\[
  \tr\mat{A}=4+1=5=\lambda_1+\lambda_2=5+0,
  \qquad
  \det\mat{A}=4-4=0=\lambda_1\lambda_2=5\cdot 0.
\]
Both check out. For a
$2\times 2$ symmetric matrix this lets you find the eigenvalues quickly:
they are the two numbers summing to the trace and multiplying to the
determinant.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
A real matrix with real eigenvalues is \emph{not} necessarily symmetric. The shear
$\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ has the real (repeated) eigenvalue $1$ but
is not symmetric and is not even diagonalizable -- it has only a one-dimensional
line of eigenvectors. Symmetry is a much stronger condition than ``real
eigenvalues'': on top of real eigenvalues, it gives an
\emph{orthonormal basis} of eigenvectors.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{historybox}
The Spectral Theorem grew out of mechanics: the study of spinning bodies, elastic materials and planetary orbits. When an elastic solid is squeezed, the internal forces across any small plane inside it are described by the \textbf{stress tensor}, a $3\times 3$ matrix recording the tension, compression and shear (sideways sliding force) acting across each cross-section of the material. Because the internal twisting forces must balance, the stress tensor is symmetric: $\sigma_{ij} = \sigma_{ji}$. In arbitrary coordinates, compression and tension are mixed up with shear. In the 1750s, Leonhard Euler showed that every rigid body has three perpendicular axes about which it can spin without wobbling. Elasticity raised the same question: is there always a perpendicular frame of axes in which all shear vanishes, leaving only pure compression or tension along each axis?

In 1829, Augustin-Louis Cauchy, working on the slow drift of planetary orbits, proved that the eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix of any size are real. His argument is the one in Section~\ref{ch26:real-eigenvalues}: for a complex eigenvector $\vec{z}$, symmetry forces the number $\bar{\vec{z}}\T\mat{A}\vec{z} = \lambda \norm{\vec{z}}^2$ to equal its own conjugate. The case of repeated eigenvalues, where general matrices can be defective (short of eigenvectors), took longer. In 1858, Karl Weierstrass showed that a real symmetric matrix is never defective: the dimension of each eigenspace always equals the number of times the eigenvalue repeats as a root of the characteristic polynomial, so an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors always exists.

The result is the factorization $\mat{A} = \mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T$: a stretch along perpendicular directions, with an inverse rotation that costs nothing ($\mat{Q}\inv = \mat{Q}\T$). Its limitation is that it applies only to symmetric matrices; data matrices and directed networks are rarely symmetric. Also, the eigenvalues may be negative, giving saddle shapes (surfaces that curve up along one axis and down along another) instead of bowls. Chapter~27 studies symmetric matrices whose eigenvalues are all positive, and Chapter~29 applies the Spectral Theorem to $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ to reach the singular value decomposition of every matrix, rectangular or not. In infinite-dimensional settings such as quantum mechanics, the analogue of a symmetric matrix can have a continuous range of ``eigenvalues'' rather than a list; David Hilbert and John von Neumann extended the Spectral Theorem to cover that case.
\end{historybox}

\section{Summary}
\label{ch26:summary}

A symmetric matrix $\mat{A}=\mat{A}\T$ is, geometrically, a stretch
along a set of perpendicular axes. The dot-product identity
$\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}=\inner{\vec{x}}{\mat{A}\vec{y}}$ is what makes
everything work:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Real eigenvalues.} No rotation; only stretching
        (Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-real}).
  \item \textbf{Orthogonal eigenvectors.} Distinct eigenvalues force perpendicular
        directions, and repeated ones can always be chosen perpendicular
        (Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-orthogonal}).
  \item \textbf{Spectral theorem.} $\mat{A}=\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T$ with
        $\mat{Q}$ orthogonal and $\mat{\Lambda}$ real diagonal
        (Theorem~\ref{ch26:thm-spectral}); equivalently
        $\mat{A}=\sum_k \lambda_k\,\vec{q}_k\vec{q}_k\T$, a weighted sum of
        perpendicular projections.
  \item \textbf{Geometry.} Rotate the principal axes onto the coordinate axes,
        stretch by the eigenvalues, rotate back -- with the inverse rotation
        given by a transpose.
  \item \textbf{Cost.} Because $\mat{Q}\inv=\mat{Q}\T$, computing $\mat{A}^k$,
        $\sqrt{\mat{A}}$ or $\mat{A}\inv$ (when no eigenvalue is $0$) reduces to
        ``apply $\mat{Q}\T$, apply the function to each eigenvalue, apply
        $\mat{Q}$'', with no general matrix inversion. Orthogonal matrices also
        keep rounding errors from growing, since they preserve lengths.
  \item \textbf{Why it matters.} Covariances, Hessians, Gram matrices, distances,
        and undirected networks are all symmetric, so PCA, the second-derivative
        test, least squares, and the SVD all rely on this theorem.
\end{itemize}

In Chapter~27, we specialize one step further: what happens when every
eigenvalue is strictly positive? Then the quadratic form
$q(\vec{x}) = \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}$ curves upward in every direction like a
bowl with a single lowest point at the origin, and the matrix is called
\textbf{positive definite}. Such matrices have their own fast factorization,
give new ways to measure lengths and angles, and are the test for a lowest point
in optimization.

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch26:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Warmup: spot the symmetric ones]
\label{ch26:ex-spot}
Which of these are symmetric? For each symmetric one, state without computing what
its principal axes will be (perpendicular, real eigenvalues) and whether you can
predict any eigenvalue by inspection.
\[
  \begin{bmatrix}1&3\\3&1\end{bmatrix},\quad
  \begin{bmatrix}1&3\\-3&1\end{bmatrix},\quad
  \begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\\0&5&0\\0&0&5\end{bmatrix},\quad
  \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}.
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Full decomposition by hand]
\label{ch26:ex-byhand}
Find the eigenvalues, orthonormal eigenvectors, $\mat{Q}$, and $\mat{\Lambda}$ for
$\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}5&-2\\-2&5\end{bmatrix}$. Verify $\mat{Q}\T\mat{Q}=\mat{I}$
and that $\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T$ reproduces $\mat{A}$. Then write $\mat{A}$
as a sum $\lambda_1\vec{q}_1\vec{q}_1\T + \lambda_2\vec{q}_2\vec{q}_2\T$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Gram matrices are always symmetric]
\label{ch26:ex-gram}
Let $\mat{M}$ be any (possibly rectangular) real matrix. Prove
$\mat{M}\T\mat{M}$ is symmetric using only transpose rules. Then compute it for
$\mat{M}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ and confirm the result is
symmetric.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The orthogonality proof, in your own hand]
\label{ch26:ex-ortho-proof}
Let $\mat{A}$ be symmetric with $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\lambda\vec{x}$ and
$\mat{A}\vec{y}=\mu\vec{y}$. Starting from the single quantity
$\inner{\mat{A}\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}$, derive $(\lambda-\mu)\inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}=0$
and explain in one sentence why $\lambda\neq\mu$ forces orthogonality. Where
exactly did you use $\mat{A}\T=\mat{A}$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Quadratic form to matrix and back]
\label{ch26:ex-quadform}
Write $q(x,y)=2x^2 - 6xy + 2y^2$ as $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}$ with $\mat{A}$
symmetric. Diagonalize $\mat{A}$, find its principal axes, and describe the level
set $q(\vec{x})=1$ (ellipse? hyperbola?). What about the signs of the eigenvalues
makes it one or the other?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trace and determinant shortcut]
\label{ch26:ex-trace}
A symmetric $2\times 2$ matrix has trace $6$ and determinant $5$. Without finding
the matrix, give its eigenvalues. Are its eigenvectors guaranteed perpendicular?
Why?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Reflections and signs]
\label{ch26:ex-reflection-ex}
The matrix $\mat{R}=\frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix}3&4\\4&-3\end{bmatrix}$ is symmetric.
Show $\mat{R}\T\mat{R}=\mat{I}$ (so $\mat{R}$ is also orthogonal). Find its
eigenvalues -- you should get $+1$ and $-1$ -- and interpret $\mat{R}$ geometrically
as a reflection. Along which line does the $+1$ eigenvector point?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Functions of a symmetric matrix]
\label{ch26:ex-function}
Using $\mat{A}=\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T$, explain why
$\mat{A}^k=\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}^k\mat{Q}\T$, and why one may sensibly define
$\sqrt{\mat{A}}=\mat{Q}\sqrt{\mat{\Lambda}}\,\mat{Q}\T$ (taking square roots of the
eigenvalues) when all $\lambda_k\ge 0$. Compute $\sqrt{\mat{A}}$ for
$\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ (eigenvalues $3$ and $1$). What goes
wrong if some eigenvalue is negative?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The invariance step, made concrete]
\label{ch26:ex-invariance}
The proof sketch hinged on: if $\vec{q}_1$ is an eigenvector of symmetric
$\mat{A}$ and $\vec{w}\perp\vec{q}_1$, then $\mat{A}\vec{w}\perp\vec{q}_1$ too.
Verify this concretely for $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$,
$\vec{q}_1=(1,1)$, and $\vec{w}=(1,-1)$: check $\vec{w}\perp\vec{q}_1$, compute
$\mat{A}\vec{w}$, and confirm it is still perpendicular to $\vec{q}_1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Challenge: a symmetric matrix from a rotation]
\label{ch26:ex-challenge}
Let $\mat{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}$
be a rotation. It is \emph{not} symmetric (unless $\theta$ is a multiple of $\pi$). But
$\mat{B}\T\mat{B}$ is symmetric -- compute it and identify the result. What are its
eigenvalues, and what does that say about how a rotation stretches lengths?
Connect your answer to the fact that rotations are rigid motions.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Challenge: spectral theorem fails without symmetry]
\label{ch26:ex-fail}
Give a $2\times 2$ real matrix that has two real eigenvalues but whose
eigenvectors are \emph{not} perpendicular, demonstrating that orthogonality is a
genuine consequence of symmetry and not of real eigenvalues alone. (Hint: try an
upper-triangular matrix with distinct diagonal entries.) Then give a $2\times 2$
real matrix with \emph{no} real eigenvalues at all, and explain why it cannot be
symmetric.
\end{exercise}
