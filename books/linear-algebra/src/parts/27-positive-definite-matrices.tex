\chapter{Positive Definite Matrices}

Some symmetric matrices behave like \emph{positive numbers}. Their eigenvalues are positive, the
pivots you meet during elimination are positive, their determinants are positive, and each one has a
square root. These are the \emph{positive definite} matrices. They appear wherever a quantity can
never be negative: the energy stored in a loaded structure, the variance of a measurement, the
curvature at the bottom of a valley.

The spectral theorem of the previous chapter showed that a symmetric matrix stretches space along a
set of perpendicular axes, with one stretch factor (an eigenvalue) on each axis. This chapter asks:
\emph{what if every stretch factor is positive?} The answer ties a large part of linear algebra to
one physical idea, \textbf{energy}.

\section{The energy of a matrix}

\subsection{From a number to a matrix}

Start with a single positive number $a > 0$. What does ``positive'' give you?
Multiplying by $a$ never flips a sign: $a x$ has the same sign as $x$. The function $f(x) = a x^2$
is a bowl opening upward with its bottom at $x = 0$; it is positive for every $x \neq 0$. You can
take a square root $\sqrt{a}$. Every one of these facts has a matrix analogue, and the matrices that
inherit them are exactly the positive definite ones.

For a number $a$, call the quantity $a x^2 = x \, a \, x$ the ``energy'' (a spring of stiffness $a$
stretched by $x$ stores energy $\tfrac12 a x^2$, which is where the name comes from). For an
$n \times n$ symmetric matrix $\mat{A}$ and a vector $\vec{x} \in \R^n$, the analogue is the number

\[
  q(\vec{x}) \;=\; \vec{x}\T \mat{A}\, \vec{x}.
\]

This number (a vector, times a matrix, times the same vector again) runs through the whole chapter.
It is called a \textbf{quadratic form}: a polynomial in $x_1,\dots,x_n$ in which every term has
degree two (such as $2x_1^2 + 2x_1 x_2 + 3x_2^2$), with no linear or constant terms. Here is what it
computes for a $2\times 2$ matrix.

\begin{example}[Unpacking $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}$ by hand]
\label{ch27:ex-unpack}
Take
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  \vec{x} = \colvec{x_1 \\ x_2}.
\]
Compute $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ first:
\[
  \mat{A}\vec{x} = \colvec{2x_1 + x_2 \\ x_1 + 3x_2}.
\]
Now dot it with $\vec{x}$:
\begin{align*}
  \vec{x}\T \mat{A}\vec{x}
  &= x_1(2x_1 + x_2) + x_2(x_1 + 3x_2) \\
  &= 2x_1^2 + 2x_1 x_2 + 3x_2^2.
\end{align*}
The diagonal entries $2$ and $3$ became the coefficients of $x_1^2$ and
$x_2^2$. The off-diagonal entry $1$ appears \emph{twice} (once as $A_{12}$, once as $A_{21}$), and
those two copies combine into the single cross term $2 x_1 x_2$. A symmetric matrix is exactly a
bookkeeping device for a quadratic in several variables.
\end{example}

In general, expanding the product gives

\[
  \vec{x}\T \mat{A}\, \vec{x} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}\, x_i x_j
  \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n A_{ii}\, x_i^2 \;+\; 2\sum_{i<j} A_{ij}\, x_i x_j,
\]

where the last step used $A_{ij} = A_{ji}$. The diagonal carries the pure squares; the
off-diagonal carries the interactions.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}$ as the \emph{height of a curved surface} above the input point $\vec{x}$.
For an ordinary number $a>0$, $f(x) = ax^2$ is a parabola -- a 1D valley. For a matrix, $q(\vec{x})$
is a surface over the $n$-dimensional input space. The question of this chapter is: \emph{is that
surface a bowl that curves up in every direction?} If yes, $\mat{A}$ is positive definite.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Only the \emph{symmetric part} of a matrix affects the quadratic form. Any square $\mat{A}$ splits
as $\mat{A} = \mat{A}_{\text{sym}} + \mat{B}$ with $\mat{A}_{\text{sym}} = \tfrac12(\mat{A} +
\mat{A}\T)$ and $\mat{B} = \tfrac12(\mat{A} - \mat{A}\T)$. The second piece is
\emph{antisymmetric} ($\mat{B}\T = -\mat{B}$), and it contributes nothing: the number
$\vec{x}\T\mat{B}\vec{x}$ equals its own transpose $\vec{x}\T\mat{B}\T\vec{x} =
-\vec{x}\T\mat{B}\vec{x}$, so it is $0$. Hence $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x} = \vec{x}\T
\mat{A}_{\text{sym}}\,\vec{x}$. So when we talk about positive definiteness we \emph{always} assume
$\mat{A}$ is symmetric; otherwise we replace it by its symmetric part and lose nothing.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{The definition}

\begin{definition}[Positive definite]
\label{ch27:def-pd}
A symmetric matrix $\mat{A} \in \R^{n\times n}$ is \textbf{positive definite} (PD) if
\[
  \vec{x}\T \mat{A}\, \vec{x} \;>\; 0 \qquad \text{for every nonzero } \vec{x} \in \R^n.
\]
It is \textbf{positive semidefinite} (PSD) if the weaker $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x} \geq 0$ holds for
all $\vec{x}$. We write $\mat{A} \succ 0$ for positive definite and $\mat{A} \succeq 0$ for positive
semidefinite. Reversing the inequalities gives \emph{negative (semi)definite}, written $\mat{A}\prec 0$
(and $\mat{A}\preceq 0$); a matrix whose form
takes both positive and negative values is \textbf{indefinite} (meaning the surface bends up along
some directions and down along others, forming a saddle).
\end{definition}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check.} Why does Definition~\ref{ch27:def-pd} demand $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x} > 0$
for \emph{nonzero} $\vec{x}$, rather than all $\vec{x}$? Because at $\vec{x} = \vec{0}$, the form
$\vec{0}\T\mat{A}\vec{0}$ equals zero for every matrix. What positive definiteness demands is that
as soon as you step away from the origin in \emph{any} direction, the energy strictly climbs. That
is what makes the surface a bowl.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{focusbox}
In one sentence: \emph{positive definite means the quadratic surface
$\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}$ is a bowl that opens strictly upward in every direction, with its unique
lowest point at the origin.} Everything else in this chapter (the eigenvalue, pivot and determinant
tests, the triangular square root, covariance matrices) is a different way of checking that one
geometric fact.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Test 1: eigenvalues}

The first test comes from the spectral theorem. Because $\mat{A}$ is symmetric, it has real
eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ and an \emph{orthonormal} basis of eigenvectors
$\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_n$, which are the columns of $\mat{Q}$:
\[
  \mat{A} = \mat{Q}\,\mat{\Lambda}\,\mat{Q}\T, \qquad
  \mat{Q}\T\mat{Q} = \mat{I}, \quad \mat{\Lambda} = \diag(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n).
\]
Substitute this into the energy and change coordinates. Let $\vec{y} = \mat{Q}\T\vec{x}$ (the
coordinates of $\vec{x}$ in the eigenvector basis). Then
\begin{align*}
  \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}
  &= \vec{x}\T\mat{Q}\mat{\Lambda}\mat{Q}\T\vec{x} \\
  &= (\mat{Q}\T\vec{x})\T \mat{\Lambda}\, (\mat{Q}\T\vec{x}) \\
  &= \vec{y}\T\mat{\Lambda}\vec{y} \\
  &= \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n y_n^2.
\end{align*}

This is the most important computation in the chapter. \emph{In the eigenvector
coordinates the cross terms vanish.} The form $\sum A_{ij} x_i x_j$ becomes a weighted sum of pure
squares, and the weights are the eigenvalues. Because $\mat{Q}$ is an orthogonal matrix (so
$\vec{x} = \mat{Q}\vec{y}$), $\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ if and only if $\vec{y} = \vec{0}$. So the
question ``is the form positive for all $\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$?'' becomes ``is $\sum \lambda_i y_i^2 >
0$ for all $\vec{y}\neq\vec{0}$?'', and that holds exactly when every $\lambda_i > 0$.

\begin{theorem}[Eigenvalue test]
\label{ch27:thm-eig}
A symmetric matrix $\mat{A}$ is positive definite if and only if all of its eigenvalues are
strictly positive. It is positive semidefinite iff all eigenvalues are $\geq 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
($\Leftarrow$) If every $\lambda_i > 0$ and $\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$, then $\vec{y}=\mat{Q}\T\vec{x}
\neq\vec{0}$, so at least one $y_k \neq 0$, and $\sum \lambda_i y_i^2 \geq \lambda_k y_k^2 > 0$.
($\Rightarrow$) Conversely, suppose $\mat{A}\succ 0$ and let $\vec{q}_k$ be a unit eigenvector for
$\lambda_k$. Plug $\vec{x} = \vec{q}_k$ into the form:
$\vec{q}_k\T\mat{A}\vec{q}_k = \vec{q}_k\T(\lambda_k\vec{q}_k) = \lambda_k \norm{\vec{q}_k}^2 =
\lambda_k$. Positive definiteness forces this to be $>0$, so $\lambda_k > 0$. The semidefinite
statement is identical with $\geq$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Reading positivity off the eigenvalues]
\label{ch27:ex-eig}
For $\mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ the characteristic equation is
\[
  \det(\mat{A} - \lambda\mat{I}) = (2-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 1 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 5 = 0,
\]
so
\[
  \lambda = \frac{5 \pm \sqrt 5}{2}.
\]
That gives $\lambda_1 \approx 3.618$ and $\lambda_2 \approx 1.382$, both positive, so $\mat{A}\succ
0$. Two checks you can do in your head: the sum $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 5$ equals the trace $2+3$,
and the product $\lambda_1\lambda_2 = 5 = \det\mat{A}$. Both being positive is \emph{consistent}
with PD, but the determinant alone is not enough, as the next warning shows.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
A positive determinant does \emph{not} guarantee positive definiteness. Consider
$\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$: its determinant is $+6 > 0$, but both eigenvalues
are negative, so the form $-2x_1^2 - 3x_2^2$ is negative everywhere. A positive determinant in 2D
only says the eigenvalues have the \emph{same} sign; they could both be negative. You need more,
which is why the determinant test (Test~3 below) checks the determinant of \emph{every} top-left
corner of the matrix, not only the full determinant.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Test 2: pivots}

Computing eigenvalues means finding the roots of a polynomial, which is expensive. A cheaper test
comes out of elimination. Recall that Gaussian elimination without row swaps factors a matrix as
$\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{U}$, where $\mat{L}$ is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal and
$\mat{U}$ is upper triangular. The diagonal entries of $\mat{U}$ are the \textbf{pivots} (the
entries used to clear out the column below them during elimination).

\begin{theorem}[Pivot test]
\label{ch27:thm-pivots}
A symmetric matrix $\mat{A}$ is positive definite if and only if elimination runs to completion
\emph{without any row exchanges} and produces $n$ \emph{strictly positive pivots}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check.} Suppose you begin elimination on a symmetric matrix $\mat{A}$ and find
$A_{11} \leq 0$. Can $\mat{A}$ possibly be positive definite? No, because $A_{11}$ is the first
pivot $d_1$, and it also equals the energy of the first standard basis vector:
$\vec{e}_1\T\mat{A}\vec{e}_1 = A_{11}$. If $A_{11} \leq 0$, the test vector $\vec{e}_1 = (1, 0, \dots, 0)\T$
already gives zero or negative energy, so the matrix fails positive definiteness before elimination takes
a single step.
\end{recoverybox}

Here is why the test works. For a symmetric $\mat{A}$ the $\mat{L}\mat{U}$ factorization can be
written symmetrically as $\mat{A} = \mat{L}\,\mat{D}\,\mat{L}\T$, where $\mat{D} =
\diag(d_1,\dots,d_n)$ holds the pivots. (Divide each row of $\mat{U}$ by its pivot; for a symmetric
matrix the result is exactly $\mat{L}\T$, so $\mat{U} = \mat{D}\mat{L}\T$.) Substitute into the
energy and set $\vec{y} = \mat{L}\T\vec{x}$:
\begin{align*}
  \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}
  &= \vec{x}\T\mat{L}\mat{D}\mat{L}\T\vec{x} \\
  &= (\mat{L}\T\vec{x})\T \mat{D} (\mat{L}\T\vec{x}) \\
  &= \vec{y}\T\mat{D}\vec{y} \\
  &= d_1 y_1^2 + d_2 y_2^2 + \cdots + d_n y_n^2.
\end{align*}
Because $\mat{L}\T$ is invertible (ones on the diagonal), $\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$ if and only if
$\vec{y}\neq\vec{0}$. So, as with eigenvalues, the form is a weighted sum of squares, but now the
weights are the \emph{pivots}. The form is positive exactly when every pivot is positive.

The pivots and the eigenvalues are usually different numbers; what the test says is that they are
all positive together. The two sets share their product, $\det\mat{A} = \prod d_i = \prod
\lambda_i$, but not their sum: the trace equals the sum of the eigenvalues, not of the pivots. For
$\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}$ the pivots $2$ and $\tfrac52$ add up to $\tfrac92$, while
the trace is $5$.

\begin{example}[Completing the square is elimination]
\label{ch27:ex-square}
Take $\mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ again. One elimination step subtracts
$\tfrac12$ times row 1 from row 2:
\[
  \mat{A} \;\longrightarrow\;
  \mat{U} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & \tfrac52 \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad \text{pivots } d_1 = 2,\; d_2 = \tfrac52.
\]
Both positive, so $\mat{A}\succ 0$. The same pivots appear when you \emph{complete the square} on
the quadratic form directly:
\[
  2x_1^2 + 2x_1 x_2 + 3x_2^2
  = \underbrace{2}_{d_1}\Big(x_1 + \tfrac12 x_2\Big)^2 + \underbrace{\tfrac52}_{d_2}\, x_2^2 .
\]
The pivot $2$ multiplies the completed square; the pivot $\tfrac52$ multiplies the leftover. The
multiplier $\tfrac12$ inside the parenthesis is exactly the entry $L_{21}$ from elimination. With
two positive coefficients in front of two squares, the form is a sum of nonnegative pieces that are
both zero only at $\vec{x}=\vec{0}$. \emph{Completing the square and Gaussian elimination are the
same computation.}
\end{example}

\begin{strangbox}
Strang often says ``the pivots tell you the signs.'' In his course the $\mat{LDL}\T$ factorization
and the energy test appear together, and the punch line is Example~\ref{ch27:ex-square}: each pivot
is the coefficient of a completed square. If you read this chapter \emph{before} Strang, carry this
in: elimination does more than solve $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$; it also detects positivity. If you
are here \emph{after} Strang, the pivot test is what makes checking positive definiteness
\emph{cheap}: elimination on a symmetric matrix takes about $n^3/3$ multiplications, several times
fewer than computing all the eigenvalues.
\end{strangbox}

\section{Test 3: leading minors (the determinant test)}

The third test needs no factorization, only the determinants of the top-left corners of the matrix.
The $k$th \textbf{leading principal minor} $\Delta_k$ (capital Greek delta) is the determinant of
the top-left $k\times k$ block of $\mat{A}$, the square submatrix formed by the first $k$ rows and
the first $k$ columns. Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-minors} shows the nested blocks.

\begin{theorem}[Sylvester's criterion]
\label{ch27:thm-sylvester}
A symmetric matrix $\mat{A}$ is positive definite if and only if every leading principal minor is
positive:
\[
  \Delta_1 = A_{11} > 0,\quad
  \Delta_2 = \det\begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22}\end{bmatrix} > 0,\quad
  \dots,\quad
  \Delta_n = \det\mat{A} > 0.
\]
\end{theorem}

The criterion is named after James Joseph Sylvester (see the history box at the end of the chapter).
The pivot test explains why it works. Elimination on the top-left $k\times k$ block uses only those
$k$ rows and columns, so it produces the same first $k$ pivots as elimination on all of $\mat{A}$.
The determinant of that block is therefore the product of the first $k$ pivots, $\Delta_k = d_1 d_2
\cdots d_k$. Dividing two consecutive minors leaves one pivot:
\[
  d_k = \frac{\Delta_k}{\Delta_{k-1}}, \qquad (\Delta_0 := 1).
\]
If all $\Delta_k > 0$ then every ratio $d_k = \Delta_k/\Delta_{k-1} > 0$, so all pivots are
positive and $\mat{A}\succ 0$ by Theorem~\ref{ch27:thm-pivots}; conversely positive pivots make
every $\Delta_k = d_1 d_2\cdots d_k > 0$.

\begin{example}[All three tests on one matrix]
\label{ch27:ex-allthree}
Let
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix},
\]
called the \emph{second-difference} matrix: row $i$ of $\mat{A}\vec{u}$ is $-u_{i-1} + 2u_i -
u_{i+1}$ (with $u_0 = u_4 = 0$), which is minus the discrete version of a second derivative. Run
every test.

\emph{Leading minors:} $\Delta_1 = 2 > 0$, and $\Delta_2 = \det\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}
= 3 > 0$. Expanding the full determinant along the first row,
\[
  \Delta_3 = \det\mat{A} = 2\,(4-1) - (-1)\,(-2-0) = 6 - 2 = 4 > 0.
\]
All positive: PD.

\emph{Pivots:} elimination gives $d_1 = \Delta_1 = 2$, $d_2 = \Delta_2/\Delta_1 = 3/2$, $d_3 =
\Delta_3/\Delta_2 = 4/3$. All positive, and their product confirms $2 \cdot \tfrac32 \cdot \tfrac43 = 4 = \det\mat{A}$.

\emph{Eigenvalues:} for this matrix they are known in closed form, $\lambda_k = 2 - 2\cos\frac{k\pi}{4}$
for $k=1,2,3$, giving $2-\sqrt2 \approx 0.586$, $2$, and $2+\sqrt2 \approx 3.414$ -- all positive.
Three tests, three different sets of positive numbers, one verdict: $\mat{A}\succ 0$.
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Test $\mat{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ by all three methods and confirm they
agree that it is \emph{not} positive definite.
\emph{Minors:} $\Delta_1 = 1 > 0$ but $\Delta_2 = 1 - 4 = -3 < 0$. The failure of $\Delta_2$ is the
red flag.
\emph{Pivots:} $d_1 = 1$, then eliminate to get $d_2 = -3$. A negative pivot.
\emph{Eigenvalues:} $\lambda = 1 \pm 2$, i.e. $3$ and $-1$. The negative eigenvalue means the form
$x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + x_2^2$ is a saddle -- try $\vec{x}=(1,-1)$ to get $1 - 4 + 1 = -2 < 0$.
\end{labbox}

\begin{trapbox}
Sylvester's criterion uses the \emph{leading} (top-left) minors and is a test for \emph{positive
definiteness only}. Do not try to detect positive \emph{semi}definiteness by relaxing to
$\Delta_k \geq 0$ -- that is false. The matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}$ has
$\Delta_1 = 0$ and $\Delta_2 = 0$, both $\geq 0$, yet it is negative semidefinite, not positive. For
semidefiniteness you must check \emph{all} principal minors, not only the leading ones. A
\textbf{principal minor} is the determinant of a sub-block that keeps the same set of rows and
columns, for example rows and columns $1$ and $3$. (Here the $1\times1$ principal minor from row and
column $2$ is $-1 < 0$, which exposes the problem.) Or fall back to the eigenvalue test $\lambda_i
\geq 0$.
\end{trapbox}

\section{The geometry: ellipses and ellipsoids}

To see the three tests geometrically, look at the bowl from above and draw its \emph{level sets}:
the curves where the energy is constant, like the contour lines on a hiking map.

For a positive definite $2\times2$ matrix, the set
\[
  \{\,\vec{x} : \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x} = 1\,\}
\]
is an \textbf{ellipse} centered at the origin. (If $\mat{A}$ were indefinite you would get a
hyperbola; if it were semidefinite with a zero eigenvalue, a pair of parallel lines.) The ellipse is
the constant-energy contour, and positive definiteness says exactly that these contours are
\emph{closed loops} around the origin rather than open curves running off to infinity.

The spectral decomposition gives the ellipse's exact shape. In the eigenvector coordinates
$\vec{y} = \mat{Q}\T\vec{x}$, the equation $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}=1$ becomes
\[
  \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2 = 1,
\]
the standard equation of an ellipse with semi-axis lengths $1/\sqrt{\lambda_1}$ and
$1/\sqrt{\lambda_2}$ along the eigenvector directions $\vec{q}_1, \vec{q}_2$. (A \emph{semi-axis} is
the distance from the centre to the curve along one of the ellipse's axes of symmetry. Setting
$y_2 = 0$ gives $y_1 = \pm 1/\sqrt{\lambda_1}$.) Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-ellipse} draws these
contours for a concrete matrix.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The eigenvectors are the axes of the ellipse; the eigenvalues set the axis lengths.} A
\emph{large} eigenvalue means the form grows fast in that direction, so the contour is \emph{close}
to the origin there (short axis $1/\sqrt\lambda$). A \emph{small} eigenvalue means a long, shallow axis.
Positive definite $\Leftrightarrow$ both axes are real and finite $\Leftrightarrow$ the contour is a
genuine ellipse.
\end{focusbox}

In $\R^n$ the same statement holds: the level set $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x} = 1$ is an
\textbf{ellipsoid} with principal axes along the eigenvectors and semi-axis lengths
$1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. So a positive definite matrix and an ellipsoid centred at the origin carry the
same information. The same picture returns below for Hessians and covariance matrices.

\begin{walkbox}
What happens to the contour $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}=1$ as one eigenvalue $\lambda$ decreases toward
$0$? The semi-axis $1/\sqrt\lambda$ in that direction grows without limit, and the ellipse stretches
out. At $\lambda = 0$ the matrix is only positive semidefinite and the contour is a pair of parallel
lines (in three dimensions, a cylinder): it no longer closes up. If $\lambda$ becomes negative, the
contour becomes a hyperbola (in three dimensions, a hyperboloid), and the matrix is indefinite.
Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-walk} shows the four stages. Closed, bounded contours go with strictly positive
eigenvalues.
\end{walkbox}

\section{Cholesky: the square root of a matrix}

A positive number $a>0$ has a square root $\sqrt a$, and you can write $a = (\sqrt a)(\sqrt a)$.
Positive definite matrices have a matrix version of this, and it is used constantly in numerical
computing.

\begin{theorem}[Cholesky factorization]
\label{ch27:thm-cholesky}
A symmetric matrix $\mat{A}$ is positive definite if and only if it can be written
\[
  \mat{A} = \mat{R}\T\mat{R}
\]
for some upper-triangular matrix $\mat{R}$ with \emph{strictly positive} diagonal entries. This
$\mat{R}$ is unique, and is called the \textbf{Cholesky factor} of $\mat{A}$. It is a kind of square
root: an upper-triangular matrix that, multiplied on the left by its own transpose, gives back
$\mat{A}$.
\end{theorem}

The name honours the French surveyor Andr\'e-Louis Cholesky (see the history box).

\begin{proof}[Why it exists]
We already have $\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{D}\mat{L}\T$ with positive pivots $d_i$ (the pivot test). Since
each $d_i > 0$, define $\mat{D}^{1/2} = \diag(\sqrt{d_1},\dots,\sqrt{d_n})$, so that $\mat{D} =
\mat{D}^{1/2}\mat{D}^{1/2}$. Then
\begin{align*}
  \mat{A} &= \mat{L}\mat{D}^{1/2}\mat{D}^{1/2}\mat{L}\T \\
  &= \big(\mat{D}^{1/2}\mat{L}\T\big)\T\big(\mat{D}^{1/2}\mat{L}\T\big) \\
  &= \mat{R}\T\mat{R},
\end{align*}
with $\mat{R} = \mat{D}^{1/2}\mat{L}\T$ upper triangular and positive on the diagonal. Conversely, if
$\mat{A}=\mat{R}\T\mat{R}$ with $\mat{R}$ invertible then for $\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$,
\[
  \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x} = \vec{x}\T\mat{R}\T\mat{R}\vec{x} = \norm{\mat{R}\vec{x}}^2 > 0,
\]
because $\mat{R}\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$. So $\mat{A}\succ0$.
\end{proof}

The converse argument shows up so often with data that it gets a box of its own.

\begin{aestheticbox}
The same argument works for any matrix $\mat{R}$, square or rectangular: $\mat{R}\T\mat{R}$ is
always positive semidefinite, because its quadratic form is a squared length,
\[
  \vec{x}\T\mat{R}\T\mat{R}\vec{x}=\norm{\mat{R}\vec{x}}^2 \geq 0.
\]
It is positive definite exactly when $\mat{R}$ has linearly independent columns (then
$\mat{R}\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$ whenever $\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$). So Gram matrices, sample covariance
matrices, and the normal-equation matrix $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ of least squares are all positive
semidefinite by construction.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{example}[Computing a Cholesky factor by hand]
\label{ch27:ex-cholesky}
Factor $\mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ as $\mat{R}\T\mat{R}$ with
$\mat{R} = \begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} \\ 0 & r_{22} \end{bmatrix}$. Multiply out:
\[
  \mat{R}\T\mat{R} =
  \begin{bmatrix} r_{11}^2 & r_{11}r_{12} \\ r_{11}r_{12} & r_{12}^2 + r_{22}^2 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Match entries top-left first, then move right and down:
\begin{align*}
  r_{11}^2 &= 4 &&\Rightarrow\; r_{11} = 2,\\
  r_{11}r_{12} &= 2 &&\Rightarrow\; r_{12} = 1,\\
  r_{12}^2 + r_{22}^2 &= 5 &&\Rightarrow\; r_{22}^2 = 4 \;\Rightarrow\; r_{22}=2.
\end{align*}
So $\mat{R} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, and you can check
$\mat{R}\T\mat{R} = \mat{A}$. The positive diagonal $r_{11}=r_{22}=2$ certifies $\mat{A}\succ0$. If at
any step you had to take the square root of a \emph{negative} number, the matrix would not be
positive definite. So Cholesky is itself a positivity test, and the cheapest one in practice.
\end{example}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Generating correlated random data.} Simulations often need random vectors whose coordinates
are correlated in a prescribed way, for example the daily returns of several stocks that tend to
move together. The usual starting point is a vector $\vec{u}$ of independent \emph{standard normal}
random numbers: each follows the bell curve with mean $0$ and variance $1$, and they are
uncorrelated, so their covariance matrix is $\Cov(\vec{u})=\mat{I}$. (Covariance matrices are
treated in Section~\ref{ch27:sec-cov} below. Here you need only one rule: for a fixed matrix
$\mat{M}$, $\Cov(\mat{M}\vec{u}) = \mat{M}\,\Cov(\vec{u})\,\mat{M}\T$.)

To produce a random vector $\vec{z}$ with mean zero and a chosen positive definite covariance matrix
$\mat{\Sigma}$, factor $\mat{\Sigma}=\mat{R}\T\mat{R}$, draw $\vec{u}$, and set $\vec{z} =
\mat{R}\T\vec{u}$. Then
\[
  \Cov(\vec{z}) = \mat{R}\T\,\Cov(\vec{u})\,\mat{R} = \mat{R}\T\mat{I}\mat{R} = \mat{R}\T\mat{R}
  = \mat{\Sigma}.
\]
Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-sampling} shows this for the matrix of Example~\ref{ch27:ex-cholesky}: $\mat{R}\T$
turns a round cloud of points into a tilted, stretched one.

The same factor solves $\mat{\Sigma}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ quickly and accurately: $\mat{R}\T\mat{R}\vec{x}
= \vec{b}$ splits into two triangular systems, $\mat{R}\T\vec{y} = \vec{b}$ and $\mat{R}\vec{x} =
\vec{y}$, each solved by substitution in about $n^2$ operations. It also gives the determinant for
free: $\det\mat{\Sigma} = (\det\mat{R})^2 = \prod_i r_{ii}^2$, so $\log\det\mat{\Sigma} = 2\sum_i \log
r_{ii}$, a quantity that statistical software needs constantly.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Positive definiteness in optimization}

Positive definiteness is the multivariable version of ``the second derivative is positive,'' and
that is the condition that makes a critical point a genuine \emph{minimum}. (A \textbf{critical
point} is a point where all the first partial derivatives are zero, so the surface is flat there.)

Recall single-variable calculus: at a critical point $f'(x^*)=0$, you look at $f''(x^*)$. If it is
positive the curve bends up and you sit at the bottom of a valley; if negative, at a hilltop. With
several variables the second derivative is replaced by the \textbf{Hessian} $\mat{H}$ (Chapter~26),
the symmetric matrix of all second partial derivatives:
\[
  H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \, \partial x_j}.
\]
Near a point $\vec{x}^*$, a smooth function $f$ (one whose second partial derivatives exist and are
continuous) is approximated by its \emph{second-order Taylor expansion}, the best approximation of
$f$ by a polynomial of degree two. (For a refresher on Taylor series, see
Section~\ref{ch39:calculus} in the Chapter~39 appendix.) Write $\vec{h}$ for a small step away from
$\vec{x}^*$ and $\nabla f$ for the gradient, the vector of first partial derivatives. Then
\[
  f(\vec{x}^* + \vec{h}) \;\approx\; f(\vec{x}^*) \;+\; \nabla f(\vec{x}^*)\T \vec{h}
  \;+\; \tfrac12\, \vec{h}\T \mat{H}\, \vec{h}.
\]
At a critical point $\nabla f(\vec{x}^*) = \vec{0}$, so the middle term is gone. \emph{The local
shape of $f$ around a critical point is governed by the quadratic form $\vec{h}\T\mat{H}\vec{h}$},
and we now know what its sign tells us.

\begin{theorem}[Second-derivative test, matrix form]
\label{ch27:thm-secondderiv}
Let $\vec{x}^*$ be a critical point of a smooth $f:\R^n\to\R$ with Hessian $\mat{H}$ at $\vec{x}^*$
(often written $\nabla^2 f(\vec{x}^*)$). Then
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mat{H}\succ 0$ (positive definite) $\Rightarrow$ $\vec{x}^*$ is a strict local
  \emph{minimum};
  \item $\mat{H}\prec 0$ (negative definite) $\Rightarrow$ a strict local \emph{maximum};
  \item $\mat{H}$ indefinite $\Rightarrow$ a \emph{saddle point} (a min in some directions, a max in
  others).
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

``Strict local minimum'' means $f(\vec{x}^*+\vec{h}) > f(\vec{x}^*)$ for every small nonzero step
$\vec{h}$. If $\mat{H}$ is only semidefinite (with a zero eigenvalue), the test gives no answer and
the higher-order terms decide.

This is Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-three-surfaces} again, as a calculus statement: bowl, upside-down bowl,
saddle. Gradient descent and Newton's method (Chapter~19) behave well near a minimum because the
surface there is, to second order, a positive definite bowl, and a bowl has one bottom to roll down
to.

\begin{example}[Classifying a critical point]
\label{ch27:ex-hessian}
Let $f(x,y) = x^2 + xy + y^2 - 3x$. The gradient is $\nabla f = (2x + y - 3,\; x + 2y)$. Setting it
to zero gives $x=2,\ y=-1$, a single critical point. Writing $f_{xx}$ for $\partial^2 f/\partial x^2$,
$f_{xy}$ for $\partial^2 f/\partial x\,\partial y$, and so on, the Hessian is constant:
\[
  \mat{H} = \begin{bmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{yx} & f_{yy} \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Leading minors: $\Delta_1 = 2 > 0$, $\Delta_2 = 4-1 = 3 > 0$. Positive definite, so $(2,-1)$ is a
strict local minimum. Because $f$ is a quadratic, the Taylor expansion is exact:
$f(2+h_1,\,-1+h_2) = -3 + \tfrac12\vec{h}\T\mat{H}\vec{h}$, so $(2,-1)$ is also the global minimum,
with $f = -3$. Two determinants settled it; Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-hessian} draws the contours to
confirm.
\end{example}

\begin{strangbox}
After Strang, this leads to his lectures on optimization and positive definite matrices, then (after
similarity and Jordan form in the next chapter) to the singular value decomposition (SVD) and
principal component analysis (PCA). The common thread: \emph{symmetric positive definite matrices are
the well-behaved ones}, the ones whose quadratic minimization problems have a unique answer
$\vec{x}^* = \mat{A}\inv\vec{b}$, found by solving one linear system. The function
\[
  \tfrac12\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x} - \vec{b}\T\vec{x}
\]
has gradient $\mat{A}\vec{x}-\vec{b}$, which is zero exactly when $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$. Positive
definiteness of $\mat{A}$ guarantees that this critical point is the unique global minimum rather
than a saddle. Solving a linear system \emph{is} minimizing a quadratic bowl.
\end{strangbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Any positive definite $\mat{A}$ defines a new inner product (a new way to measure lengths and
angles),
\[
  \inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}_{\mat{A}} := \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{y}.
\]
It has the three properties required of an inner product: it is symmetric (because $\mat{A}$ is),
linear in each slot, and positive, $\inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{x}}_{\mat{A}} = \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}>0$
for $\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$. The last property is exactly positive definiteness. In this inner product
the vectors of length $1$ are the points of the ellipse $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}=1$ in
Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-ellipse}, which plays the role of the unit circle. The same idea reappears in
more advanced subjects: the geometry of curved spaces puts a positive definite matrix at every point
to measure lengths there, and \emph{kernel methods} in machine learning measure similarity between
data points with a positive definite matrix of pairwise similarities.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Covariance: positive definiteness in statistics}
\label{ch27:sec-cov}

Statistics is the other place where positive definite matrices are everywhere. The
\textbf{covariance matrix} of several variables (a symmetric table whose $(i, j)$ entry measures how
much the $i$th and $j$th variables move together) is always positive semidefinite, and it is
positive definite unless the variables satisfy an exact linear relation.

A \textbf{random vector} $\vec{X} = (X_1,\dots,X_n)$ is a list of $n$ random quantities measured
together, such as the height, weight and age of a randomly chosen person. Write $\mathbb{E}$ for
\emph{expected value}, the long-run average over many repetitions, and $\vec{\mu} =
\mathbb{E}[\vec{X}]$ for the mean. The covariance matrix is
\[
  \mat{\Sigma} = \Cov(\vec{X}) = \mathbb{E}\big[(\vec{X}-\vec{\mu})(\vec{X}-\vec{\mu})\T\big].
\]
Inside the brackets is an $n\times n$ matrix whose $(i,j)$ entry is $(X_i-\mu_i)(X_j-\mu_j)$, so the
$(i,j)$ entry of $\mat{\Sigma}$ is the covariance $\Cov(X_i,X_j)$, and the diagonal entries are the
variances. Why must $\mat{\Sigma}$ be positive semidefinite? Take any fixed vector
$\vec{a}\in\R^n$ and form the single random number $\vec{a}\T\vec{X} = a_1X_1 + \cdots + a_nX_n$.
Its variance cannot be negative, and since $\vec{a}\T(\vec{X}-\vec{\mu})$ is a number equal to its
own transpose,
\begin{align*}
  0 \;\leq\; \Var(\vec{a}\T\vec{X})
  &= \mathbb{E}\big[\big(\vec{a}\T(\vec{X}-\vec{\mu})\big)^2\big] \\
  &= \mathbb{E}\big[\vec{a}\T(\vec{X}-\vec{\mu})(\vec{X}-\vec{\mu})\T\vec{a}\big] \\
  &= \vec{a}\T\mat{\Sigma}\vec{a}.
\end{align*}
The quadratic form $\vec{a}\T\mat{\Sigma}\vec{a}$ is a \emph{variance}, and variances are never
negative. So $\mat{\Sigma}\succeq0$ automatically. It fails to be positive \emph{definite} only when
some combination $\vec{a}\T\vec{X}$ has zero variance, that is, when the data lies exactly on a
line, plane, or other flat set of lower dimension (a perfect linear dependence among the variables).

\begin{intuitionbox}
For centered data points $\vec{x}$ (data with the mean subtracted), the \emph{covariance ellipsoid}
$\vec{x}\T\mat{\Sigma}\inv\vec{x} = 1$ shows the shape of the data cloud. Its axes point along the
eigenvectors of $\mat{\Sigma}$, the directions of largest and smallest variance. Because
$\mat{\Sigma}\inv$ has eigenvalues $1/\lambda_i$, the semi-axis lengths are $1/\sqrt{1/\lambda_i} =
\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, the standard deviations along those directions. In principal component analysis
(PCA), the principal components are exactly these eigenvectors, ordered by eigenvalue size. Positive
definiteness guarantees that the ellipsoid is not flattened in any direction.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{example}[A sample covariance is $\frac1m\mat{X}\T\mat{X}$]
\label{ch27:ex-cov}
Stack $m$ mean-centered data vectors as the rows of a matrix $\mat{X}\in\R^{m\times n}$. The sample
covariance is $\mat{S} = \tfrac1m\mat{X}\T\mat{X}$. By the argument in the box after
Theorem~\ref{ch27:thm-cholesky}, this is positive semidefinite automatically, since for any
$\vec{a}$,
\[
  \vec{a}\T\mat{S}\vec{a} = \tfrac1m\,\vec{a}\T\mat{X}\T\mat{X}\vec{a}
  = \tfrac1m\,\norm{\mat{X}\vec{a}}^2 \;\geq\; 0,
\]
again a squared length. It is strictly positive definite exactly when $\mat{X}$ has independent
columns, that is, when no feature is a linear combination of the others. The same matrix
$\mat{X}\T\mat{X}$ is the one you invert in the least-squares normal equations, and its positive
definiteness is \emph{why} ordinary least squares has a unique solution.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
In practice, a sample covariance can be singular (only positive semidefinite), for instance when
there are fewer data points than features ($m<n$) or when columns are linearly dependent. Then
$\mat{S}$ is not invertible and the Cholesky factorization fails. The standard remedy is
\emph{regularization}: replace $\mat{S}$ by $\mat{S} + \epsilon\mat{I}$ for a small number
$\epsilon>0$. Adding $\epsilon\mat{I}$ shifts every eigenvalue up by $\epsilon$ (if $\mat{S}\vec{v} =
\lambda\vec{v}$ then $(\mat{S}+\epsilon\mat{I})\vec{v} = (\lambda+\epsilon)\vec{v}$), so the smallest
eigenvalue is at least $\epsilon$ and the matrix is positive definite. Ridge regression
(Chapter~19) does exactly this to $\mat{X}\T\mat{X}$. Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-regularize} shows the
effect on the covariance ellipse.
\end{trapbox}

\section{A gallery of consequences}

Once a matrix is known to be positive definite, several more facts follow from the tests above;
some of them make good exercises.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Invertible, with positive determinant.} All eigenvalues are positive, so none is
  zero, so $\det\mat{A} = \prod\lambda_i > 0$ and $\mat{A}\inv$ exists.
  \item \textbf{The inverse is also PD.} The eigenvalues of $\mat{A}\inv$ are $1/\lambda_i$, still
  positive.
  \item \textbf{Positive diagonal.} Plugging the standard basis vector $\vec{e}_i$ into the form
  gives $A_{ii} = \vec{e}_i\T\mat{A}\vec{e}_i > 0$. (Useful as a quick \emph{disqualifier}: a
  negative or zero diagonal entry instantly rules PD out.)
  \item \textbf{Largest entry on the diagonal.} For a PD matrix the biggest entry in absolute value
  always sits on the diagonal. The $2\times 2$ block from rows and columns $i$ and $j$ is itself
  positive definite (use only vectors that are zero outside positions $i$ and $j$), so its
  determinant is positive: $A_{ii} A_{jj} - A_{ij}^2 > 0$. Hence
  \[
    |A_{ij}| < \sqrt{A_{ii} A_{jj}} \leq \max(A_{ii}, A_{jj}),
  \]
  so no off-diagonal entry can exceed the largest diagonal entry.
  \item \textbf{Sums and congruence stay PD.} If $\mat{A},\mat{B}\succ0$ then $\mat{A}+\mat{B}\succ0$
  (add the energies), and $\mat{C}\T\mat{A}\mat{C}\succ0$ for any invertible $\mat{C}$. Passing from
  $\mat{A}$ to $\mat{C}\T\mat{A}\mat{C}$ is called a \emph{congruence}; it is a change of coordinates
  in the quadratic form: if $\vec{y} = \mat{C}\vec{x}$, then
  $\vec{x}\T(\mat{C}\T\mat{A}\mat{C})\vec{x} = \vec{y}\T\mat{A}\vec{y} > 0$ for $\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$.
  \item \textbf{A unique PD square root.} There is a unique positive definite $\mat{A}^{1/2}$ with
  $\mat{A}^{1/2}\mat{A}^{1/2}=\mat{A}$, found by taking the square root of each eigenvalue:
  $\mat{A}^{1/2}=\mat{Q}\,\diag(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\dots,\sqrt{\lambda_n})\,\mat{Q}\T$. (It differs
  from the triangular Cholesky factor, but it is a square root too.)
\end{itemize}

\begin{focusbox}
\emph{Positive definite matrices are the matrix counterpart of positive numbers.} They are
invertible, have square roots, stay positive definite when added, and make quadratic minimization
problems have a unique minimum. When a theorem in optimization, statistics, or numerical analysis
assumes $\mat{A}\succ0$, it relies on this upward-curving bowl shape.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{Non-symmetric matrices: looking ahead to Chapter~28.}
In this chapter and the previous one, symmetric matrices gave perpendicular eigenvectors and real
eigenvalues, and positive definiteness made the energy curve upward in all directions. In the
eigenvector coordinates ($\mat{Q}\T\mat{A}\mat{Q} = \mat{\Lambda}$) the quadratic form splits into
separate positive squares.

Most matrices in applications are not symmetric: transition matrices of Markov chains, adjacency
matrices of directed graphs, and models of systems with friction or fluid flow. Without symmetry,
eigenvectors need not be perpendicular, eigenvalues can be complex numbers, and defective matrices
lack a full basis of eigenvectors.

Chapter~28 studies \textbf{similarity} ($\mat{B} = \mat{P}\inv\mat{A}\mat{P}$, the same map written in
another basis), asks what survives when diagonalization fails, and introduces the \textbf{Jordan
form}, the simplest matrix a defective matrix is similar to. Chapters~29 and~30 then return to the
tools of this chapter: from the positive semidefinite matrix $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ they build the
\emph{singular values} of \emph{any} matrix, square or rectangular, which measure how much it
stretches space along perpendicular axes.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{historybox}
The theory of positive definite matrices began in the mechanics of conservative systems (physical
systems in which total mechanical energy is conserved and forces come from a potential energy $V$).
In his 1788 treatise \emph{M\'ecanique Analytique}, Joseph-Louis Lagrange stated the principle of
mechanical stability: an equilibrium of such a system is stable if its potential energy has a strict
local minimum there. Expanding the potential energy in a Taylor series shows that the forces vanish
at an equilibrium ($\nabla V = \vec{0}$), and that whether the equilibrium is a minimum is usually
decided by the Hessian of second derivatives. So the question became: does the quadratic form
$\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}$ of second derivatives curve upward in every direction? That is the
question of this chapter.

Lagrange's own argument had a gap: it assumed the terms beyond second order could be ignored. In
1846 Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet gave a rigorous proof that a strict minimum of the potential
energy does guarantee stability. The converse (an equilibrium where the potential is \emph{not} a
minimum must be unstable) stayed open much longer, and counterexamples were eventually found for
potentials that are smooth but not \emph{analytic} (not given by a convergent power series).
Meanwhile, mathematicians looked for efficient algebraic tests. Checking all $2^n-1$ principal
minors works but is slow; the test using only the $n$ nested leading principal minors is named after
the English mathematician James Joseph Sylvester, who worked extensively on quadratic forms in the
1850s, although its exact origin is unclear.

Around 1910 the French military geodesist (a surveyor who measures the shape of the Earth)
Andr\'e-Louis Cholesky had to solve large systems of normal equations (the systems
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vec{x} = \mat{A}\T\vec{b}$ of least squares) for map-making surveys in Crete and
North Africa. He found that symmetric positive definite systems have a triangular square root
$\mat{A} = \mat{R}\T\mat{R}$ (often written $\mat{L}\mat{L}\T$ with $\mat{L}=\mat{R}\T$). His method
needs about half the arithmetic of ordinary Gaussian elimination and needs no row exchanges to keep
rounding errors small. Cholesky never published it. He died of wounds received in battle in 1918,
and the method appeared only in 1924, in a note by his fellow officer Commandant Beno\^it in the
\emph{Bulletin G\'eod\'esique}.

Today positive definiteness underlies convex optimization, the Cholesky and conjugate-gradient
solvers (the conjugate-gradient method is an iterative method for large positive definite systems),
and the Mahalanobis distance of statistics (distance measured with a covariance ellipsoid, so that a
step along a noisy direction counts for less than a step along a tight one). Active research areas
include \emph{matrix completion}: when only some entries of a large data matrix are observed, can
the missing entries be filled in so that the result is positive semidefinite? Such problems are
solved by \emph{semidefinite programming}, optimization in which the unknown is a matrix required to
be positive semidefinite. Similarly, deciding whether a polynomial of degree four or more in several
variables is nonnegative everywhere is NP-hard; a standard workaround checks instead whether it is a
sum of squares of polynomials, which is a semidefinite program.
\end{historybox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Warmup: expand the form]
\label{ch27:ex-warmup}
For $\mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, write out $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}$ as
a polynomial in $x_1, x_2$. Then complete the square to express it as $d_1(x_1 + c\,x_2)^2 + d_2
x_2^2$ and read off the pivots $d_1, d_2$. Is $\mat{A}$ positive definite?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Three tests agree]
\label{ch27:ex-threetests}
For $\mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$, verify positive definiteness three
ways: (a) compute both eigenvalues; (b) compute both pivots by elimination; (c) check the two
leading minors. Confirm that the products $\lambda_1\lambda_2$ and $d_1 d_2$ both equal $\det\mat{A}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Find the boundary]
\label{ch27:ex-boundary}
Consider $\mat{A}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & t \\ t & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. For which values of $t$ is
$\mat{A}(t)$ positive definite? Positive semidefinite? Indefinite? Use Sylvester's criterion, and
describe what happens to the energy contour as $t$ crosses each boundary value.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The diagonal disqualifier]
\label{ch27:ex-diag}
Explain, in one or two sentences, why a symmetric matrix with a negative entry anywhere on its
diagonal cannot be positive definite. Give a $2\times2$ example whose diagonal is all positive but
which is nonetheless \emph{not} positive definite, and identify the test that catches it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Cholesky by hand]
\label{ch27:ex-chol}
Find the Cholesky factor $\mat{R}$ (upper triangular, positive diagonal) of
$\mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ so that $\mat{A}=\mat{R}\T\mat{R}$. Then use
$\mat{R}$ to compute $\det\mat{A}$ from the diagonal of $\mat{R}$ alone (no cofactor expansion).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Classify the critical points]
\label{ch27:ex-classify}
Let $f(x,y) = x^3 - 3x + y^2$. Find all critical points, compute the Hessian at each, and use the
second-derivative test (Theorem~\ref{ch27:thm-secondderiv}) to classify each as a local minimum,
local maximum, or saddle. Note that the Hessian is \emph{not} constant here.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is always PSD]
\label{ch27:ex-ata}
Let $\mat{B}$ be any (not necessarily square) real matrix. Prove that $\mat{B}\T\mat{B}$ is positive
semidefinite, and that it is positive definite if and only if the columns of $\mat{B}$ are linearly
independent. (Hint: look at $\vec{x}\T\mat{B}\T\mat{B}\vec{x}$ and recognize it as a squared length.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Eigenvalues of the regularized matrix]
\label{ch27:ex-reg}
Suppose $\mat{S}$ is symmetric positive semidefinite with eigenvalues $\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq
\lambda_n\geq 0$, and at least one $\lambda_i = 0$ (so $\mat{S}$ is singular). Show that
$\mat{S}+\epsilon\mat{I}$ is positive definite for every $\epsilon>0$ by finding its eigenvalues.
Why does this justify the ``add $\epsilon\mat{I}$'' regularization method?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Sum of PD matrices]
\label{ch27:ex-sum}
Prove that if $\mat{A}$ and $\mat{B}$ are both positive definite (same size), then so is
$\mat{A}+\mat{B}$. Give a one-line energy argument, then explain why the product $\mat{A}\mat{B}$
need \emph{not} even be symmetric, let alone positive definite.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The $\mat{A}$-inner product]
\label{ch27:ex-innerproduct}
Let $\mat{A}\succ0$ and define $\inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}_{\mat{A}} = \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\vec{y}$.
Verify the three inner-product axioms: symmetry $\inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}_{\mat{A}} =
\inner{\vec{y}}{\vec{x}}_{\mat{A}}$, linearity in the first slot, and positivity
$\inner{\vec{x}}{\vec{x}}_{\mat{A}} > 0$ for $\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$. Which property uses positive
definiteness, and which only uses symmetry?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Challenge: minors and the energy]
\label{ch27:ex-challenge}
Prove directly (without invoking the eigenvalue test) that if $\mat{A}\succ0$ then every leading
principal submatrix $\mat{A}_k$ (the top-left $k\times k$ block) is also positive definite. (Hint:
restrict the energy to vectors whose last $n-k$ coordinates are zero.) Explain how this gives one
direction of Sylvester's criterion, $\Delta_k>0$ for all $k$.
\end{exercise}
