\chapter{Vectors: Arrows, Lists, and Points}

Chapter 1 gave the overview: linear algebra is the mathematics of vectors, the linear
combinations you can build from them, and the transformations that move them. This
chapter starts with the first of these, the \emph{vector} itself. Everything later in the
book (matrices, spans, projections, eigenvalues, the singular value decomposition) is
built from vectors, so the aim is to know them the way you know the number $3$. You can
picture $3$, add it, scale it, and recognise it when it turns up. By the end of this
chapter a vector like $\colvec{2 \\ 1}$ should feel just as familiar.

The chapter is organised around one fact: a vector can be read in three ways, and the
three readings always agree.

\begin{itemize}
  \item An \textbf{arrow}: a directed step, with a length and a heading.
  \item A \textbf{list} of numbers: something you can type into a computer.
  \item A \textbf{point}: a location in space, or a single data record.
\end{itemize}

We will look at the same objects in all three ways until switching between them is
automatic. Being able to switch is the skill: the arrow gives you intuition, the list of
numbers lets you compute, and the point reading connects vectors to data.

\section{An Arrow Is a Step, Not a Place}
\label{ch02:arrow}

Start with the most physical picture. A vector is an \emph{arrow}: it has a length and a
direction, and that is \emph{all} it has. Draw an arrow that goes two units right and
one unit up. That arrow is a vector. It says ``move two right and one up,'' and it says
nothing else.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A vector is a \emph{displacement} --- a recipe for moving. ``Walk three blocks east and
one block north'' is a vector. It does not care where you start. The same instruction
moves you the same way whether you begin at your house or at the library. So the very
same arrow can be drawn anywhere on the page; sliding it around without rotating or
stretching it does not change the vector. Length and direction are everything; position
is nothing.
\end{intuitionbox}

Beginners often get this wrong, so here it is as a warning.

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{An arrow is not glued to the origin.} A vector has no fixed home. The two arrows
in Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-translate}, one starting at the origin and one starting somewhere
else, are the \emph{same vector}, because they have the same length and the same
direction. Any particular drawing of a vector is called a \emph{representative} of it. We
often \emph{choose} to draw a vector starting at the origin (the \emph{standard} or
\emph{position} representative), but that is a convenience, not a law.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.0,>=Stealth]

  \draw[->,thick,gray!60] (0,-0.5)--(0,3.7) node[above]{$y$};

  % a translated copy

\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{One vector, two drawings. Both arrows go ``2 right, 1 up,'' so both represent

\label{ch02:fig-translate}
\end{figure}

Why insist on this so early? Because the arrow-as-step picture is what makes vector
\emph{addition} easy to see in the next section. If a vector were stuck at the origin, it
would be unclear what adding two of them should mean. As steps, adding means ``do this
move, then do that one.''

\subsection*{Length and direction, made precise}

The \emph{length} (or \emph{magnitude}, or \emph{norm}) of an arrow is how far it
reaches: a single number, zero or positive, giving the straight-line distance from tail
to tip. For the arrow that goes $2$ right and $1$ up, the Pythagorean theorem gives
length $\sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$. We write the length of $\vec{v}$ as
$\norm{\vec{v}}$, with double bars, read ``the norm of $\vec{v}$.'' The
\emph{direction} is the heading the arrow points. Chapter~4
(Section~\ref{ch04:dot-def}) makes both ideas fully precise; for now the picture is
enough: an arrow is fixed by its length and direction, and so is a vector.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} What is the length of $\colvec{-3\\4}$? Before reaching for
paper: $(-3)^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$, so its length is $\sqrt{25} = 5$. The minus sign
disappears when squared: stepping $3$ units west covers the same distance as stepping $3$
units east. Length measures distance travelled and ignores signs.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{A List Is How You Compute With an Arrow}
\label{ch02:list}

Pictures help intuition, but you cannot type an arrow into a computer. So we
pin a coordinate grid onto the plane and \emph{measure} the arrow. The arrow that goes
$2$ right and $1$ up gets recorded as the ordered list of its measurements:
\[
  \vec{v} = \colvec{2 \\ 1}.
\]
The top number, $2$, is how far the arrow reaches in the $x$-direction; the bottom
number, $1$, is how far in the $y$-direction. These numbers are the \emph{components} (or
\emph{coordinates}, or \emph{entries}) of the vector.

\begin{focusbox}
The arrow is the \emph{idea}; the list is the \emph{measurement} of that idea in a chosen
coordinate system. Change the coordinate system and the list changes, while the
displacement it describes stays the same. We will write vectors as \emph{columns} almost
everywhere in this book, because columns are what matrices act on (you will see why in
the chapter on matrices as machines).
\end{focusbox}

The \emph{order} of the entries matters. The list $\colvec{2 \\ 1}$ is
the arrow ``2 right, 1 up,'' while $\colvec{1 \\ 2}$ is the arrow ``1 right, 2 up.''
Different lists, different arrows. A vector is an \emph{ordered} list of numbers;
mathematicians call an ordered list of $n$ numbers an \emph{$n$-tuple}.

\begin{remark}
You will see vectors written as columns $\colvec{2 \\ 1}$ and, to save space in running
text, as rows $(2,1)$ or as $(2,1)\T$, where the superscript $\T$ (read ``transpose'')
means ``stand this row up as a column.'' They all mean the same arrow. The
distinction between a true \emph{row vector} and a true \emph{column vector} will
start to matter when we multiply by matrices and take transposes; until then, read a row
in prose as ``the column written sideways.''
\end{remark}

\section{Adding Vectors: Walk, Then Walk Again}
\label{ch02:add}

To add two vectors, \emph{take one step, then take the other from where you landed}.
Geometrically: place the tail of the second arrow at the head of the first; the sum is
the new arrow from the very start to the very end. This is the \emph{tip-to-tail} rule,
drawn in Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-add}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.0,>=Stealth]
  \draw[help lines,gray!30] (-0.5,-0.5) grid (5.5,4.5);
  \draw[->,thick,gray!60] (-0.5,0)--(5.7,0) node[right]{$x$};
  \draw[->,thick,gray!60] (0,-0.5)--(0,4.7) node[above]{$y$};
  % u = (3,1)
  \draw[->,very thick,vecblue] (0,0)--(3,1) node[midway,below right,fill=white,inner sep=1.2pt]{$\vec{u}$};
  % v = (1,2) starting at head of u
  \draw[->,very thick,vecred] (3,1)--(4,3) node[pos=0.32,right=2pt]{$\vec{v}$};
  % sum
  \draw[->,very thick,structurepurple] (0,0)--(4,3)
       node[midway,above left,fill=white,inner sep=1.2pt]{$\vec{u}+\vec{v}$};
  % faint parallelogram completion
  \draw[->,very thick,vecred,opacity=0.35] (0,0)--(1,2);
  \draw[->,very thick,vecblue,opacity=0.35] (1,2)--(4,3);
  \filldraw[black] (0,0) circle (1.6pt);
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Tip-to-tail addition. Start at the origin, walk along $\vec{u}=\colvec{3\\1}$,
then walk along $\vec{v}=\colvec{1\\2}$ from where you landed. The purple arrow from
start to finish is $\vec{u}+\vec{v}=\colvec{4\\3}$. The faint arrows show that adding in
the other order ($\vec{v}$ first) lands in the same place: the two routes complete a
\emph{parallelogram}.}
\label{ch02:fig-add}
\end{figure}

In coordinates, \emph{add the matching components}:
\[
  \vec{u}+\vec{v}
  = \colvec{3 \\ 1} + \colvec{1 \\ 2}
  = \colvec{3+1 \\ 1+2}
  = \colvec{4 \\ 3}.
\]
The geometric ``walk then walk'' and the algebraic ``add componentwise'' are the same
operation seen from two sides. The total rightward motion is the sum of the two rightward
motions ($3+1$); the total upward motion is the sum of the two upward motions ($1+2$).

\begin{intuitionbox}
The faint parallelogram in Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-add} shows why the order of addition
does not matter. Going $\vec{u}$ then $\vec{v}$ and going $\vec{v}$ then $\vec{u}$ both
land at the opposite corner. So $\vec{u}+\vec{v}=\vec{v}+\vec{u}$ is not an arbitrary
rule to memorize; it says that walking around either side of a parallelogram reaches the
same opposite corner.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{example}
\label{ch02:ex-add3}
Three vectors in $\R^3$. Let
\[
  \vec{a}=\colvec{1\\0\\2},\quad
  \vec{b}=\colvec{-1\\3\\1},\quad
  \vec{c}=\colvec{4\\-2\\0}.
\]
Then
\[
  \vec{a}+\vec{b}+\vec{c}
  = \colvec{1+(-1)+4 \\ 0+3+(-2) \\ 2+1+0}
  = \colvec{4 \\ 1 \\ 3}.
\]
We never had to picture three arrows in 3D at once. Componentwise addition works the same
way however many dimensions there are and however many vectors you add: add the entries
in each row separately. Because of this, we can later work in $\R^{784}$ with no extra
effort. A $28\times 28$ grayscale image is a grid of $784$ pixel brightness values, so
the whole image is one vector with $784$ components.
\end{example}

\section{Scaling Vectors: Stretch, Shrink, Flip}
\label{ch02:scale}

The second basic operation is multiplying a vector by an ordinary number. In this
context a plain number is called a \emph{scalar}, because its job is to \emph{scale} the
arrow: to change its length without changing the line it lies along.

Geometrically, $2\vec{v}$ is the arrow $\vec{v}$ stretched to twice its length, pointing
the same way. The vector $\tfrac{1}{2}\vec{v}$ is half as long. And $-\vec{v}$ has the same
length but points the \emph{opposite} way: a negative scalar flips the arrow around.
Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-scale} shows all three.

  \draw[->,thick,gray!60] (0,-2.5)--(0,2.7) node[above]{$y$};
  \draw[dashed,gray!60,thick] (-3.4,-1.7)--(4.4,2.2) node[right,gray!70!black]{\small line of all $c\vec{v}$};
  \draw[->,very thick,vecred] (0,0)--(-2,-1);
  \filldraw[vecred] (-2,-1) circle (1.8pt) node[below right]{$-\vec{v}$};
  \draw[->,very thick,structurepurple,opacity=0.6] (0,0)--(4,2);
  \filldraw[structurepurple] (4,2) circle (1.8pt) node[above left]{$2\vec{v}$};
  \draw[->,line width=1.4pt,vecblue] (0,0)--(2,1);

\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Scalar multiples of $\vec{v}=\colvec{2\\1}$ all lie on one straight line through the

scalar multiples $c\vec{v}$.}
\label{ch02:fig-scale}
\end{figure}

In coordinates, \emph{multiply every component by the scalar}:
\[
  2\colvec{2\\1}=\colvec{4\\2},\qquad
  \tfrac12\colvec{2\\1}=\colvec{1\\0.5},\qquad
  -\colvec{2\\1}=\colvec{-2\\-1}.
\]
Stretching the arrow to double length doubles how far right \emph{and} how far up it
reaches; that is why each component is multiplied by the same $2$.

\begin{focusbox}
The set of all scalar multiples of a single nonzero vector $\vec{v}$ is written
\[
  \set{t\,\vec{v} : t \in \R},
\]
read ``the set of all vectors $t\vec{v}$ such that $t$ is a real number'' (the curly
braces mean ``the set of,'' and the colon means ``such that''). This set is a
\emph{line through the origin}. Let $t$ run through all real numbers, negative and
positive, and the tip of $t\vec{v}$ traces out the whole line. This is your first
\emph{subspace}: a flat piece of space through the origin that you never leave by
scaling and adding (studied formally in Chapter 11). It is also the geometric meaning
of the word ``span'' that the next chapter is built around.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Scaling a vector is not the same as moving its tip by a fixed amount.} The vector
$2\vec{v}$ is \emph{not} ``$\vec{v}$ with each component plus $2$.'' Doubling means
\emph{multiply}, not add. From $\colvec{2\\1}$, scaling by $2$ gives $\colvec{4\\2}$,
while adding $2$ to each entry would give $\colvec{4\\3}$, a different arrow pointing in a
different direction (Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-scale-shift}). Multiplication scales along the
line; adding a constant vector slides off it.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection*{Combining the two: linear combinations}

Addition and scaling are the only two operations vectors come with, and almost everything
in this book is built by using them together. An expression like
\[
  3\vec{u} - 2\vec{v}
\]
means: stretch $\vec{u}$ by $3$, stretch $\vec{v}$ by $2$ and flip it, then add the
results tip-to-tail. With $\vec{u}=\colvec{1\\2}$ and $\vec{v}=\colvec{3\\1}$,
\[
  3\vec{u}-2\vec{v}
  = 3\colvec{1\\2} - 2\colvec{3\\1}
  = \colvec{3\\6} - \colvec{6\\2}
  = \colvec{-3\\4}.
\]
Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-lincomb} draws this. A \emph{linear combination} is a mix formed by
scaling each vector by some weight and adding the results together: choose how much of
each direction you want, then add them up. Chapter~3 (starting at
Definition~\ref{ch03:def-lincomb}) is devoted to it. We already have everything needed
to build one: scale, then add.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} If $\vec{u}=\colvec{1\\0}$ and $\vec{v}=\colvec{0\\1}$, what linear
combination gives the target vector $\colvec{4\\-3}$? In your head:
\[
  4\vec{u} + (-3)\vec{v} = 4\colvec{1\\0} - 3\colvec{0\\1} = \colvec{4\\-3}.
\]
The coordinates $4$ and $-3$ are the scaling factors (how much of each basic direction)
that build the vector.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{A Point Is Where an Arrow's Tip Lands}
\label{ch02:point}

The third reading of a vector is the one that leads to data. Take the
arrow $\colvec{2\\1}$ and draw it in standard position, with its tail at the origin. Its
tip lands at a specific spot in the plane: the location two units right and one unit up.
That \emph{location} is the point $(2,1)$, as Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-point} shows.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Arrow and point, two sides of one coin.} Anchor a vector at the origin and its
tip names a point. Conversely, any point names the vector that reaches it from the origin.
So $\colvec{2\\1}$ \emph{is} the displacement ``2 right, 1 up'' \emph{and} the location
$(2,1)$, depending on whether you care about the journey or the destination. Same list of
numbers, two readings.
\end{intuitionbox}

This identification is so useful that we usually stop distinguishing them. When we say
``the point $\vec{x}$ in $\R^2$,'' we mean the location named by the vector $\vec{x}$. The
arrow reading is best when you are \emph{moving} (adding displacements, following a
velocity); the point reading is best when you are \emph{locating} (a data record, a pixel,
a position). Train yourself to flip between them at will.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.0,>=Stealth]
  \draw[help lines,gray!30] (0,0) grid (3.5,2.5);
  \draw[->,thick,gray!60] (-0.5,0)--(3.7,0) node[right]{$x$};
  \draw[->,thick,gray!60] (0,-0.5)--(0,2.7) node[above]{$y$};
  \draw[->,very thick,vecblue] (0,0)--(2,1);
  \filldraw[vecred] (2,1) circle (2.2pt) node[above right]{$(2,1)$};
  \draw[dashed,gray] (2,0)--(2,1);
  \draw[dashed,gray] (0,1)--(2,1);
  \node[below=2pt] at (2,0) {$2$};
  \node[left=2pt] at (0,1) {$1$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{The arrow $\colvec{2\\1}$ in standard position and the point $(2,1)$ where its
tip lands. The dashed lines read off the components as coordinates. Arrow and point are
the same vector, seen as a journey or as a destination.}
\label{ch02:fig-point}
\end{figure}

\begin{connectionbox}
This is René Descartes' idea of \emph{analytic geometry} (1637): pin a coordinate grid
onto the plane so that every \emph{geometric} point becomes an \emph{algebraic} list of
numbers, and vice versa. Linear algebra takes this idea and applies it in any number of
dimensions. When you later study motion, you will use the same arrow $\leftrightarrow$
point dictionary: the position of a moving particle is a point, while its velocity and
acceleration are arrows attached to that moving point.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{The Zero Vector: Standing Still}
\label{ch02:zero}

One vector gets its own name: the \emph{zero vector}, written $\vec{0}$.
It is the vector all of whose components are $0$:
\[
  \vec{0} = \colvec{0\\0} \in \R^2,\qquad
  \vec{0} = \colvec{0\\0\\0} \in \R^3,\qquad
  \text{and so on.}
\]
As an arrow it is the ``arrow'' of length zero: the instruction ``do not move.'' As a
point it is the origin. It is the only vector with no direction at all, and it plays the
role for vectors that the number $0$ plays for numbers.

\begin{focusbox}
The zero vector is the \emph{additive identity}, meaning that adding it changes nothing:
\[
  \vec{v} + \vec{0} = \vec{v}\qquad\text{for every }\vec{v}.
\]
``Move by $\vec{v}$, then don't move'' lands you exactly where ``move by $\vec{v}$'' did.
And every vector has an \emph{opposite} that undoes it: $\vec{v} + (-\vec{v}) = \vec{0}$,
because walking out along $\vec{v}$ and back along $-\vec{v}$ returns you to start.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The zero vector $\vec{0}$ is not the number $0$.} One is a list of numbers (an
arrow, a point); the other is a single scalar. They are written almost the same and they
cooperate --- $0\,\vec{v}=\vec{0}$ for every $\vec{v}$ --- but they live in different
places. Writing ``$\vec{v}=0$'' when you mean the zero \emph{vector} is a common
shorthand, harmless as long as you know which $0$ you mean. A subtler trap: a subspace (a flat slice through
the origin that is closed under scaling and addition, studied in Chapter 11) must always
contain $\vec{0}$. A line through the origin is a subspace; a parallel line that misses
the origin is not, because it lacks the zero vector.
\end{trapbox}

\section{From the Plane to \texorpdfstring{$\R^n$}{Rn}}
\label{ch02:rn}

Everything so far has lived in the plane, which we call $\R^2$: the set of all lists of
\emph{two} real numbers. The advantage of the list picture is that nothing stops at two.

A vector in three-dimensional space $\R^3$ is a list of three numbers,
$\colvec{x\\y\\z}$, an arrow in the room you are sitting in. We can still draw it
(Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-r3}). Beyond three, pictures fail (nobody can draw four mutually
perpendicular axes), but \emph{lists} keep working. A vector in $\R^n$ is an ordered
list of $n$ real numbers,
\[
  \vec{x} = \colvec{x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n},
  \qquad x_1,\dots,x_n \in \R.
\]
Here $x_1$ is the first entry, $x_2$ the second, and so on: the small number below the
letter (the \emph{subscript}) gives the position in the list, and
``$x_1,\dots,x_n\in\R$'' says that every entry is a real number. We call $n$ the
\emph{dimension} of the space, and $x_i$ the $i$-th \emph{component}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.0,>=Stealth]
  \def\xx{-0.62} \def\xy{-0.42}
  \draw[->,thick,gray!60] (0,0)--(3.2,0) node[right]{$y$};
  \draw[->,thick,gray!60] (0,0)--(0,3.2) node[above]{$z$};
  \draw[->,thick,gray!60] (0,0)--({3.0*\xx},{3.0*\xy}) node[below left]{$x$};
  \coordinate (O) at (0,0);
  \coordinate (Px) at ({2*\xx},{2*\xy});
  \coordinate (Pxy) at ({2*\xx+3},{2*\xy});
  \coordinate (P) at ({2*\xx+3},{2*\xy+2});

\label{ch02:fig-r3}

\begin{formalbox}
\begin{definition}[Real $n$-space]
\label{ch02:def-rn}
For a positive integer $n$, \emph{real $n$-space} $\R^n$ is the set of all ordered
$n$-tuples of real numbers,
\[
  \R^n = \set[\Big]{\, \colvec{x_1\\ \vdots \\ x_n} : x_1,\dots,x_n \in \R \,}.
\]
Its elements are \emph{vectors}. Addition and scalar multiplication are defined
\emph{componentwise}: for $\vec{x},\vec{y}\in\R^n$ and a scalar $c\in\R$,
\[
  (\vec{x}+\vec{y})_i = x_i + y_i,
  \qquad
  (c\,\vec{x})_i = c\,x_i,
  \qquad i = 1,\dots,n,
\]
where $(\vec{x}+\vec{y})_i$ means the $i$-th component of $\vec{x}+\vec{y}$, and
likewise for $(c\,\vec{x})_i$. The zero vector $\vec{0}$ has every component equal to $0$.
\end{definition}
\end{formalbox}

These componentwise definitions are chosen so that
the algebra matches the geometry of arrows in every dimension. They obey a
short list of rules that we will use constantly.

\begin{formalbox}
\begin{proposition}[The vector arithmetic rules]
\label{ch02:prop-axioms}
For all $\vec{u},\vec{v},\vec{w}\in\R^n$ and all scalars $c,d\in\R$:
\begin{align*}
  &\text{(commutativity)} & \vec{u}+\vec{v} &= \vec{v}+\vec{u}, \\
  &\text{(associativity)} & (\vec{u}+\vec{v})+\vec{w} &= \vec{u}+(\vec{v}+\vec{w}), \\
  &\text{(identity)} & \vec{v}+\vec{0} &= \vec{v}, \\
  &\text{(inverse)} & \vec{v}+(-\vec{v}) &= \vec{0}, \\
  &\text{(distributivity over vectors)} & c(\vec{u}+\vec{v}) &= c\vec{u}+c\vec{v}, \\
  &\text{(distributivity over scalars)} & (c+d)\vec{v} &= c\vec{v}+d\vec{v}, \\
  &\text{(compatibility)} & c(d\vec{v}) &= (cd)\vec{v}, \\
  &\text{(unit scalar)} & 1\vec{v} &= \vec{v}.
\end{align*}
\end{proposition}
\end{formalbox}

These eight rules are the \emph{axioms} (the required starting rules) of a
\emph{vector space}, the idea met in Chapter~1: any collection of objects that can be
scaled and added without leaving the collection, while obeying these eight rules
(Chapter 11 studies vector spaces in depth). For $\R^n$ each rule is easy to prove: it is
an ordinary fact about numbers, applied in every slot at once.

\begin{proof}[Proof of commutativity, as a sample]
Compare the two sides component by component. For each $i$,
\[
  (\vec{u}+\vec{v})_i = u_i + v_i = v_i + u_i = (\vec{v}+\vec{u})_i,
\]
where the middle equality is commutativity of ordinary real-number addition. Since
the two vectors agree in every component, they are equal. The other seven rules fall the
same way: write out one component, use the matching rule for real numbers, and you are
done.
\end{proof}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Look back at the steps. The geometry of arrows in the plane gave us rules:
parallelograms, stretching, flipping. We turned those rules into componentwise
arithmetic, and that arithmetic makes sense in $\R^{100}$ or $\R^{1{,}000{,}000}$,
where no arrow can be drawn. So the algebra carries the geometry beyond what we can see.
This lets you reason \emph{geometrically} about spaces far too big to picture, trusting
that the same rules hold. When you read that a deep neural network works with vectors
in a $4096$-dimensional space, it is the same parallelogram rule and stretching, with
more slots.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Unit Vectors and the Standard Basis}
\label{ch02:basis}

Some vectors are special enough to earn names. A \emph{unit vector} is any vector of
length exactly $1$. Unit vectors are the natural way to talk about \emph{pure direction}:
strip a vector of its length and what remains is a unit vector pointing the same way.

To turn any nonzero vector into a unit vector, divide it by its own length --- a move
called \emph{normalizing}:
\[
  \hat{\vec{v}} = \frac{\vec{v}}{\norm{\vec{v}}}.
\]
A hat over a letter, as in $\hat{\vec{v}}$, marks a unit vector. For example, with
$\vec{v}=\colvec{3\\4}$,
\[
  \norm{\vec{v}}=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=\sqrt{25}=5,
  \qquad
  \hat{\vec{v}} = \frac{1}{5}\colvec{3\\4} = \colvec{0.6\\0.8},
\]
and you can check that
\[
  \norm{\hat{\vec{v}}} = \sqrt{0.6^2 + 0.8^2} = \sqrt{0.36+0.64} = \sqrt{1} = 1.
\]
Same direction as $\vec{v}$, length trimmed to $1$ (Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-normalize}).

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{You cannot normalize the zero vector.} Its length is $0$, and dividing by $0$ is
undefined --- which matches the geometry, since $\vec{0}$ has no direction to point a unit
vector along. Normalizing is a move reserved for \emph{nonzero} vectors.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection*{What is a basis?}

What does \textbf{basis}, the second word in this section's title, mean?

A \emph{basis} is a set of reference directions such that \emph{every} vector in the
space can be built from them by scaling and adding, in exactly one way, with no direction
wasted. Think of the directions as measuring rulers: each vector gets a unique recipe of
so many steps along each ruler. (Chapter~14 defines bases carefully and shows that one
space has many different bases.)

In $\R^n$ the simplest basis comes from the coordinate axes we already draw. The
\emph{standard basis} consists of the unit vectors pointing straight along each axis:
$\vec{e}_1$ along the first axis, $\vec{e}_2$ along the second, and so on. We call it
``standard'' because its directions line up with the grid we use to write down numbers.

\begin{formalbox}
\begin{definition}[Standard basis of $\R^n$]
\label{ch02:def-ei}
The \emph{standard basis} of $\R^n$ is the collection of unit vectors
$\{\vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_2, \dots, \vec{e}_n\}$, where the $i$-th \emph{standard basis vector}
$\vec{e}_i$ has a $1$ in the $i$-th slot and $0$ in all other slots:
\[
  \vec{e}_1 = \colvec{1\\0\\ \vdots \\0},\qquad
  \vec{e}_2 = \colvec{0\\1\\ \vdots \\0},\qquad
  \dots,\qquad
  \vec{e}_n = \colvec{0\\0\\ \vdots \\1}.
\]
In $\R^3$, these are
\[
  \vec{e}_1 = \colvec{1\\0\\0},\qquad
  \vec{e}_2 = \colvec{0\\1\\0},\qquad
  \vec{e}_3 = \colvec{0\\0\\1}.
\]
In physics and engineering the $\R^3$ versions are often written
$\hat{\imath},\hat{\jmath},\hat{k}$.
\end{definition}
\end{formalbox}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.4,>=Stealth]
  \draw[help lines,gray!30] (-0.4,-0.4) grid (2.4,2.4);

  \draw[->,thick,vecblue,opacity=0.6] (0,0)--(2,0) node[pos=0.75,below=2pt,vecblue!80!black]{\footnotesize $2\vec{e}_1$};
  \draw[->,thick,vecred,opacity=0.6] (2,0)--(2,1.5) node[midway,right,vecred!80!black]{\footnotesize $1.5\vec{e}_2$};
  \draw[->,very thick,structurepurple] (0,0)--(2,1.5)

assembled step by step: scale $\vec{e}_1$ by $2$, scale $\vec{e}_2$ by $1.5$, and add.}
\label{ch02:fig-basis}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-basis} shows the two standard basis vectors of $\R^2$. They matter
because \emph{every} vector in $\R^n$ is a linear combination of the standard basis
vectors, and its coordinates \emph{are} the coefficients (the numbers multiplying
each $\vec{e}_i$):
\[
  \colvec{x_1\\x_2\\\vdots\\x_n}
  = x_1\vec{e}_1 + x_2\vec{e}_2 + \cdots + x_n\vec{e}_n.
\]
For instance $\colvec{2\\1.5} = 2\vec{e}_1 + 1.5\,\vec{e}_2$ in $\R^2$, and
$\colvec{4\\-1\\7} = 4\vec{e}_1 - \vec{e}_2 + 7\vec{e}_3$ in $\R^3$.

\begin{focusbox}
This is the geometric meaning of ``a vector is a list of numbers.'' The list
$\colvec{x_1\\\vdots\\x_n}$ is a set of \emph{instructions}: take $x_1$ steps along
$\vec{e}_1$, $x_2$ steps along $\vec{e}_2$, and so on, then add them up. The standard basis
is the unstated framework behind every coordinate you have ever written. When we get to
\emph{change of basis} (describing the same vectors using a different set of reference
directions), we will swap this framework for a different one, and the same
arrow will get a different list. The arrow is real; the list depends on the basis you
measure it against.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{labbox}
Verify the assembly by hand in $\R^3$. Let $\vec{x}=\colvec{4\\-1\\7}$. Compute the linear
combination $4\vec{e}_1 - \vec{e}_2 + 7\vec{e}_3$ slot by slot:
\[
  4\colvec{1\\0\\0} - \colvec{0\\1\\0} + 7\colvec{0\\0\\1}
  = \colvec{4\\0\\0} + \colvec{0\\-1\\0} + \colvec{0\\0\\7}
  = \colvec{4\\-1\\7}.
\]
The $\vec{e}_i$ act like labelled buckets: the coefficient of $\vec{e}_i$ lands in slot
$i$ and nowhere else. That separation is what makes coordinates work.
\end{labbox}

\section{Vectors as Data: A Row of Features}
\label{ch02:data}

So far a vector has been a thing in space. The same object, an ordered list of numbers,
is also how computers store information about the world.

A single record about something in the world is a list of measured numbers, and that list
\emph{is} a vector. The idea is to let each coordinate axis stand for a different
\emph{feature} (one measured property, such as size or age) rather than a direction in
physical space.

\begin{example}[A house as a vector]
\label{ch02:ex-house}
Describe a house by three numbers: its area in square metres, its number of bedrooms, and
its age in years. A particular house then becomes the vector
\[
  \vec{h} = \colvec{120 \\ 3 \\ 15} \in \R^3
  \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
  \text{(120 m\textsuperscript{2}, \ 3 bedrooms, \ 15 years old)}.
\]
A whole neighbourhood is a \emph{cloud of points} in $\R^3$, one point per house. Data
questions then become geometric questions: ``which two houses are most alike?'' becomes
``which two points are closest?''; ``what's a typical house?'' becomes ``where is the
centre of the cloud?'' The centre is the \emph{average} of the vectors,
\[
  \bar{\vec{h}} = \frac{1}{N}\bigl(\vec{h}_1 + \vec{h}_2 + \cdots + \vec{h}_N\bigr),
\]
where $N$ is the number of houses, $\vec{h}_1,\dots,\vec{h}_N$ are their vectors, and
the bar over $\vec{h}$ marks an average. Computing it takes only vector addition and
scaling.
\end{example}

Once data is geometry, the operations of this chapter take on plain-English meanings.
Adding vectors and dividing by $N$ \emph{averages} records into a typical record, called
the \emph{centroid} (the centre point of the cloud).
Scaling a feature axis \emph{reweights} or \emph{rescales} a feature (multiply the age
coordinate by $12$ to convert years to months). Subtracting one vector from another,
$\vec{h}_2 - \vec{h}_1$, gives the \emph{difference} record (how house 2 differs from
house 1, feature by feature), and its length measures how far apart they are.
Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-data-features} shows this for two houses, keeping only two features
so that it can be drawn.

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Word embeddings and the geometry of meaning.} Language models (computer programs
that read and generate text, such as chatbots) begin by turning every word into a
vector, called a \emph{word embedding}, often in a space like $\R^{768}$. The vectors
are chosen so that words used in similar ways land near each other. The \emph{arrow}
picture survives: directions in this space carry meaning, and tip-to-tail addition
gives meaningful results. The best-known example is
\[
  \vec{v}_{\text{king}} - \vec{v}_{\text{man}} + \vec{v}_{\text{woman}}
  \;\approx\; \vec{v}_{\text{queen}},
\]
where $\vec{v}_{\text{king}}$ is the vector for the word ``king'' and so on, and
$\approx$ means ``approximately equal'': the result lands near ``queen,'' not exactly on it.
Read it as displacements: the arrow from ``man'' to ``king'' is roughly the same arrow as
from ``woman'' to ``queen'' (a ``royalty'' displacement), so adding it to ``woman''
lands you near ``queen.'' That is the parallelogram of Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-add}, in
$768$ dimensions. Recommendation systems (which suggest products or videos), search
engines, and chatbots all rely on adding and scaling vectors like the ones in this chapter.
\end{applicationbox}

\end{strangbox}

\section{Subtraction and the Arrow Between Two Points}
\label{ch02:subtract}

One more picture will be used over and over: subtraction. By definition,
\[
  \vec{u} - \vec{v} = \vec{u} + (-\vec{v}),
\]
so to subtract, flip the second arrow and add. Componentwise, subtract matching entries:
\[
  \colvec{5\\2} - \colvec{1\\3} = \colvec{4\\-1}.
\]
The picture is what to remember. Think of $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ as
\emph{points} (tips of arrows from the origin). Then $\vec{u}-\vec{v}$ is the arrow that
points \emph{from $\vec{v}$ to $\vec{u}$}: the displacement you would walk to get from the
second point to the first (Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-subtract}).

This fact is used throughout the book. The arrow between two data points is their
\emph{difference}, and its length is their \emph{distance}. Distances between data points

\emph{clustering} (grouping records that lie close together), and least squares, where
the differences between measured and predicted values are called \emph{residuals}.

of $\vec{v}$ to the tip of $\vec{u}$. Figure~\ref{ch02:fig-parallelogram-diagonals} puts
sum and difference side by side: they are the two diagonals of the parallelogram built
on $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$.

them --- and roughly how far is the walk? Now check: the displacement is
$\vec{u}-\vec{v}=\colvec{4\\-1}$ (four blocks toward larger $x$, one block toward smaller
$y$), a walk of length $\sqrt{4^2+(-1)^2}=\sqrt{17}\approx 4.1$ blocks. Did your arm point
the right way before you did any arithmetic? If so, the arrow picture has taken root.
\end{walkbox}

\section{What to Carry Forward}
\label{ch02:summary}

You now know one object in three ways.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The chapter in six lines.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A vector is an \emph{arrow} (length and direction), a \emph{list} of numbers
        (its components in a coordinate system), and a \emph{point} (where the arrow's tip
        lands from the origin) --- one object, three readings.
  \item \textbf{Addition} is tip-to-tail / componentwise: $(\vec{u}+\vec{v})_i = u_i+v_i$.
        It is commutative because parallelograms are symmetric.
  \item \textbf{Scaling} stretches, shrinks, or flips along a line through the origin:
        $(c\vec{v})_i = c\,v_i$.
  \item The \textbf{zero vector} $\vec{0}$ is ``don't move,'' the origin, and the additive
        identity --- and it is not the scalar $0$.
  \item $\R^n$ is the set of lists of $n$ numbers; the geometry of arrows survives into
        dimensions you cannot draw.
  \item The \textbf{standard basis} $\vec{e}_i$ turns coordinates into instructions:
        $\vec{x} = x_1\vec{e}_1 + \cdots + x_n\vec{e}_n$.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{historybox}
The idea of a vector began not in abstract algebra, but in the practical mechanics of weights, ropes, and celestial motions.
In 1586, the Flemish engineer and mathematician Simon Stevin published \emph{De Beghinselen der Weeghconst}
(The Principles of Statics). Stevin analyzed the balance of weights on inclined ramps and discovered the
\emph{parallelogram of forces}: two simultaneous mechanical pulls combine into a single effective pull along
the diagonal of the parallelogram they form. A century later, in 1687, Isaac Newton made this the very first
corollary to his laws of motion in the \emph{Principia Mathematica} (Corollary~1): ``A body by two forces conjoined
will describe the diagonal of a parallelogram in the same time that it would describe the sides by those forces
apart.''

For the next two centuries, mathematicians wrestled with how to formalize these physical directed segments.
In 1835, the Italian mathematician Giusto Bellavitis introduced the concept of \emph{equipollence}: two directed
line segments represent the same mathematical entity if they have the same length and direction, regardless of
where they sit on the plane. This is our modern concept of a free vector.

Viewing vectors only as geometric arrows works in two and three dimensions,
but not in modern scientific computing and data science. In
economics, finance, or machine learning, vectors live in $\R^{100}$ or $\R^{10{,}000}$
(asset returns, word embeddings, image pixels), where no arrow can be drawn on paper.
On the other hand, treating a vector as a bare list of numbers $(x_1, \dots, x_n)$
with no geometry is also risky: if you rotate your coordinate frame or change measuring units,
the numbers change completely even though the physical displacement or force is the same.
The resolution, proposed by Hermann Grassmann (1844) and made formal by Giuseppe Peano (1888),
is to recognize that coordinates only have meaning relative to a chosen basis:
\[
  \vec{x} = x_1\vec{e}_1 + x_2\vec{e}_2 + \cdots + x_n\vec{e}_n.
\]
The basis vectors $\vec{e}_i$ fix the coordinate frame; the vector $\vec{x}$ is the intrinsic geometric object.

Unifying arrows, lists, and points gave physics and mathematics one language
for quantities with several components: displacements, velocities, forces, and data
records, all scaled and added with the same componentwise arithmetic.
\end{historybox}

The next chapter (starting at Definition~\ref{ch03:def-lincomb}) takes the one operation
we built everything from, ``scale, then add,'' and asks a question that runs through all
of linear algebra: starting from a handful of vectors, \emph{which other vectors can you
reach}? That set of reachable vectors is the \emph{span}, and working it out leads to
lines, planes, subspaces, and the four fundamental subspaces that organise Strang's
course. Keep the three pictures close.

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch02:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Warm-up arithmetic and mental checks]
\label{ch02:exr-arith}
Let $\vec{u}=\colvec{2\\-1}$ and $\vec{v}=\colvec{-3\\4}$.
Compute componentwise:
(a) $\vec{u}+\vec{v}$; \quad
(b) $\vec{u}-\vec{v}$; \quad
(c) $3\vec{u}$; \quad
(d) $2\vec{u}-\tfrac12\vec{v}$; \quad
(e) $\vec{u}+\vec{v}+\vec{0}$.

\noindent
(f) \emph{Mental checks}: Without touching pen or paper, what is the length of $\colvec{0\\-7}$?
What is the length of $\colvec{3\\-4}$? If a vector $\vec{w}$ satisfies $3\vec{w}=\colvec{9\\-6}$,
what is $\vec{w}$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Draw the parallelogram]
\label{ch02:exr-parallelogram}
With $\vec{u}=\colvec{3\\1}$ and $\vec{v}=\colvec{1\\2}$, draw a coordinate grid and
carefully sketch $\vec{u}$, $\vec{v}$, $\vec{u}+\vec{v}$, and $\vec{u}-\vec{v}$, all from
the origin (draw $\vec{u}-\vec{v}$ also as the arrow between the appropriate tips). Confirm
visually that $\vec{u}$, $\vec{v}$, and $\vec{u}+\vec{v}$ form three corners of a
parallelogram, and identify the fourth corner.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Normalizing]
\label{ch02:exr-normalize}
Find the length of each vector and then its unit vector $\hat{\vec{v}}$:
(a) $\colvec{6\\8}$; \quad
(b) $\colvec{1\\1}$; \quad
(c) $\colvec{2\\-1\\2}$ in $\R^3$.
For (b), why does $\hat{\vec{v}}=\colvec{1/\sqrt2 \\ 1/\sqrt2}$ make sense geometrically?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Coordinates are coefficients]
\label{ch02:exr-basis}
Write each vector explicitly as a linear combination of standard basis vectors:
(a) $\colvec{5\\-2}$ in $\R^2$; \quad
(b) $\colvec{0\\3\\-7}$ in $\R^3$; \quad
(c) $\colvec{1\\1\\1\\1}$ in $\R^4$.
Then, going the other way, write out the single vector equal to
$2\vec{e}_1 - 5\vec{e}_3$ in $\R^3$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Scaling versus shifting]
\label{ch02:exr-trap}
A classmate claims that ``doubling a vector means adding $2$ to each entry.'' Using
$\vec{v}=\colvec{2\\1}$, compute both the true $2\vec{v}$ and the classmate's
``$\vec{v}+\colvec{2\\2}$,'' draw both results, and explain in one or two sentences the
geometric difference between scaling and shifting.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Midpoint and average]
\label{ch02:exr-midpoint}
Two points are $\vec{p}=\colvec{1\\2}$ and $\vec{q}=\colvec{5\\4}$.
(a) Compute the midpoint $\tfrac12(\vec{p}+\vec{q})$ and verify it sits halfway along the
segment from $\vec{p}$ to $\vec{q}$.
(b) Show in general that $\tfrac12(\vec{p}+\vec{q})$ is the midpoint by writing it as
$\vec{p} + \tfrac12(\vec{q}-\vec{p})$ and reading that as ``start at $\vec{p}$, walk
halfway toward $\vec{q}$.''
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A house cloud]
\label{ch02:exr-data}
Three houses are recorded as $\vec{h}_1=\colvec{100\\2\\30}$,
$\vec{h}_2=\colvec{140\\3\\10}$, $\vec{h}_3=\colvec{120\\4\\20}$ (area, bedrooms, age).
(a) Compute the ``average house'' $\bar{\vec{h}}=\tfrac13(\vec{h}_1+\vec{h}_2+\vec{h}_3)$.
(b) Compute the difference vectors $\vec{h}_2-\vec{h}_1$ and $\vec{h}_3-\vec{h}_1$ and
state, in words, how houses 2 and 3 each differ from house 1.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The arrow between points]
\label{ch02:exr-displacement}
For $\vec{u}=\colvec{7\\1}$ and $\vec{v}=\colvec{2\\3}$, find the displacement from
$\vec{v}$ to $\vec{u}$ and the displacement from $\vec{u}$ to $\vec{v}$. How are the two
related, and why does that match the picture of flipping an arrow?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Prove a rule]
\label{ch02:exr-proof}
Prove the distributive law $c(\vec{u}+\vec{v}) = c\vec{u} + c\vec{v}$ for all
$\vec{u},\vec{v}\in\R^n$ and all scalars $c\in\R$, by comparing the two sides one
component at a time (imitate the sample proof of commutativity in the text). Which single
fact about ordinary real numbers does your proof rely on?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[When is a multiple a unit vector?]
\label{ch02:exr-challenge}
Let $\vec{v}=\colvec{3\\4}$. Find \emph{every} scalar $c$ for which $c\vec{v}$ is a unit
vector. (Hint: $\norm{c\vec{v}} = \abs{c}\,\norm{\vec{v}}$; set this equal to $1$.) How
many such $c$ are there, and where do the two resulting vectors point?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Collinear or not --- a geometric eye]
\label{ch02:exr-collinear}
Three points are $\vec{a}=\colvec{1\\1}$, $\vec{b}=\colvec{3\\2}$, $\vec{c}=\colvec{7\\4}$.
By comparing the displacement $\vec{b}-\vec{a}$ with $\vec{c}-\vec{a}$, decide whether the
three points lie on a single straight line. (They are collinear exactly when one
displacement is a scalar multiple of the other --- connect this to the line-through-the-origin
picture of scalar multiples.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The triangle inequality: why straight is shortest]
\label{ch02:exr-triangle-inequality}
In any triangle, the length of any one side cannot exceed the sum of the lengths of the other two sides.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Draw $\vec{u}=\colvec{3\\0}$ and $\vec{v}=\colvec{0\\4}$ tip-to-tail. Compute $\norm{\vec{u}}$,
        $\norm{\vec{v}}$, and $\norm{\vec{u}+\vec{v}}$. Verify that the strict inequality
        $\norm{\vec{u}+\vec{v}} < \norm{\vec{u}} + \norm{\vec{v}}$ holds.
  \item When does equality hold: $\norm{\vec{u}+\vec{v}} = \norm{\vec{u}} + \norm{\vec{v}}$? State the
        precise geometric condition on $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ in plain English, and verify it with a simple
        numerical example.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
