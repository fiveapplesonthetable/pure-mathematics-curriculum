\chapter{Projections}

Suppose you stand in a flat field off a straight road and want the shortest walk
to the road. You walk straight toward it, meeting the road at a right angle.
That point, the foot of the perpendicular, is the closest point of the road. It is called the
\emph{orthogonal projection} of your position onto the road (``orthogonal'' because you meet the
road at a right angle).

The same picture works when we move from a line in
$\R^2$ to a plane in $\R^3$ or an $r$-dimensional subspace inside $\R^n$: to
find the point in a subspace closest to $\vec{b}$, drop a perpendicular.

Projections matter because linear systems that come from measurements are
often overdetermined: they have more equations than unknowns. In $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$, when
$\vec{b}$ is not in the column space $\col(\mat{A})$, there is no exact solution. Instead we look
for the reachable vector closest to $\vec{b}$, which is the orthogonal projection of $\vec{b}$ onto
$\col(\mat{A})$. This is the idea behind \emph{least squares}, the standard way to fit a model to
noisy data, which the next chapter develops.

\section{Projecting onto a line}

Start with the simplest case: a single line through the origin, and a point we
want to approximate. Let the line be the span of a vector $\vec{a}$, and let
$\vec{b}$ be the point we are trying to reach. The line is
$\set{\,t\vec{a} : t \in \R\,}$ -- all scalar multiples of $\vec{a}$. We seek
the point $\vec{p}$ on this line that minimizes the distance $\norm{\vec{b}-\vec{p}}$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The projection $\vec{p}$ is the point on the line closest to $\vec{b}$. The segment
connecting $\vec{b}$ to $\vec{p}$ meets the line at an exact right angle. This
perpendicular vector is the \emph{error} $\vec{e} = \vec{b} - \vec{p}$, and
demanding that the error be orthogonal to $\vec{a}$ uniquely determines $\vec{p}$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Finding the projection from the right angle}

Because $\vec{p}$ lies on the line, it is \emph{some} multiple of $\vec{a}$:
\[
  \vec{p} = \hat{x}\,\vec{a}
\]
for an unknown scalar $\hat{x}$ (read ``$x$-hat''; the hat will always mean ``our best estimate'').
The whole problem is to find this one number. We have one fact to pin it down: the error
$\vec{e} = \vec{b} - \vec{p} = \vec{b} - \hat{x}\vec{a}$ must be \emph{perpendicular to the line},
which means perpendicular to $\vec{a}$. Perpendicular means the dot product is zero:
\[
  \vec{a}\T(\vec{b} - \hat{x}\vec{a}) = 0.
\]
That is one linear equation in the one unknown $\hat{x}$. Expand it:
\[
  \vec{a}\T\vec{b} - \hat{x}\,\vec{a}\T\vec{a} = 0
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  \hat{x} = \frac{\vec{a}\T\vec{b}}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}.
\]
And then the projection itself is
\[
  \boxed{\;\vec{p} = \hat{x}\,\vec{a} = \frac{\vec{a}\T\vec{b}}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}\,\vec{a}.\;}
\]

\begin{focusbox}
The projection of $\vec{b}$ onto the line $\spn(\vec{a})$ is
\[
  \vec{p} = \frac{\vec{a}\T\vec{b}}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}\,\vec{a},
  \qquad
  \hat{x} = \frac{\vec{a}\T\vec{b}}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}.
\]
The numerator $\vec{a}\T\vec{b}$ measures how much $\vec{b}$ leans along $\vec{a}$; the denominator
$\vec{a}\T\vec{a}=\norm{\vec{a}}^2$ corrects for the length of $\vec{a}$. Rescaling $\vec{a}$ does not
change $\vec{p}$: only the line matters, not which arrow on it we picked.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}\label{ch18:line-numeric}
Project $\vec{b}=\colvec{2\\3}$ onto the line through $\vec{a}=\colvec{1\\0}$ (the $x$-axis).
\[
  \vec{a}\T\vec{b} = 1\cdot 2 + 0\cdot 3 = 2,
  \qquad
  \vec{a}\T\vec{a} = 1,
  \qquad
  \hat{x} = \tfrac{2}{1} = 2,
\]
so $\vec{p} = 2\colvec{1\\0} = \colvec{2\\0}$ and the error is
$\vec{e} = \vec{b}-\vec{p} = \colvec{0\\3}$. This matches the picture: projecting onto the $x$-axis
keeps the $x$-coordinate and sets the $y$-coordinate to zero. The error $\vec{e}=\colvec{0\\3}$ is
perpendicular to $\vec{a}=\colvec{1\\0}$, since their dot product is $0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ch18:line-diagonal}
Now a case you could not eyeball. Project $\vec{b}=\colvec{1\\4}$ onto the line through
$\vec{a}=\colvec{2\\1}$.
\[
  \vec{a}\T\vec{b} = 2\cdot1 + 1\cdot4 = 6,
  \qquad
  \vec{a}\T\vec{a} = 2^2 + 1^2 = 5,
  \qquad
  \hat{x} = \tfrac{6}{5}.
\]
So
\[
  \vec{p} = \tfrac{6}{5}\colvec{2\\1} = \colvec{12/5\\6/5},
  \qquad
  \vec{e} = \vec{b}-\vec{p} = \colvec{1-12/5\\ 4-6/5} = \colvec{-7/5\\14/5}.
\]
Check perpendicularity: $\vec{a}\T\vec{e} = 2(-7/5)+1(14/5) = -14/5+14/5 = 0$, so the error is
orthogonal to the line (Figure~\ref{ch18:line-diagonal-fig}). Dotting the error with $\vec{a}$ and
getting zero is the quickest way to catch an arithmetic slip in any projection problem.
\end{example}

\begin{walkbox}
Take the formula $\vec{p} = \dfrac{\vec{a}\T\vec{b}}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}\,\vec{a}$ on a walk and ask:
what happens if $\vec{b}$ is already \emph{on} the line, say $\vec{b}=5\vec{a}$? What should the
shadow be, and does the formula give it? And what if $\vec{b}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{a}$ -- where
does the shadow land then? These are two checks that every projection must pass.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{Packaging the projection as a matrix}

Look again at $\vec{p} = \dfrac{\vec{a}\T\vec{b}}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}\,\vec{a}$. The quantity
$\vec{a}\T\vec{b}$ is a number, so we may move it to the other side of $\vec{a}$ and regroup:
\begin{align*}
  \vec{p} &= \vec{a}\,\frac{\vec{a}\T\vec{b}}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}
           = \frac{\vec{a}\,\vec{a}\T}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}\,\vec{b}
           = \mat{P}\,\vec{b},
  \qquad\text{where}\qquad
  \mat{P} = \frac{\vec{a}\,\vec{a}\T}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}.
\end{align*}
The object $\vec{a}\,\vec{a}\T$ is a \emph{column times a row} (the outer product from Chapter~8):
an $n\times1$ times a $1\times n$, so it is an $n\times n$ \emph{matrix}, not a number. (Compare
$\vec{a}\T\vec{a}$, a row times a column, which is a single number.) Dividing that matrix by the
number $\vec{a}\T\vec{a}$ gives the \textbf{projection matrix} $\mat{P}$. It does not depend on
$\vec{b}$ at all. Build it once, and then projecting \emph{any} vector onto the line is a single
matrix multiplication $\vec{p}=\mat{P}\vec{b}$.

\begin{trapbox}
Keep the two products straight. $\vec{a}\T\vec{a}$ (row times column) is a \emph{scalar}; for
$\vec{a}=\colvec{2\\1}$ it is $5$. But $\vec{a}\,\vec{a}\T$ (column times row) is a \emph{matrix}:
\[
  \vec{a}\,\vec{a}\T = \colvec{2\\1}\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Swapping them is the most common projection mistake. Order matters with $\T$.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{example}\label{ch18:line-Pmatrix}
For $\vec{a}=\colvec{2\\1}$ we found $\vec{a}\,\vec{a}\T=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$ and
$\vec{a}\T\vec{a}=5$, so
\[
  \mat{P} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}
          = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0.4 \\ 0.4 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Apply it to $\vec{b}=\colvec{1\\4}$ from Example~\ref{ch18:line-diagonal}:
\[
  \mat{P}\vec{b} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\colvec{1\\4}
  = \frac{1}{5}\colvec{4+8\\2+4} = \frac{1}{5}\colvec{12\\6} = \colvec{12/5\\6/5}.
\]
This is the same $\vec{p}$ as before.
\end{example}

This $\mat{P}$ already has the two properties that, as Section~\ref{ch18:sec-two-props} shows,
every projection matrix has. It is \textbf{symmetric}: $\mat{P}=\mat{P}\T$ (the two off-diagonal
entries are both $0.4$). And projecting twice gives nothing new: once you are on the line,
projecting again leaves you where you are, so $\mat{P}^2=\mat{P}$. Here is the check for the
second property:
\begin{align*}
  \mat{P}^2 &= \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}
             = \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix}16+4 & 8+2\\ 8+2 & 4+1\end{bmatrix}\\
            &= \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix}20&10\\10&5\end{bmatrix}
             = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix} = \mat{P}.
\end{align*}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Geometrically, an orthogonal projection keeps the part of a vector that lies along the line (or,
later in this chapter, inside a subspace $S$) and throws away the part perpendicular to it. The
equation $\mat{P}^2 = \mat{P}$ records this: once a vector has been projected, projecting it a
second time leaves it unchanged. A matrix with $\mat{P}^2 = \mat{P}$ is called \emph{idempotent}
(Latin for ``the same power'': every power $\mat{P}^2,\mat{P}^3,\dots$ equals $\mat{P}$ itself).
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Why the perpendicular is the closest point}

So far we have assumed that the foot of the perpendicular is the nearest point. The Pythagorean
theorem proves it, for a line or for any subspace.

\begin{theorem}[The projection is the closest point]\label{ch18:closest-thm}
Let $\vec{p}$ be the projection of $\vec{b}$ onto a subspace $S$, so that the error
$\vec{e}=\vec{b}-\vec{p}$ is orthogonal to $S$. Then for \emph{every} other point $\vec{y}\in S$,
\[
  \norm{\vec{b}-\vec{y}} \;\ge\; \norm{\vec{b}-\vec{p}},
\]
with equality only when $\vec{y}=\vec{p}$. The projection is strictly the closest point of $S$ to
$\vec{b}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Pick any $\vec{y}\in S$ and split the trip from $\vec{b}$ to $\vec{y}$ into two legs through
$\vec{p}$:
\[
  \vec{b}-\vec{y} = (\vec{b}-\vec{p}) + (\vec{p}-\vec{y}).
\]
The first leg $\vec{b}-\vec{p}=\vec{e}$ is orthogonal to $S$. The second leg $\vec{p}-\vec{y}$
lives \emph{inside} $S$, because $\vec{p}$ and $\vec{y}$ are both in $S$ and the difference of two
vectors of a subspace is again in the subspace. A vector orthogonal to $S$ and a vector inside $S$
are perpendicular to each other, so Pythagoras applies:
\[
  \norm{\vec{b}-\vec{y}}^2 = \norm{\vec{b}-\vec{p}}^2 + \norm{\vec{p}-\vec{y}}^2.
\]
The right side is $\norm{\vec{b}-\vec{p}}^2$ plus something that is never negative, so it is at
least $\norm{\vec{b}-\vec{p}}^2$. It equals it only when $\norm{\vec{p}-\vec{y}}^2=0$, i.e.\ when
$\vec{y}=\vec{p}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{focusbox}
Everything in this chapter comes from one condition:
\textbf{the error vector is orthogonal to the subspace}. By the Pythagorean
theorem, the foot of the perpendicular is then strictly closer to $\vec{b}$
than any other point of the subspace.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Projecting onto a subspace}

Now we leave the line behind. Suppose $S$ is not a single direction but a whole subspace -- a plane
in $\R^3$, or an $r$-dimensional flat slab inside $\R^n$. Give $S$ a set of basis vectors and stack
them as the columns of a matrix:
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{a}_1 & \vec{a}_2 & \cdots & \vec{a}_r \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  S = \col(\mat{A}).
\]
Every point of $S$ is a combination of the columns, i.e.\ has the form $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ for some
coefficient vector $\vec{x}\in\R^r$. So projecting $\vec{b}$ onto $S$ means: find the coefficient
vector $\hat{\vec{x}}$ that makes $\vec{p}=\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$ as close to $\vec{b}$ as possible.

\begin{intuitionbox}
For an $r$-dimensional subspace we need $r$ numbers
$\hat{\vec{x}} = (\hat{x}_1,\dots,\hat{x}_r)$, the coefficients of the best
combination $\vec{p} = \mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}} = \hat{x}_1\vec{a}_1+\dots+\hat{x}_r\vec{a}_r$.
As with a line, one condition pins them down: the error
$\vec{e} = \vec{b} - \mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$ must be orthogonal to every direction in $S$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Why the error must be perpendicular to every column}

Theorem~\ref{ch18:closest-thm} says: \emph{if} the error is perpendicular to $S$, the point is the
closest one. Now run the argument the other way. Why must the closest point have a perpendicular
error? Because if the error leans toward any column, you can slide along that column and get
closer.

Here is that sentence as algebra. Take any point $\vec{q}=\mat{A}\vec{x}$ of $S$, with error
$\vec{b}-\vec{q}$, and suppose the error leans toward the first column: its dot product with
$\vec{a}_1$ is some number $c\neq 0$. Slide from $\vec{q}$ a distance $t$ along $\vec{a}_1$. The
new point $\vec{q}+t\vec{a}_1$ is still in $S$ (it is a combination of the columns), and its
squared distance to $\vec{b}$ is
\begin{align*}
  \norm{\vec{b}-(\vec{q}+t\vec{a}_1)}^2
    &= \bigl((\vec{b}-\vec{q})-t\vec{a}_1\bigr)\T\bigl((\vec{b}-\vec{q})-t\vec{a}_1\bigr)
      && \text{(squared length is a dot product)}\\
    &= \norm{\vec{b}-\vec{q}}^2 - 2t\,\vec{a}_1\T(\vec{b}-\vec{q}) + t^2\norm{\vec{a}_1}^2
      && \text{(multiply out)}\\
    &= \norm{\vec{b}-\vec{q}}^2 - 2tc + t^2\norm{\vec{a}_1}^2
      && \text{(the lean is $c$).}
\end{align*}
Choose $t$ small and with the same sign as $c$. Then $-2tc$ is negative, and for small $t$ it
outweighs the positive $t^2\norm{\vec{a}_1}^2$ (any $t$ between $0$ and $2c/\norm{\vec{a}_1}^2$
works). The distance went down, so $\vec{q}$ was not the closest point
(Figure~\ref{ch18:lean-fig}). The same holds for every column. So at the closest point
$\vec{p}=\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$ the error has zero lean toward every column:
\[
  \vec{a}_1\T\vec{e}=0,\quad \vec{a}_2\T\vec{e}=0,\quad\dots,\quad \vec{a}_r\T\vec{e}=0.
\]

Why is checking the columns enough to make $\vec{e}$ perpendicular to all of $S$? Any vector in
$S$ has the form $\mat{A}\vec{y}$. If $\mat{A}\T\vec{e}=\vec{0}$, then its dot product with
$\vec{e}$ is
\[
  (\mat{A}\vec{y})\T\vec{e} = \vec{y}\T(\mat{A}\T\vec{e}) = \vec{y}\T\vec{0} = 0.
\]

\subsection{The normal equations}

The $r$ conditions $\vec{a}_j\T\vec{e}=0$ stack into one matrix equation, because the rows of
$\mat{A}\T$ are the columns of $\mat{A}$:
\[
  \mat{A}\T\vec{e}
  = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{a}_1\T \\ \vdots \\ \vec{a}_r\T \end{bmatrix}\vec{e}
  = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{a}_1\T\vec{e} \\ \vdots \\ \vec{a}_r\T\vec{e} \end{bmatrix}
  = \vec{0}.
\]
Now write the error in terms of the unknown coefficients and tidy up:
\begin{align*}
  \mat{A}\T\vec{e} &= \vec{0}
     && \text{(no lean toward any column)}\\
  \mat{A}\T(\vec{b}-\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}) &= \vec{0}
     && \text{(the error is $\vec{e}=\vec{b}-\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$)}\\
  \mat{A}\T\vec{b}-\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\,\hat{\vec{x}} &= \vec{0}
     && \text{(multiply out)}\\
  \mat{A}\T\mat{A}\,\hat{\vec{x}} &= \mat{A}\T\vec{b}
     && \text{(move one term across).}
\end{align*}
\begin{equation}\label{ch18:normal-eq}
  \boxed{\;\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\,\hat{\vec{x}} = \mat{A}\T\vec{b}\;}
\end{equation}
These are the \textbf{normal equations}. The name uses an old meaning of the word:
\emph{normal} means \emph{perpendicular} (from the Latin \emph{norma}, a carpenter's square for
drawing right angles). The equations are the right-angle condition $\mat{A}\T\vec{e}=\vec{0}$,
rewritten in terms of $\hat{\vec{x}}$.

\subsection{Reading the normal equations aloud}

Each side of \eqref{ch18:normal-eq} is a table of dot products. With two columns,
\[
  \mat{A}\T\mat{A} =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \vec{a}_1\T\vec{a}_1 & \vec{a}_1\T\vec{a}_2\\
    \vec{a}_2\T\vec{a}_1 & \vec{a}_2\T\vec{a}_2
  \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{A}\T\vec{b} =
  \begin{bmatrix} \vec{a}_1\T\vec{b} \\ \vec{a}_2\T\vec{b} \end{bmatrix},
\]
because entry $(i,j)$ of a product is row $i$ of the first factor dotted with column $j$ of the
second (Figure~\ref{ch18:ata-grid}). Read them like this.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$ lists \emph{how far $\vec{b}$ reaches along each column}. This is all
        the equations ever learn about $\vec{b}$.
  \item $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ lists \emph{how the columns overlap with each other}. The diagonal holds
        their squared lengths; the off-diagonal entries say how much each pair points the same way.
        It is symmetric, since $\vec{a}_1\T\vec{a}_2=\vec{a}_2\T\vec{a}_1$. (It is often called
        the \emph{Gram matrix} of the columns.)
  \item The equation itself, written as $\mat{A}\T(\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}})=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$, says:
        the point $\vec{p}=\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$ has the \emph{same} dot product with every column
        as $\vec{b}$ does. Measured along any column, $\vec{p}$ and $\vec{b}$ look alike. They
        differ only by $\vec{e}$, which no column can see.
\end{itemize}
If the columns are perpendicular to each other, the off-diagonal entries are zero, and the system
falls apart into separate line projections, $\hat{x}_j=\vec{a}_j\T\vec{b}/\vec{a}_j\T\vec{a}_j$.
Overlap between columns is what couples the unknowns, and solving the system untangles it.

Compare the line and the subspace side by side:
\[
  \text{line:}\quad (\vec{a}\T\vec{a})\,\hat{x} = \vec{a}\T\vec{b},
  \qquad\qquad
  \text{subspace:}\quad (\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\,\hat{\vec{x}} = \mat{A}\T\vec{b}.
\]
The scalar $\vec{a}\T\vec{a}$ grew into the $r\times r$ matrix $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$; the number
$\vec{a}\T\vec{b}$ grew into the vector $\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$. Same skeleton, more dimensions.

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{The projection matrix.} If the columns of $\mat{A}$ are independent, then $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$
is invertible, and we may solve the normal equations:
\[
  \hat{\vec{x}} = (\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T\vec{b},
  \qquad
  \vec{p} = \mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}} = \mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T\,\vec{b}.
\]
The matrix that projects any $\vec{b}$ onto $\col(\mat{A})$ is therefore
\[
  \boxed{\;\mat{P} = \mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T.\;}
\]
\end{formalbox}

Read $\mat{P}\vec{b}=\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$ from right to left, as three
machines in a row (Figure~\ref{ch18:P-pipeline}). First $\mat{A}\T$ measures $\vec{b}$: it
produces the $r$ dot products $\vec{a}_j\T\vec{b}$. Next $(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv$ corrects those
measurements for the lengths and overlaps of the columns, turning them into the coefficients
$\hat{\vec{x}}$. Finally $\mat{A}$ builds the combination $\hat{x}_1\vec{a}_1+\dots+\hat{x}_r\vec{a}_r$,
which is the shadow $\vec{p}$. The middle step is the only hard one, and it disappears when the
columns are perpendicular unit vectors, because then $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}=\mat{I}$
(Exercise~\ref{ch18:ex-orthonormal}).

When $\mat{A}$ is a single column $\vec{a}$, this reduces to the line formula. Then
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}=\vec{a}\T\vec{a}$ is a number, $(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv$ is
$1/(\vec{a}\T\vec{a})$, and
\[
  \mat{P}=\vec{a}(\vec{a}\T\vec{a})\inv\vec{a}\T = \frac{\vec{a}\,\vec{a}\T}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}.
\]

\begin{trapbox}
You might be tempted to ``simplify''
$\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$ by distributing the inverse:
$\mat{A}\mat{A}\inv(\mat{A}\T)\inv\mat{A}\T = \mat{I}$. \textbf{Do not.} That step is only allowed
when $\mat{A}$ is square and invertible (the check below covers that case). The interesting case is
the rectangular one ($n>r$): $\mat{A}$ has more rows than columns, $\mat{A}\inv$ does not exist,
and $\mat{P}$ is \emph{not} the identity. Treat $(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv$ as one indivisible block.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: when $\mat{A}$ is square and invertible.} What happens to the formula
$\mat{P} = \mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$ if $\mat{A}$ is a square, invertible $n\times n$ matrix?
\emph{Pause and predict before checking the algebra:} If the columns of $\mat{A}$ form a basis of all of
$\R^n$, then $\col(\mat{A})$ is the whole of $\R^n$. Every vector $\vec{b}$ already lives in the
subspace, so the closest point to $\vec{b}$ is $\vec{b}$ itself, and the projection must be the
identity matrix $\mat{I}$. In this square invertible case we may distribute the inverse:
\[
  \mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T = \mat{A}\mat{A}\inv(\mat{A}\T)\inv\mat{A}\T
  = \mat{I}\cdot\mat{I} = \mat{I}.
\]
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Why $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is invertible when the columns are independent}

The formula for $\mat{P}$ assumed that $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is invertible. Here is the proof. It also
tells you when the formula works: whenever the columns of $\mat{A}$ are independent.

\begin{lemma}\label{ch18:ata-invertible}
If the columns of $\mat{A}$ are linearly independent, then $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is invertible.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is a square $r\times r$ matrix, so it suffices to show its nullspace is trivial:
the only $\vec{x}$ with $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{0}$ is $\vec{x}=\vec{0}$. Suppose
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{0}$. Dot both sides with $\vec{x}$:
\[
  \vec{x}\T\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vec{x} = 0
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  (\mat{A}\vec{x})\T(\mat{A}\vec{x}) = 0
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  \norm{\mat{A}\vec{x}}^2 = 0.
\]
A vector with zero length is the zero vector, so $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{0}$. But the columns of
$\mat{A}$ are independent, which means the \emph{only} combination of them giving zero is the
trivial one: $\vec{x}=\vec{0}$. Hence $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ has trivial nullspace and is invertible.
\end{proof}

The middle step is a trick worth keeping: $\vec{x}\T\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}=\norm{\mat{A}\vec{x}}^2$
turns a matrix expression into a squared length, which can only be zero one way. You will see this
move again in Chapter~27 on positive-definite matrices (symmetric matrices $\mat{S}$ with
$\vec{x}\T\mat{S}\vec{x}>0$ for every nonzero $\vec{x}$), where it is the central tool.

\subsection{What goes wrong when the columns are dependent}

If the columns are dependent, the argument runs backwards: some $\vec{x}\neq\vec{0}$ has
$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{0}$, hence $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{0}$, and $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is
singular. The geometry is still fine. The columns span some subspace, and $\vec{b}$ still has
exactly one closest point $\vec{p}$ in it. What fails is the \emph{description} of $\vec{p}$: there
are several ways to write it as a combination of the columns, so $\hat{\vec{x}}$ is not unique.

\begin{example}\label{ch18:dependent}
Take $\vec{a}_1=\colvec{1\\2\\0}$ and $\vec{a}_2=\colvec{2\\4\\0}=2\vec{a}_1$, and
$\vec{b}=\colvec{0\\5\\1}$. The two columns lie on one line, so $S$ is that line, and the line
formula gives $\vec{p}=\frac{10}{5}\vec{a}_1=\colvec{2\\4\\0}$. The normal equations are
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 10\\ 10 & 20 \end{bmatrix}\colvec{\hat{x}_1\\ \hat{x}_2}
  = \colvec{10\\20}.
\]
The second row is twice the first, so there is only one condition, $\hat{x}_1+2\hat{x}_2=2$. Every
solution, such as $(2,0)$, $(0,1)$ or $(4,-1)$, gives the same $\vec{p}=\hat{x}_1\vec{a}_1+
\hat{x}_2\vec{a}_2=\colvec{2\\4\\0}$. The cure is to delete the redundant column $\vec{a}_2$; the
subspace, and so the projection, do not change.
\end{example}

\section{The two properties that define a projection}\label{ch18:sec-two-props}

Whatever $\mat{A}$ is (with independent columns), the matrix
$\mat{P}=\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$ has the two properties we met for the line. They
characterize orthogonal projections: conversely, any matrix with both properties is the orthogonal
projection onto its own column space (we do not prove this converse here).

\begin{theorem}[Symmetry and idempotence]\label{ch18:P-props}
The projection matrix $\mat{P}=\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$ satisfies
\[
  \text{(1)}\quad \mat{P}\T = \mat{P}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \text{(2)}\quad \mat{P}^2 = \mat{P}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Symmetry.} Transpose, remembering that $(\mat{XYZ})\T=\mat{Z}\T\mat{Y}\T\mat{X}\T$ and that
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is itself symmetric (so its inverse is too):
\begin{align*}
  \mat{P}\T &= \big(\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T\big)\T\\
            &= (\mat{A}\T)\T\,\big((\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\big)\T\,\mat{A}\T\\
            &= \mat{A}\,(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\,\mat{A}\T = \mat{P}.
\end{align*}
\emph{Idempotence.} Multiply $\mat{P}$ by itself and watch the middle collapse. Let
$\mat{M}=(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv$ for brevity:
\[
  \mat{P}^2 = \mat{A}\mat{M}\mat{A}\T\,\mat{A}\mat{M}\mat{A}\T
  = \mat{A}\mat{M}\,(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\,\mat{M}\,\mat{A}\T.
\]
But $\mat{M}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A}) = (\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv(\mat{A}\T\mat{A}) = \mat{I}$, so the middle
three factors $\mat{M}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\mat{M}$ collapse to $\mat{M}$, leaving
$\mat{P}^2 = \mat{A}\mat{M}\mat{A}\T = \mat{P}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Both properties have a geometric meaning. Idempotence $\mat{P}^2=\mat{P}$: once $\vec{b}$'s shadow
has landed in $S$, it is already in $S$, so projecting it again does nothing. Symmetry
$\mat{P}\T=\mat{P}$ is subtler. It says the projection treats any two vectors $\vec{b}$ and
$\vec{c}$ evenly: the amount of $\vec{c}$ along $\vec{b}$'s shadow equals the amount of $\vec{b}$
along $\vec{c}$'s shadow, that is, $\vec{c}\T(\mat{P}\vec{b}) = (\mat{P}\vec{c})\T\vec{b}$. Drop
symmetry and you get a \emph{slanted} (oblique) projection that still satisfies
$\mat{P}^2=\mat{P}$ but no longer drops a true perpendicular, and no longer finds the closest point
(Figure~\ref{ch18:orthog-vs-oblique}).
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: projecting the zero vector.} Where does any projection matrix send $\vec{0}$?
And if a matrix satisfies $\mat{P}\vec{v} = \vec{v}$ for \emph{every} vector $\vec{v}$ in $\R^n$, what is $\mat{P}$?
\emph{Pause and think:} $\mat{P}\vec{0} = \vec{0}$, because every linear subspace passes through the
origin. If $\mat{P}\vec{v}=\vec{v}$ everywhere, the subspace is the entire ambient space $\R^n$, so the error is
always zero and $\mat{P} = \mat{I}$.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{historybox}
That the shortest path from a point to a plane is the perpendicular dropped onto it was known to
ancient Greek geometers. Writing that perpendicular drop as a matrix $\mat{P}$ that acts on vectors
with any number of entries came much later, in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

In the early 1900s, David Hilbert, Maurice Fréchet and others carried the idea over to spaces whose
``vectors'' are functions, with infinitely many coordinates. The projection theorem that came out of
this work says the same thing as this chapter: (for suitable subspaces) every vector splits in
exactly one way into a part inside the subspace and a perpendicular error, and that part is the
closest point of the subspace.
\end{historybox}

\begin{remark}
The complementary matrix $\mat{I}-\mat{P}$ is \emph{also} a projection. It projects onto the
orthogonal complement $S^\perp$ of $S$ (all vectors perpendicular to $S$, Chapter~15), which is
where the errors live. Check:
\[
  (\mat{I}-\mat{P})^2 = \mat{I}-2\mat{P}+\mat{P}^2 = \mat{I}-2\mat{P}+\mat{P} = \mat{I}-\mat{P},
\]
and it is symmetric because $\mat{P}$ is. So $\mat{P}$ keeps the part of $\vec{b}$ inside $S$, and
$\mat{I}-\mat{P}$ keeps the part orthogonal to $S$. Together they split every vector,
$\vec{b} = \mat{P}\vec{b} + (\mat{I}-\mat{P})\vec{b} = \vec{p} + \vec{e}$, as
Figure~\ref{ch18:decomp-fig} shows.
\end{remark}

\section{A fully worked plane in three dimensions}

Here is one projection onto a plane in $\R^3$, computed from start to finish.

\begin{example}\label{ch18:plane-worked}
Project $\vec{b}=\colvec{6\\0\\0}$ onto the plane $S$ spanned by
$\vec{a}_1=\colvec{1\\1\\0}$ and $\vec{a}_2=\colvec{0\\1\\1}$. Assemble
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
\textbf{Step 1: form $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$.}
\[
  \mat{A}\T\mat{A}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Read the entries as dot products of the columns: $\vec{a}_1\T\vec{a}_1=\vec{a}_2\T\vec{a}_2=2$ (each
column has squared length $2$), and $\vec{a}_1\T\vec{a}_2=1$ (the columns overlap in their middle
entry).

\textbf{Step 2: form $\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$.}
\[
  \mat{A}\T\vec{b}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\colvec{6\\0\\0}
  = \colvec{6\\0}.
\]
So $\vec{b}$ reaches $6$ along $\vec{a}_1$ and $0$ along $\vec{a}_2$.

\textbf{Step 3: solve the normal equations $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\,\hat{\vec{x}}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$.}
The inverse of $\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ is
$\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$ (determinant $4-1=3$), so
\[
  \hat{\vec{x}} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\colvec{6\\0}
  = \frac{1}{3}\colvec{12\\-6} = \colvec{4\\-2}.
\]
\textbf{Step 4: form the projection $\vec{p}=\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}$.}
\[
  \vec{p} = \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\colvec{4\\-2}
  = \colvec{4\\\,4-2\,\\-2} = \colvec{4\\2\\-2}.
\]
\textbf{Step 5: the error and the check.}
\[
  \vec{e} = \vec{b}-\vec{p} = \colvec{6\\0\\0}-\colvec{4\\2\\-2} = \colvec{2\\-2\\2}.
\]
Is $\vec{e}$ perpendicular to the plane? Dot it with each basis vector:
\[
  \vec{a}_1\T\vec{e} = 1\cdot2 + 1\cdot(-2) + 0\cdot2 = 0,
  \qquad
  \vec{a}_2\T\vec{e} = 0\cdot2 + 1\cdot(-2) + 1\cdot2 = 0.
\]
Both are zero, so the error is orthogonal to the plane and $\vec{p}=\colvec{4\\2\\-2}$ is the
closest point of $S$ to $\vec{b}$. The error $\vec{e}=\colvec{2\\-2\\2}=2\colvec{1\\-1\\1}$ points
along the plane's \emph{normal} direction $\colvec{1\\-1\\1}$, the one direction perpendicular to
both $\vec{a}_1$ and $\vec{a}_2$, as it must. Figure~\ref{ch18:plane-fig} shows the general picture.
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Build the full $3\times3$ projection matrix for that plane. With
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ and inverse
$\frac13\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$, multiply out
$\mat{P}=\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$. You should land on
\[
  \mat{P} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Check three things by hand: (a) $\mat{P}$ is symmetric; (b) $\mat{P}\vec{b}=\colvec{4\\2\\-2}$ for
$\vec{b}=\colvec{6\\0\\0}$, matching Step~4; (c) the \emph{trace} (the sum of the diagonal entries,
$\tr(\mat{P}) = P_{11}+P_{22}+P_{33}$) is $\frac13(2+2+2)=2$, which equals the dimension
of the plane. This is true of every projection matrix: the sum of its diagonal entries equals the
dimension of the subspace it projects onto (Exercise~\ref{ch18:ex-trace} shows why).
\end{labbox}

\section{Two special cases worth checking}

Two extreme cases test the formula $\mat{P}=\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$.

\textbf{When $\vec{b}$ is already in the space.} If $\vec{b}\in\col(\mat{A})$ then its closest point
is itself, so we expect $\mat{P}\vec{b}=\vec{b}$. Indeed, $\vec{b}=\mat{A}\vec{c}$ for some
$\vec{c}$, and
\begin{align*}
  \mat{P}\vec{b} &= \mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vec{c}\\
                 &= \mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\vec{c}
                  = \mat{A}\vec{c} = \vec{b}.
\end{align*}
Projection leaves every vector of the space it projects onto fixed.

\textbf{When $\vec{b}$ is orthogonal to the space.} If $\vec{b}\perp\col(\mat{A})$ then
$\mat{A}\T\vec{b}=\vec{0}$ (every column dots to zero with $\vec{b}$), so $\hat{\vec{x}}=\vec{0}$
and $\vec{p}=\vec{0}$. The shadow collapses to the origin, and all of $\vec{b}$ is error.

\begin{aestheticbox}
Compare how $\mat{P}$ acts on these two families of vectors.
Vectors \emph{in} the subspace $S$ are untouched: $\mat{P}\vec{v} = 1\cdot\vec{v}$ (they are kept).
Vectors \emph{perpendicular} to the subspace are mapped to zero: $\mat{P}\vec{v} = 0\cdot\vec{v}$.

Directions that a matrix only stretches, without turning them, are a central theme of linear
algebra. In Chapter~23 we call such directions \emph{eigenvectors} and their stretch factors
\emph{eigenvalues}. A projection matrix has only two possible stretch factors: $1$ (for directions
inside $S$) and $0$ (for directions perpendicular to $S$). The condition $\mat{P}^2=\mat{P}$ forces
this: if stretching a vector twice has the same effect as stretching it once ($\lambda^2=\lambda$),
the factor $\lambda$ can only be $1$ or $0$ (Exercise~\ref{ch18:ex-eigen}). The number of
independent directions with factor $1$ is the dimension of $S$, which also equals the rank of
$\mat{P}$ and the sum of its diagonal entries (the trace).
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Where this is heading: the inconsistent system}

We opened with the promise that projection rescues the unsolvable equation $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$.
When $\vec{b}$ is not in the column space, no $\vec{x}$ makes
$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$. The sensible move is to solve instead the \emph{nearest} solvable problem:
replace $\vec{b}$ by its projection $\vec{p}$ onto $\col(\mat{A})$, and solve $\mat{A}\hat{\vec{x}}
=\vec{p}$. By construction $\vec{p}$ \emph{is} in the column space, so this has a solution, and it
is the $\hat{\vec{x}}$ from the normal equations. This $\hat{\vec{x}}$ is called the
\emph{least-squares solution}: it makes the length of the error, and so the sum of the squared
errors in the entries, as small as possible.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The bridge to least squares.} To ``solve'' an inconsistent $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$, project
$\vec{b}$ onto $\col(\mat{A})$ and accept the projection $\vec{p}$ as the best we can hit. The
best-fit coefficients are
\[
  \hat{\vec{x}} = (\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T\vec{b},
\]
and they minimize the squared error $\norm{\mat{A}\vec{x}-\vec{b}}^2$ -- because, by
Theorem~\ref{ch18:closest-thm}, the projection is the closest point. The next chapter puts this
one idea to work on curve fitting and regression.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Least squares wherever a model meets noisy data.} Suppose a price is believed to depend
linearly on some measured inputs (floor area and number of bedrooms, say). Each house gives one row
of $\mat{A}$ and one entry of $\vec{b}$. With many more houses than inputs, no exact solution
exists, and the regression coefficients that statistics packages report are the projection
coefficients $\hat{\vec{x}}$ of this chapter. Chapter~19 builds this up from scratch.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{Projection in function spaces: Fourier series.} The same perpendicular drop works when the
``vectors'' are functions (Chapter~1 showed that functions form a vector space). For functions on
$[0,2\pi]$ the role of the dot product is played by
\[
  \inner{f}{g}=\int_0^{2\pi} f(t)\,g(t)\,dt:
\]
multiply the two functions and add up the result over the interval, just as the dot product
multiplies entries and adds. The functions $1,\cos t,\sin t,\cos 2t,\sin 2t,\dots$ are mutually
orthogonal under this inner product, like an orthogonal basis of $\R^n$ but with infinitely many
members. A Fourier series approximation of a signal $f$ is the \emph{projection} of $f$ onto the
subspace spanned by the first few of these waves. Write $\phi_k$ for the $k$-th wave in the list.
Because the waves are orthogonal, each coefficient is a separate projection onto a line,
\[
  \hat{x}_k = \frac{\inner{f}{\phi_k}}{\inner{\phi_k}{\phi_k}},
\]
which is the line-projection formula $\hat{x}=\vec{a}\T\vec{b}/\vec{a}\T\vec{a}$ with integrals in
place of dot products. ``Find the closest combination of sines and cosines'' is least squares in a
space of functions, and the leftover ($f$ minus its truncated series) is orthogonal to every wave
you kept. Figure~\ref{ch18:fourier-fig} shows the first two steps for a square wave. This is where
linear algebra meets Fourier analysis and signal processing.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{strangbox}
Strang builds projections in the same order -- line first, then subspace, then the matrix
$\mat{P}=\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$ -- and he hammers the same two properties,
$\mat{P}\T=\mat{P}$ and $\mat{P}^2=\mat{P}$. If you are reading this \emph{before} Strang, the move
to internalize is ``error orthogonal to the space $\Rightarrow$ closest point $\Rightarrow$ normal
equations.'' Everything in his projection and least-squares lectures hangs on that chain. If you are
reading this \emph{after} Strang, look ahead: $\mat{P}$ is your first nontrivial example of a matrix
whose action is completely described by its eigenvalues ($0$ and $1$) and the directions that go
with them ($S^\perp$ and $S$). When you reach the spectral theorem (every symmetric matrix can be
built from perpendicular eigenvectors) and the singular value decomposition (SVD), you will
recognize projection as the simplest symmetric matrix, and you will write any symmetric matrix as a
weighted sum of projections onto its eigenvectors.
\end{strangbox}

\section{What to carry forward}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Summary of core concepts.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item To find the closest point of a subspace $S$ to $\vec{b}$, drop a perpendicular: demand
        that the error $\vec{e}=\vec{b}-\vec{p}$ be orthogonal to $S$.
  \item \textbf{Onto a line} $\spn(\vec{a})$:
        $\;\vec{p}=\dfrac{\vec{a}\T\vec{b}}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}\,\vec{a}$,
        \; $\mat{P}=\dfrac{\vec{a}\,\vec{a}\T}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}$.
  \item \textbf{Onto a subspace} $\col(\mat{A})$: solve the normal equations
        $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\,\hat{\vec{x}}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$, then
        $\mat{P}=\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$.
  \item Every orthogonal projection matrix is \textbf{symmetric} ($\mat{P}\T=\mat{P}$) and \textbf{idempotent}
        ($\mat{P}^2=\mat{P}$). It acts as the identity on $S$ ($\mat{P}\vec{v}=\vec{v}$), collapses
        $S^\perp$ to the origin ($\mat{P}\vec{v}=\vec{0}$), and has $\rank(\mat{P}) = \dim S$.
  \item The closest-point guarantee (Pythagoras) is what turns projection into \textbf{least
        squares}, the engine of regression and data fitting.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Projection matrices replace impossible equations with best approximations. Whenever
$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ has no solution because $\vec{b} \notin \col(\mat{A})$, the projection
matrix $\mat{P} = \mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$ splits $\vec{b}$ into its reachable
shadow $\vec{p} \in \col(\mat{A})$ and its orthogonal residual $\vec{e} \in \nullsp(\mat{A}\T)$.
Chapter~19 uses this split to fit models to data. Chapter~20 computes the same projections from an
orthonormal basis (the QR factorization) without ever forming $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$, which is how
computers do it; Chapter~19 explains why forming $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ costs accuracy.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-mental-checks}
\textbf{(Mental checks: three instant projections.)} Answer each of these without computing any matrix inverses:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item What is the $3\times3$ matrix $\mat{P}$ that projects every vector in $\R^3$ onto the $xy$-plane? (Think: which coordinates are kept, and which are dropped?)
  \item If $\vec{b}$ lies on the line spanned by $\vec{a}$, what is its projection $\mat{P}\vec{b}$? If $\vec{b}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{a}$, what is $\mat{P}\vec{b}$?
  \item If an orthogonal projection matrix $\mat{P}$ satisfies $\mat{P}^2=\mat{P}$ and $\mat{P}\T=\mat{P}$, what is $(\mat{I}-\mat{P})^2$? What is $\mat{P}(\mat{I}-\mat{P})$?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-line-basic}
Project $\vec{b}=\colvec{3\\4}$ onto the line through $\vec{a}=\colvec{1\\1}$. Find $\hat{x}$,
$\vec{p}$, and $\vec{e}$, and verify that $\vec{a}\T\vec{e}=0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-Pmatrix}
For $\vec{a}=\colvec{3\\4}$, build the line-projection matrix
$\mat{P}=\dfrac{\vec{a}\,\vec{a}\T}{\vec{a}\T\vec{a}}$ explicitly as a $2\times2$ matrix. Confirm by
direct multiplication that $\mat{P}^2=\mat{P}$ and that $\mat{P}\T=\mat{P}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-already-in}
Let $\mat{P}$ be the matrix from Exercise~\ref{ch18:ex-Pmatrix}. Compute $\mat{P}\colvec{3\\4}$ and
explain geometrically why the answer is what it is. Then compute $\mat{P}\colvec{4\\-3}$ and explain
that answer too. (Hint: how is $\colvec{4\\-3}$ related to $\colvec{3\\4}$?)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-plane}
Project $\vec{b}=\colvec{0\\0\\3}$ onto the plane spanned by $\vec{a}_1=\colvec{1\\0\\1}$ and
$\vec{a}_2=\colvec{0\\1\\1}$. Form $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ and $\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$, solve for
$\hat{\vec{x}}$, find $\vec{p}$ and $\vec{e}$, and check that $\vec{e}$ is orthogonal to both basis
vectors. (You should find $\hat{\vec{x}}=\colvec{1\\1}$, $\vec{p}=\colvec{1\\1\\2}$, and
$\vec{e}=\colvec{-1\\-1\\1}$; note that $\vec{b}$ does \emph{not} lie in the plane, so the projection
moves it.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-trace}
Recall that the \emph{trace} of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries, $\tr(\mat{M}) = \sum_i M_{ii}$.
You may use the fact that the trace is \emph{cyclic}: $\tr(\mat{X}\mat{Y})=\tr(\mat{Y}\mat{X})$ whenever both products are defined.
Show that for the plane in Exercise~\ref{ch18:ex-plane} the trace of the projection matrix
$\mat{P}=\mat{A}(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$ equals $2$, without computing all nine entries.
(Hint: let $\mat{X}=\mat{A}$ and $\mat{Y}=(\mat{A}\T\mat{A})\inv\mat{A}\T$; apply the cyclic property to push
$\mat{A}$ to the right, and watch the expression collapse to the trace of a $2\times 2$ identity matrix.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-complement}
Let $\mat{P}$ project onto a subspace $S$. Prove that $\mat{Q}=\mat{I}-\mat{P}$ is also a projection
matrix (symmetric and idempotent), and argue geometrically that $\mat{Q}$ projects onto the
orthogonal complement $S^\perp$. What is $\mat{P}\mat{Q}$, and why does the answer make geometric
sense?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-closest-num}
For the line through $\vec{a}=\colvec{2\\1}$ and the point $\vec{b}=\colvec{1\\4}$ (from
Example~\ref{ch18:line-diagonal}), pick any \emph{other} point on the line, say $\vec{y}=2\vec{a}$,
and verify numerically that $\norm{\vec{b}-\vec{y}} > \norm{\vec{b}-\vec{p}}$. Then confirm the
Pythagorean identity $\norm{\vec{b}-\vec{y}}^2 = \norm{\vec{e}}^2 + \norm{\vec{p}-\vec{y}}^2$ with
your numbers.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-eigen}
\textbf{(Preview of eigenvalues.)} A scalar $\lambda$ is called an \emph{eigenvalue} of a square
matrix $\mat{P}$ if there is a nonzero vector $\vec{v}$ that $\mat{P}$ merely scales without rotating:
$\mat{P}\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$. Using only the identity $\mat{P}^2=\mat{P}$, prove that every
such scale factor must satisfy $\lambda^2=\lambda$, hence $\lambda\in\set{0,1}$. (Hint: apply $\mat{P}$
to both sides of $\mat{P}\vec{v}=\lambda\vec{v}$.) Which vectors correspond to $\lambda=1$, and which
to $\lambda=0$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-oblique}
\textbf{(Challenge: why symmetry matters.)} Consider $\mat{P}=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$.
Verify that $\mat{P}^2=\mat{P}$, so $\mat{P}$ is idempotent, but $\mat{P}\T\neq\mat{P}$. Show that
$\mat{P}$ maps every vector onto the $x$-axis, yet does \emph{not} send $\vec{b}=\colvec{0\\1}$ to
its \emph{closest} point on the $x$-axis (which is the origin). Conclude that idempotence alone
gives a projection, but only \emph{symmetry} makes it the orthogonal, closest-point projection.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-orthonormal}
\textbf{(Challenge.)} Suppose the columns of $\mat{A}$ are pairwise \emph{orthonormal}:
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}=\mat{I}$. Show that the projection matrix simplifies to
$\mat{P}=\mat{A}\mat{A}\T$, and that $\hat{\vec{x}}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$ -- each coefficient is a
single dot product $\vec{a}_i\T\vec{b}$, with no matrix inverse anywhere. Explain why this is the
computational reason Chapter~20 builds orthonormal bases via
Gram--Schmidt.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-normal-hand}
\textbf{(The normal equations by hand.)} Project $\vec{b}=\colvec{2\\1\\3}$ onto the plane spanned
by $\vec{a}_1=\colvec{1\\0\\1}$ and $\vec{a}_2=\colvec{1\\1\\0}$. Write each entry of
$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ and $\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$ as a dot product before computing it.

\smallskip
\emph{Answer}: The column dot products are $\vec{a}_1\T\vec{a}_1=2$, $\vec{a}_1\T\vec{a}_2=1$,
$\vec{a}_2\T\vec{a}_2=2$, and the reaches of $\vec{b}$ are $\vec{a}_1\T\vec{b}=5$,
$\vec{a}_2\T\vec{b}=3$. So
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\hat{\vec{x}} = \colvec{5\\3},
  \qquad
  \hat{\vec{x}} = \frac13\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1\\ -1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\colvec{5\\3}
  = \colvec{7/3\\1/3}.
\]
Then $\vec{p}=\tfrac73\vec{a}_1+\tfrac13\vec{a}_2=\colvec{8/3\\1/3\\7/3}$ and
$\vec{e}=\vec{b}-\vec{p}=\colvec{-2/3\\2/3\\2/3}$. Check: $\vec{a}_1\T\vec{e}=-\tfrac23+\tfrac23=0$
and $\vec{a}_2\T\vec{e}=-\tfrac23+\tfrac23=0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-lean}
\textbf{(Sliding until there is no lean.)} Let $S$ be the $xy$-plane in $\R^3$, spanned by
$\vec{a}_1=\colvec{1\\0\\0}$ and $\vec{a}_2=\colvec{0\\1\\0}$, and let $\vec{b}=\colvec{1\\2\\3}$.
Start at the point $\vec{q}=\colvec{0\\2\\0}$ of $S$. Compute the lean $\vec{a}_1\T(\vec{b}-\vec{q})$,
then the squared distance from $\vec{b}$ to $\vec{q}+t\vec{a}_1$ as a function of $t$. Which $t$ is
best, and where does it take you?

\smallskip
\emph{Answer}: $\vec{b}-\vec{q}=\colvec{1\\0\\3}$, so the lean toward $\vec{a}_1$ is $1$ and the
lean toward $\vec{a}_2$ is $0$. The squared distance is
$\norm{\colvec{1-t\\0\\3}}^2=(1-t)^2+9$, which decreases as $t$ goes from $0$ toward $1$ and is
smallest at $t=1$. The new point $\colvec{1\\2\\0}$ has error $\colvec{0\\0\\3}$, which has no lean
toward either column. It is the foot of the perpendicular, $\vec{p}$, at distance $3$ instead of
$\sqrt{10}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch18:ex-dependent}
\textbf{(Dependent columns.)} Let $\vec{a}_1=\colvec{1\\1\\1}$, $\vec{a}_2=\colvec{2\\2\\2}$ and
$\vec{b}=\colvec{1\\2\\6}$. Show that $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is singular, find all solutions of the
normal equations, and show they all give the same $\vec{p}$.

\smallskip
\emph{Answer}: $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}3&6\\6&12\end{bmatrix}$ has determinant
$36-36=0$. With $\mat{A}\T\vec{b}=\colvec{9\\18}$, both rows say $\hat{x}_1+2\hat{x}_2=3$, a whole
line of solutions. For any of them,
$\vec{p}=\hat{x}_1\vec{a}_1+\hat{x}_2\vec{a}_2=(\hat{x}_1+2\hat{x}_2)\colvec{1\\1\\1}=\colvec{3\\3\\3}$:
the projection onto the line through $(1,1,1)$, which replaces each entry of $\vec{b}$ by their
average $3$.
\end{exercise}
