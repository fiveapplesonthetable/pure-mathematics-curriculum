\chapter{LU Factorization}

Two chapters ago, you learned to solve $\mat{A}\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ by Gaussian elimination:
sweep down the matrix, knocking out the entries below each pivot, until what remains is
upper triangular and easy to solve from the bottom up (back-substitution). In the last
chapter, you saw that row operations can be packaged as matrix multiplications, and that
solving a system is closely tied to inverses.
Elimination works, but the pencil-and-paper routine has a flaw: every time someone hands you
a new right-hand side $\vec{b}$, you seem forced to start the entire elimination sweep again
from scratch.

This chapter fixes that. \emph{The work of elimination can be stored and reused.} All the row
operations you performed -- every ``subtract $3$ times row $1$ from row $2$'' -- can be
recorded once, packaged into a single matrix, and replayed on any new $\vec{b}$.
That packaging is the LU factorization. (To \emph{factor} a matrix means to write it as a
product of simpler matrices, the way $12 = 3\cdot 4$ factors a number.) We will find that
elimination factors the matrix into two triangular pieces,
\[
  \mat{A} = \mat{L}\,\mat{U},
\]
a \textbf{l}ower triangular matrix $\mat{L}$ (nonzero entries only on and below the main
diagonal) times an \textbf{u}pper triangular matrix $\mat{U}$ (nonzero entries only on and
above the main diagonal). Once you have $\mat{L}$ and $\mat{U}$, solving a system takes two fast triangular solves instead
of a full elimination sweep. This is how scientific and engineering software solves linear
systems.

\section{The picture: triangular machines are easy machines}
\label{ch09:triangular-picture}

Start with what triangular matrices \emph{do} to space.

A matrix is a machine that takes a vector in and pushes a vector out. An \emph{upper
triangular} matrix
\[
  \mat{U} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
\]
has all its action ``above the diagonal.'' Look at what it does to the standard basis. The
first basis vector $\colvec{1\\0}$ goes to $\colvec{2\\0}$, staying flat on the
$x$-axis -- the $x$-axis is left as a line through the origin, just stretched. The second
basis vector $\colvec{0\\1}$ goes to $\colvec{1\\3}$, which leans to the right. Upper
triangular means: \emph{the first axis stays put as a direction; later axes are allowed to
spill into the earlier ones.}

A \emph{lower triangular} matrix
\[
  \mat{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\]
is the mirror image. Here $\colvec{1\\0}$ goes to $\colvec{1\\3}$ (the first axis spills
\emph{upward} into the second) while $\colvec{0\\1}$ stays put as $\colvec{0\\1}$. This
particular $\mat{L}$, with $1$'s on the diagonal, is a \textbf{shear}: it slides each point
of the plane straight up by $3$ times its $x$-coordinate, so the vertical direction stays
fixed and areas do not change. We met shears as one of the basic deformations a matrix can
apply; in this chapter they do most of the work. Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-triangular} shows both
matrices acting on the unit square.

\begin{intuitionbox}
LU factorization says: \emph{any} (nice enough) square matrix's action can be done in two
simple stages. First apply $\mat{U}$ -- stretch the space and let later coordinates lean
into earlier ones. Then apply $\mat{L}$ -- a shear that slides earlier coordinates into
later ones. For such matrices, these two triangular stages done one after the other
reproduce the whole matrix. Elimination is how we \emph{find} the two stages.
\end{intuitionbox}

Why do we care that the pieces are triangular? Because triangular systems are the easiest
systems to solve. If $\mat{U}$ is upper triangular, the bottom equation
involves only the last unknown, so you read it off directly; the second-to-last equation
then involves only the last two, and you already know one of them; and so on \emph{up} the
list. That is \textbf{back substitution}. If $\mat{L}$ is lower triangular, the same trick
runs top-to-bottom: \textbf{forward substitution}. Neither involves any choices or row
operations. LU's strategy is to trade one hard system for two easy ones.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Look at the upper triangular system $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x_1 \\ x_2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}5 \\ 6\end{bmatrix}$. Can you see why it takes zero elimination steps to solve? The bottom equation $3x_2 = 6$ gives $x_2 = 2$. With $x_2$ in hand, the top equation $2x_1 + x_2 = 5$ becomes $2x_1 + 2 = 5$, so $x_1 = 3/2$. Back substitution reads off the answers from bottom to top without ever modifying the matrix.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Elimination, watched closely}
\label{ch09:elim-watched}

We now run one elimination slowly and keep track of the numbers we usually throw away,
because those numbers make up $\mat{L}$.

Take
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The first \textbf{pivot} -- the leading nonzero entry in the active row that we use as a lever
to clear out all entries below it -- is the $2$ in the top-left corner. To clear the column below
it we ask: what multiple of row $1$ must we subtract from row $2$ to kill the $4$? The answer is
$4/2 = 2$. We call this ratio the \textbf{multiplier} and name it $\ell_{21}$ (the multiplier that
acts on row $2$ using row $1$):
\[
  \ell_{21} = \frac{4}{2} = 2, \qquad
  R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2\,R_1.
\]
Here $R_2$ stands for row $2$, and the arrow means ``replace by'': row $2$ is replaced by
row $2$ minus twice row $1$.
Likewise to kill the $8$ in row $3$ we need $\ell_{31} = 8/2 = 4$, and
$R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 4\,R_1$. After these two operations:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{bmatrix}
  \;\longrightarrow\;
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The new second pivot is the $1$ now sitting in position $(2,2)$. To clear the $3$ below it,
\[
  \ell_{32} = \frac{3}{1} = 3, \qquad R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 3\,R_2,
\]
giving
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
  \;\longrightarrow\;
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \mat{U}.
\]
That triangular matrix on the right is $\mat{U}$. Three multipliers did all the work:
\[
  \ell_{21} = 2, \quad \ell_{31} = 4, \quad \ell_{32} = 3.
\]
Now assemble those multipliers into a lower triangular matrix, with
$1$'s on the diagonal and each $\ell_{ij}$ sitting in the exact position $(i,j)$ where it
was used:
\[
  \mat{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \ell_{21} & 1 & 0 \\ \ell_{31} & \ell_{32} & 1 \end{bmatrix}
          = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Claim: $\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{U}$. Check by direct multiplication:
\[
  \mat{L}\mat{U}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Row $1$ of $\mat{L}$ is $(1,0,0)$, so row $1$ of the product is row $1$ of $\mat{U}$:
$(2,1,1)$. Row $2$ of $\mat{L}$ is $(2,1,0)$, so row $2$ of the product is
$2\cdot(2,1,1) + 1\cdot(0,1,1) = (4,3,3)$. Row $3$ of $\mat{L}$ is $(4,3,1)$, so row $3$ is
$4\cdot(2,1,1) + 3\cdot(0,1,1) + 1\cdot(0,0,2) = (8,7,9)$. Stacking,
\[
  \mat{L}\mat{U} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = \mat{A}. \checkmark
\]
It worked for a structural reason. The multipliers you used to \emph{destroy} the
lower part of $\mat{A}$ are exactly the numbers that, placed in $\mat{L}$,
\emph{rebuild} it.

\begin{focusbox}
Hold onto three sentences.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mat{U}$ is what elimination leaves behind: the upper triangular matrix of pivots.
  \item $\mat{L}$ is the bookkeeping: $1$'s on the diagonal, and the multiplier
        $\ell_{ij}=(\text{entry being cleared})/(\text{pivot})$ in position $(i,j)$.
  \item No minus signs in $\mat{L}$. You \emph{subtracted} $\ell_{ij}$ times a row during
        elimination, but $\mat{L}$ stores the plain multiplier with a $+$ sign.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{trapbox}
The single most common LU mistake is putting minus signs into $\mat{L}$. During elimination
you carried out $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 3R_2$, so it feels natural to write $-3$ in $\mat{L}$.
\textbf{Do not.} The matrix that \emph{performs} the subtraction is $\mat{E}$ with a $-3$;
but $\mat{L} = \mat{E}\inv$ \emph{undoes} the subtraction, and undoing a subtraction is an
addition. So $\mat{L}$ holds $+3$. We will see exactly why in the next section. Quick sanity
check: if every multiplier is positive (you only ever subtracted positive multiples), then
$\mat{L}$ should be all positive below the diagonal.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Why it works: elimination is left-multiplication by shears}
\label{ch09:why-it-works}

The claim ``$\mat{L}$ stores the multipliers'' deserves a real derivation. The idea: each
elementary row operation is itself a matrix, and undoing it means reversing the sign of the
multiplier.

Recall that performing a row operation on $\mat{A}$ is the same as \emph{left}-multiplying
$\mat{A}$ by an \textbf{elementary matrix} -- the identity matrix with a single modification
that executes one row operation when it multiplies another matrix from the left. The operation
$R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$ on a $3\times 3$ matrix is multiplication by
\[
  \mat{E}_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
(Read it row by row: new row $2$ $=$ $-2\cdot$ old row $1$ $+1\cdot$ old row $2$, which is
the operation.) Our full elimination was three such steps. Each new matrix multiplies on the
left, so the first step $\mat{E}_{21}$ sits next to $\mat{A}$ and the last step is written
leftmost:
\[
  \mat{E}_{32}\,\mat{E}_{31}\,\mat{E}_{21}\,\mat{A} = \mat{U},
\]
with
\[
  \mat{E}_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\-2&1&0\\0&0&1 \end{bmatrix},\;
  \mat{E}_{31} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\0&1&0\\-4&0&1 \end{bmatrix},\;
  \mat{E}_{32} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&-3&1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-shear-sequence} shows the matrix after each step.

Solve for $\mat{A}$ by moving the $\mat{E}$'s to the other side. Each one is invertible
(elimination is reversible -- you can always add back what you subtracted), so
\[
  \mat{A} = \mat{E}_{21}\inv\,\mat{E}_{31}\inv\,\mat{E}_{32}\inv\,\mat{U}.
\]
Two facts make this simple. \textbf{Fact one:} inverting a single shear flips the sign of its
off-diagonal entry, because to undo ``subtract $2$ times row $1$'' you ``add $2$ times row
$1$'':
\[
  \mat{E}_{21}\inv = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\ +2&1&0\\0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
There is the plus sign the trap warned about. \textbf{Fact two:} multiplying these
particular inverse shears together does not mix anything up -- the multipliers drop
into their slots with no interaction:
\[
  \mat{E}_{21}\inv\,\mat{E}_{31}\inv\,\mat{E}_{32}\inv
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\2&1&0\\0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\0&1&0\\4&0&1 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&3&1 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\2&1&0\\4&3&1 \end{bmatrix} = \mat{L}.
\]
Compare what happens if you multiply the original forward elementary matrices together in the order they were applied:
\[
  \mat{E}_{32}\mat{E}_{31}\mat{E}_{21}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&-3&1 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\0&1&0\\-4&0&1 \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\-2&1&0\\0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\-2&1&0\\+2&-3&1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
An unexpected $+2$ appears in position $(3,1)$: $(-3)(-2) - 4 = +2$. The row operations
interfered with each other. But when you \emph{invert} each matrix and \emph{reverse} the
product order to form $\mat{L} = \mat{E}_{21}\inv\mat{E}_{31}\inv\mat{E}_{32}\inv$, that
interaction disappears. Every multiplier $\ell_{ij}$ lands in its own position $(i,j)$ with a
positive sign. This is why $\mat{L}$, not the product of the $\mat{E}$'s, is the matrix
worth storing.

So $\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{U}$, with $\mat{L}$ exactly the multiplier table. The reason no
cross-terms appear is worth stating once.

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Why the multipliers do not interfere.} When you eliminate in the natural order --
column $1$ first (top to bottom), then column $2$, and so on -- every inverse elementary
matrix $\mat{E}_{ij}\inv$ adds a multiple of an \emph{earlier} row $j$ to a \emph{later} row
$i$ (so $i>j$). Multiplying two such matrices, the entry $\ell_{ij}$ of one never gets
touched by the other, because the second factor only modifies rows using columns that the
first factor has already finished with. Concretely, for unit lower triangular elementary
matrices (ones on the diagonal) that each have a single off-diagonal entry below the
diagonal, the product is the matrix containing \emph{all} those entries at once:
\[
  \prod (\mat{E}_{ij}\inv) = \mat{I} + \sum_{i>j} \ell_{ij}\,\vec{e}_i \vec{e}_j\T = \mat{L}.
\]
On the left, $\prod$ means the product of all the inverse elementary matrices, in the order
above. On the right, the sum runs over every position $(i,j)$ below the diagonal. Here
$\vec{e}_i$ is the $i$-th standard basis vector (a column with $1$ in row $i$ and zeros
elsewhere), and $\vec{e}_j\T$ is $\vec{e}_j$ written as a row (the transpose of
Chapter~2). So the outer product $\vec{e}_i \vec{e}_j\T$ is the
matrix with a single $1$ in row $i$, column $j$ and zeros everywhere else. This only works for
the natural column-by-column order. Eliminate out of order and the
``read off the multipliers'' rule breaks.
\end{formalbox}

\begin{walkbox}
Take a walk and chew on this. You did three subtractions to get to $\mat{U}$. To get back
to $\mat{A}$ you must undo them \emph{in reverse order} -- last subtraction undone first.
Yet $\mat{L}$ lists the multipliers in \emph{forward} order with no scrambling. How can the
order not matter for assembling $\mat{L}$ even though it absolutely matters for the row
operations themselves? (Hint: think about which rows each step reads from versus writes to.
A step writes to a row no later step ever reads.)
\end{walkbox}

\begin{strangbox}
\emph{Before Strang:} if you have not yet taken his course, absorb the slogan
``$\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$, where $\mat{U}$ is elimination's output and $\mat{L}$ is the
multipliers.'' That single sentence will make his Lecture on elimination feel like a story
whose ending you already know.

\emph{After Strang:} Strang loves to write elimination as
$\mat{E}\mat{A}=\mat{U}$ and then flip it to $\mat{A}=\mat{E}\inv\mat{U}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$,
emphasizing that $\mat{L}$ is the \emph{nicer} matrix because no minus signs survive. He
also stresses $\mat{LDU}$, which we reach in
Section~\ref{ch09:ldu}: pull the pivots out of $\mat{U}$ into a diagonal $\mat{D}$ so that
both triangular factors have unit diagonals and the asymmetry between them disappears.
\end{strangbox}

\section{Solving \texorpdfstring{$\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$}{Ax=b}: two slides instead of one sweep}
\label{ch09:two-slides}

We want $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$, and we know $\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$,
so
\[
  \mat{L}\mat{U}\vec{x} = \vec{b}.
\]
Introduce a helper vector $\vec{c} = \mat{U}\vec{x}$. Then the single problem splits into a
chain of two triangular problems:
\[
  \mat{L}\vec{c} = \vec{b} \quad(\text{forward, solve for }\vec{c}),
  \qquad
  \mat{U}\vec{x} = \vec{c} \quad(\text{backward, solve for }\vec{x}).
\]
First work down through $\mat{L}$ to find $\vec{c}$, then up through $\mat{U}$ to find
$\vec{x}$ (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-twoslides}). Here it is with our matrix and the right-hand side
$\vec{b} = \colvec{5\\11\\27}$.

\textbf{Forward substitution, $\mat{L}\vec{c}=\vec{b}$.}
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \\ 27 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Row $1$: $c_1 = 5$. Row $2$: $2c_1 + c_2 = 11$, so $c_2 = 11 - 2(5) = 1$. Row $3$:
$4c_1 + 3c_2 + c_3 = 27$, so $c_3 = 27 - 20 - 3 = 4$. So
$\vec{c} = \colvec{5\\1\\4}$.

\textbf{Back substitution, $\mat{U}\vec{x}=\vec{c}$.}
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Row $3$: $2x_3 = 4$, so $x_3 = 2$. Row $2$: $x_2 + x_3 = 1$, so $x_2 = 1 - 2 = -1$.
Row $1$: $2x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 5$, so $2x_1 = 5 - (-1) - 2 = 4$ and $x_1 = 2$. So
\[
  \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Check against the original, writing $\mat{A}\vec{x}$ as a combination of the columns of
$\mat{A}$:
\[
  \mat{A}\vec{x} = 2\colvec{2\\4\\8} - 1\colvec{1\\3\\7} + 2\colvec{1\\3\\9}
  = \colvec{4-1+2 \\ 8-3+6 \\ 16-7+18} = \colvec{5\\11\\27} = \vec{b}. \checkmark
\]

  \node[box,fill=orange!6,right=22mm of c] (x) {$\vec{x}$};
  \draw[->,thick] (b) -- node[above,font=\small]{$\mat{L}\vec{c}=\vec{b}$} node[below,font=\small\itshape]{forward} (c);
  \draw[->,thick] (c) -- node[above,font=\small]{$\mat{U}\vec{x}=\vec{c}$} node[below,font=\small\itshape]{backward} (x);
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Solving with a factorization takes two steps. Work forward (top to bottom) through
the lower triangular $\mat{L}$ to turn $\vec{b}$ into the intermediate $\vec{c}$, then
backward (bottom to top) through the upper triangular $\mat{U}$ to reach the answer
$\vec{x}$. Neither step involves any elimination.}
\label{ch09:fig-twoslides}
\end{figure}

The benefit is in the cost. Building $\mat{L}$ and $\mat{U}$ requires the full elimination
effort, once. The two triangular substitutions require far fewer operations and can be
repeated for each new right-hand side $\vec{b}$. Section~\ref{ch09:cost} counts the
operations; the rule is \textbf{factor once, substitute many times}.

\begin{labbox}
By hand, factor
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 6 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
\]
into $\mat{L}\mat{U}$. (Multiplier $\ell_{21}=6/3=2$; subtract $2$ times row $1$ from row
$2$.) You should get $\mat{L}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$,
$\mat{U}=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\0&3\end{bmatrix}$. Then solve $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\colvec{4\\11}$ by
forward-then-back substitution and confirm $\vec{x}=\colvec{1\\1}$ (you should get the
intermediate $\vec{c}=\colvec{4\\3}$). Finally multiply
$\mat{L}\mat{U}$ back out and watch $\mat{A}$ reappear.
\end{labbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose you only ever had to solve $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ for a \emph{single} right-hand side $\vec{b}$, and never again. Does LU factorization save any arithmetic over ordinary Gaussian elimination?
\textbf{Answer:} No. For an $n\times n$ matrix, factoring $\mat{A}$ into $\mat{L}\mat{U}$ costs about $\tfrac13 n^3$ arithmetic operations (Section~\ref{ch09:cost} does the count), which is the same as running elimination on the augmented matrix $[\mat{A}\mid\vec{b}]$. The two triangular substitutions cost about $n^2$, the same as updating $\vec{b}$ during elimination and back-substituting. LU does not make a single solve faster; its advantage is that for every \emph{later} right-hand side, the $\tfrac13 n^3$ elimination cost does not need to be repeated.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Many right-hand sides: the real reason to factor}
\label{ch09:many-rhs}

Here is the situation that makes LU so useful. Suppose you must solve
\[
  \mat{A}\vec{x}_1 = \vec{b}_1,\quad \mat{A}\vec{x}_2 = \vec{b}_2,\quad \dots,\quad
  \mat{A}\vec{x}_k = \vec{b}_k,
\]
with the \emph{same} matrix $\mat{A}$ but many different right-hand sides. This is the
common case, not the exception. In circuit simulation $\mat{A}$ describes the network and each
$\vec{b}$ is a different set of driving voltages. Newton's method solves a nonlinear problem
by solving a long series of linear approximations to it; often the same matrix (the
Jacobian, the matrix of partial derivatives) is reused across many of those steps. In
finite-element structural analysis, where a bridge or a wing is modeled as many small
connected pieces, $\mat{A}$ is the \emph{stiffness matrix} (how strongly the pieces resist
being pushed) and each $\vec{b}$ is a different set of loads.

The naive approach re-runs elimination $k$ times. The LU approach factors
$\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ \emph{once} and then does $k$ cheap forward/back pairs. Since
elimination is far more expensive than substitution, the savings grow without bound as $k$
grows.

There is a compact way to handle all the right-hand sides at once. Put them as columns of
a matrix $\mat{B}=[\vec{b}_1\ \vec{b}_2\ \cdots\ \vec{b}_k]$ and solve $\mat{A}\mat{X}=\mat{B}$
column by column, reusing the one factorization; column $j$ of $\mat{X}$ is the solution
$\vec{x}_j$. An important special case is
$\mat{B}=\mat{I}$: solving $\mat{A}\mat{X}=\mat{I}$ gives $\mat{X}=\mat{A}\inv$. So computing
the inverse is LU applied to the $n$ right-hand sides $\vec{e}_1,\dots,\vec{e}_n$, the
columns of the identity.

\begin{trapbox}
This last fact is also a warning. To \emph{solve} $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ you almost never
want to form $\mat{A}\inv$ first and multiply. Computing $\mat{A}\inv$ is like doing $n$ full
solves and then an extra matrix-vector multiply at the end. That is more work, and the
extra arithmetic adds more rounding error. The professional habit is: factor once, then
forward/back-substitute. If someone's code contains \texttt{inv(A)*b}, they are doing it the
slow, less accurate way; the right call is a solver that factors and substitutes (such as
\texttt{A\textbackslash b} in MATLAB or \texttt{numpy.linalg.solve(A, b)} in Python).
``Never invert; always factor.''
\end{trapbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Real-time engineering and the power grid.} An electrical power grid is modeled by a
large sparse system $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$. The matrix $\mat{A}$ records which connection
points (substations, called \emph{buses}) are linked by power lines and how easily current
flows along each line. The vector $\vec{b}$ records the power drawn and generated at each
bus at this instant. The grid's wiring -- the matrix $\mat{A}$ -- changes only when a
line is switched off or a switch flips, which is rare. But the loads $\vec{b}$ change every few
seconds as demand fluctuates. So operators factor $\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ once (for sparse
matrices a specialized sparse LU keeps $\mat{L}$ and $\mat{U}$ almost as sparse as $\mat{A}$)
and then re-solve thousands of times a day with fresh $\vec{b}$'s by cheap substitution.
The same ``factor once, substitute often'' pattern appears in fluid and climate simulation,
optimization software, robot control, and the training of large statistical models. Because
of this reuse, LU is among the most frequently run numerical algorithms.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Row exchanges: when a pivot is missing}
\label{ch09:pivoting}

Everything above assumed elimination glided through without ever hitting a zero where it
needed a pivot. Many matrices do not allow that. Consider
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The would-be first pivot is the top-left $0$. You cannot divide by zero to form a
multiplier, so elimination stalls -- not because the matrix is bad (it is invertible) but
because the rows are in an inconvenient order. The fix is to swap the two rows.
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Now there is a nonzero pivot $2$, and the matrix is already upper triangular
(Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-swap}). A row swap is
itself a matrix -- a \textbf{permutation matrix} $\mat{P}$, which is the identity with its
rows reordered:
\[
  \mat{P} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mat{P}\mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1\\1&0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0&1\\2&3 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Left-multiplying by $\mat{P}$ reorders the rows of whatever it hits. In a permutation matrix
each row and each column has exactly one $1$ and the rest $0$. Its inverse is its
transpose $\mat{P}\T$, the matrix flipped across its main diagonal (Chapter~8): $\mat{P}\inv = \mat{P}\T$. Undoing a shuffle is another shuffle, and flipping
$\mat{P}$ gives exactly the shuffle that sends every row back.

\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,every node/.style={font=\small}]
  \matrix (m) [matrix of math nodes, left delimiter={[}, right delimiter={]},
               row sep=2pt, column sep=10pt,
               nodes={minimum width=6mm}] {
    0 & 1 \\
    2 & 3 \\
  };
  \matrix (n) [matrix of math nodes, left delimiter={[}, right delimiter={]},
               row sep=2pt, column sep=10pt, xshift=5.6cm,

  % arrows showing the swap of rows
  \draw[->,vecred,thick] ($(m.east)+(0.05,0.22)$) to[bend left=28] node[above,font=\footnotesize,vecred] {top row $\downarrow$} ($(n.west)+(-0.05,-0.22)$);
  \draw[->,vecblue,thick] ($(m.east)+(0.05,-0.22)$) to[bend right=28] node[below,font=\footnotesize,vecblue] {bottom row $\uparrow$} ($(n.west)+(-0.05,0.22)$);
  \node[font=\footnotesize,gray] at (n.south)[below=4mm] {$R_1\leftrightarrow R_2$: pivot $2\neq0$ now in place};
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{A row exchange is all a permutation matrix $\mat{P}$ does. The
zero in the top-left pivot slot is swapped out for a nonzero entry by interchanging the two

exactly this. After the swap the matrix is already upper triangular, so elimination is done.}
\label{ch09:fig-swap}
\end{figure}

The general statement of the factorization, valid for \emph{every} invertible matrix,
builds the permutation in. Its name, \emph{partial pivoting}, refers to choosing row swaps as
elimination goes (Section~\ref{ch09:pivot-stability} explains the rule software uses).

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{LU with partial pivoting.} For any invertible square matrix $\mat{A}$ there is a
permutation matrix $\mat{P}$, a unit lower triangular $\mat{L}$ (lower triangular with ones on the diagonal), and
an upper triangular $\mat{U}$ such that
\[
  \mat{P}\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{U}.
\]
Read it as: \emph{if you reorder the rows of $\mat{A}$ in the right way up front, the
reordered matrix factors cleanly into $\mat{L}\mat{U}$.} The permutation $\mat{P}$ records
all the swaps elimination chose to make.
\end{formalbox}

To solve $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ from $\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$, multiply the system
by $\mat{P}$ first: $\mat{P}\mat{A}\vec{x}=\mat{P}\vec{b}$, that is,
$\mat{L}\mat{U}\vec{x}=\mat{P}\vec{b}$. So you permute the right-hand side the same way, then
do the usual forward/back substitution. Permuting $\vec{b}$ costs no arithmetic: it is
reading its entries in a different order.

\subsection{Pivoting for Numerical Stability}
\label{ch09:pivot-stability}

Real software \emph{always} pivots, even when no pivot is exactly
zero, because a \emph{tiny} pivot is nearly as bad as a zero pivot. Suppose
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.0001 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Use the tiny $0.0001$ as a pivot: the multiplier is $\ell_{21}=1/0.0001 = 10000$, and
clearing row $2$ gives $u_{22} = 1 - 10000\cdot 1 = -9999$ (we write $u_{ij}$ for the entry of
$\mat{U}$ in row $i$, column $j$). That huge multiplier amplifies
any rounding error in the data by a factor of $10^4$. The fix is \textbf{partial pivoting}:
before using a column, swap the row with the \emph{largest} available entry (in absolute
value, among the rows not yet used as pivot rows) into the pivot
position, so every multiplier satisfies $|\ell_{ij}|\le 1$. Swapping rows here puts the $1$
on top, the multiplier becomes $0.0001$, and the computation stays accurate. So
$\mat{P}$ does two jobs: it rescues you from exact zeros, and it keeps rounding errors from
growing (it keeps the computation \emph{numerically stable}). We return to this in the
numerical chapter; for now, know that $\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ is what your computer
does.

\begin{example}[A full $\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$]
\label{ch09:ex-palu}
Factor $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ with partial pivoting. Column
$1$ has entries $1$ and $3$; the larger magnitude is $3$, in row $2$, so swap first:
\[
  \mat{P} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1\\1&0 \end{bmatrix},\quad
  \mat{P}\mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Now eliminate: multiplier $\ell_{21}=1/3$, and
$R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - \tfrac13 R_1$ gives $u_{22} = 2 - \tfrac13\cdot 4 = \tfrac23$. Hence
\[
  \mat{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \tfrac13 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  \mat{U} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & \tfrac23 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Verify: $\mat{L}\mat{U} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 1 & \tfrac43 + \tfrac23 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \mat{P}\mat{A}$. \checkmark
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{The sign of a permutation, and the determinant.} A permutation matrix is the matrix
form of a permutation of $\{1,\dots,n\}$, that is, a reordering of the numbers $1$ to $n$.
Group theory calls the collection of all $n! = 1\cdot 2\cdots n$ such reorderings the
\emph{symmetric group} $S_n$. Every permutation is a product
of swaps, and although the number of swaps is not unique, its \emph{parity} (even or odd)
is -- a fixed fact about the permutation. That parity is the \emph{sign}: $\det\mat{P}=+1$
for an even number of swaps and $-1$ for an odd number. Recall that the determinant $\det$
measures the factor by which a matrix scales areas (or volumes); its chapter later in the
book proves the two rules used here: the determinant of a product is the product of the
determinants, and the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal
entries. With those rules, $\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ gives
\[
  (\det\mat{P})\,(\det\mat{A}) = (\det\mat{L})\,(\det\mat{U})
  = 1 \cdot (u_{11}u_{22}\cdots u_{nn}),
\]
since $\mat{L}$ has unit diagonal. Because $\det\mat{P} = \pm 1$, this means
$\det\mat{A} = \pm\, u_{11}u_{22}\cdots u_{nn}$: the determinant is $\pm$ the product of
the pivots. This is how numerical libraries compute determinants: factor, multiply the
pivots, fix the sign from the swaps. One $\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ factorization gives
the solver, the row-swap sign, and the determinant at once.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Pulling out the pivots: \texorpdfstring{$\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{D}\mat{U}$}{A=LDU}}
\label{ch09:ldu}

The two factors of $\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ are not treated alike, and evening them out
shows more structure. In our example
\[
  \mat{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\2&1&0\\4&3&1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  \mat{U} = \begin{bmatrix} 2&1&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&2 \end{bmatrix},
\]
the $\mat{L}$ has $1$'s on its diagonal but $\mat{U}$ carries the pivots $2,1,2$ on its
diagonal. Factor those pivots out of $\mat{U}$ into a diagonal matrix $\mat{D}$, leaving a
$\mat{U}$ with unit diagonal too:
\[
  \mat{U} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mat{D}}
            \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mat{U}'},
\]
where each row of $\mat{U}$ was divided by its pivot. (Recall that $\diag(2,1,2)$ is the
diagonal matrix with $2,1,2$ on its diagonal and zeros elsewhere.) So
\[
  \mat{A} = \mat{L}\,\mat{D}\,\mat{U}', \qquad
  \mat{D}=\diag(2,1,2),
\]
with $\mat{L}$ and $\mat{U}'$ both unit triangular and all the scaling living in $\mat{D}$.
This $\mat{L}\mat{D}\mat{U}$ form treats the two triangular factors alike, and it
pays off for \textbf{symmetric} matrices ($\mat{A} = \mat{A}\T$, matrices that are mirror
images of themselves across the main diagonal): if $\mat{A}=\mat{A}\T$ then
$\mat{U}'=\mat{L}\T$ and the factorization becomes $\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{D}\mat{L}\T$.

Suppose in addition that all pivots in $\mat{D}$ are positive. (For a symmetric matrix this
is what \textbf{positive definite} means in practice; such a matrix stretches space along
perpendicular axes by positive factors, never flattening or flipping any direction. A later
chapter gives the full definition.) Then write $\sqrt{\mat{D}}$ for the diagonal matrix of
the square roots of the pivots, so $\mat{D}=\sqrt{\mat{D}}\sqrt{\mat{D}}$, and absorb one
square root into each triangular factor.
That yields $\mat{A}=\mat{L}_C\mat{L}_C\T$ with lower triangular
$\mat{L}_C = \mat{L}\sqrt{\mat{D}}$ (often written $\mat{A}=\mat{R}\T\mat{R}$ with
$\mat{R}=\mat{L}_C\T$): the \textbf{Cholesky factorization}. It is the version of LU for
symmetric positive definite matrices and needs about half the work. It is widely used in
least squares (fitting the best line or curve to data) and in statistics with the normal
(bell-curve) distribution. We will meet it again with positive definite matrices.

\begin{aestheticbox}
Writing $\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ turns a list of row-reduction steps into an equation
between matrices, and any symmetry in $\mat{A}$ shows up immediately in the factors:
pulling out the pivots gives $\mat{L}\mat{D}\mat{U}'$; symmetry ($\mat{A}=\mat{A}\T$)
makes the upper factor mirror the lower ($\mat{L}\mat{D}\mat{L}\T$); and positive pivots
let us split $\mat{D}$ in half to get the Cholesky form $\mat{L}_C\mat{L}_C\T$.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{The cost of elimination, counted exactly}
\label{ch09:cost}

We have been saying ``elimination is expensive, substitution is cheap.'' Here are the
numbers behind that claim.

Count \textbf{flops} (floating-point operations: one arithmetic operation on stored
decimal numbers, such as one multiplication or one subtraction). We count each
multiply-and-subtract pair as one step. To clear column $1$ of an $n\times n$
matrix you process rows $2$ through $n$ -- that is $n-1$ rows -- and for each you compute one
multiplier and update about $n$ entries, so roughly $(n-1)\cdot n$ operations. Clearing
column $2$ works on the remaining $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ block, costing about $(n-2)(n-1)$. In
general, eliminating column $k$ costs about $(n-k)(n-k+1)$.
Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-active-block} shows the shrinking block. Adding up over all columns
(and writing $j = n-k$),
\[
  \text{elimination cost} \;\approx\; \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} (n-k)^2 \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} j^2
  \;\approx\; \frac{n^3}{3}.
\]
(The last step uses $1^2 + 2^2 + \cdots + m^2 = m(m+1)(2m+1)/6$, which is about
$m^3/3$ for large $m$.) The leading term is $\tfrac13 n^3$, and we write ``factoring costs $\sim\tfrac13 n^3$'' (read
$\sim$ as ``about, for large $n$''). The cost is
\emph{cubic}, growing like the cube of $n$. Double the size of the matrix and elimination
costs eight times as much.

Now count a triangular solve. Back substitution on $\mat{U}$: the last unknown needs only a
division, the next costs $1$ multiply, the next $2$, and so on up to about $n$, giving
\[
  \sum_{k=1}^{n} k \approx \frac{n^2}{2}.
\]
Forward substitution on $\mat{L}$ costs the same $\tfrac12 n^2$. So one full solve from a
finished factorization is about $n^2$ operations: the cost is \emph{quadratic}, growing like
the square of $n$. Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-cost} compares the two curves.

Compare the two costs directly. The expensive factorization costs about
$\tfrac13 n^3$; each additional solve costs about $n^2$. Their ratio is
\[
  \frac{\tfrac13 n^3}{n^2} = \frac{n}{3}.
\]
So one factorization ``is worth'' roughly $n/3$ solves. For a modest $n=300$, the factor
step costs as much as a hundred substitution steps. If you have a hundred right-hand sides
and you re-eliminate each time, you do a hundred cubic operations; if you factor once and
substitute, you do one cubic plus a hundred quadratics -- about the cost of a
\emph{single} naive solve. That is roughly the difference between a job finishing in a
second and finishing in a minute. Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-cost-vs-k} plots both strategies as the
number of right-hand sides grows.

Take $n=1000$ and $k=500$ right-hand sides. Re-eliminating each time costs about

faster here -- and the advantage only widens as $k$ grows, because every extra right-hand
side is nearly free.
\end{example}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Factoring is cubic, solving is quadratic.} The cubic cost is paid once, after
which each additional right-hand side requires only a quadratic triangular solve. Whenever
the same matrix appears with many right-hand sides, factoring saves a large amount of work.
\end{focusbox}

\section{When LU fails, and when it does not}
\label{ch09:existence}

\textbf{Pure $\mat{L}\mat{U}$ (no permutation) exists} exactly when elimination never needs a
swap, that is, when every pivot turns out nonzero without reordering. A sufficient
condition: if every \emph{leading principal submatrix} of $\mat{A}$ (the top-left
$1\times 1$, $2\times 2$, \dots, $n\times n$ blocks, the last being $\mat{A}$ itself) is invertible, then $\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$
exists and is unique. Uniqueness is easy to believe: if $\mat{L}_1\mat{U}_1=\mat{L}_2\mat{U}_2$
then $\mat{L}_2\inv\mat{L}_1=\mat{U}_2\mat{U}_1\inv$; the left side is unit lower triangular,
the right side is upper triangular, and the only matrix that is both is the identity -- so
$\mat{L}_1=\mat{L}_2$ and $\mat{U}_1=\mat{U}_2$.

\textbf{$\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ always exists} for an invertible $\mat{A}$. There is
always \emph{some} reordering of the rows that makes pivots appear, because invertibility
guarantees a nonzero entry to promote in each column as you go.

\textbf{Singular matrices} (square matrices that are not invertible) can still be reduced to an upper triangular $\mat{U}$, but $\mat{U}$
will have a zero pivot somewhere, signaling that $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ either has no
solution or infinitely many. The chapters on rank and the four fundamental subspaces deal
with that case; LU's two-step solve assumes all the pivots are nonzero.

\begin{remark}
The matrix $\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix}$ from
Section~\ref{ch09:pivoting} is invertible yet has \emph{no} plain $\mat{L}\mat{U}$
factorization -- its leading $1\times 1$ block is the zero matrix $[0]$, which is not
invertible, so the leading-submatrix test fails at the very first step. This is precisely
the case the permutation rescues. ``No $\mat{L}\mat{U}$'' is a statement about the order of
the rows, not about the matrix itself.
\end{remark}

\begin{strangbox}
\emph{After Strang:} when Strang asks ``which matrices have an $\mat{L}\mat{U}$ without row
exchanges?'', the answer is the leading-submatrix condition above. He frames the general
case as $\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ and emphasizes that this is the version your software
performs. He also connects $\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$ to the \emph{four subspaces} later: the
pivots in $\mat{U}$ reveal the rank, and the columns of $\mat{L}$ relate to the column space.
The computations in those later chapters run on this factorization.
\end{strangbox}

\section{What to carry forward}
\label{ch09:summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Elimination is factorization.} Running Gaussian elimination on $\mat{A}$
        produces $\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$: $\mat{U}$ is the upper triangular result,
        and $\mat{L}$ collects the multipliers (with a plus sign) below a unit diagonal.
  \item \textbf{Solve in two steps.} $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ becomes $\mat{L}\vec{c}=\vec{b}$
        (forward substitution) then $\mat{U}\vec{x}=\vec{c}$ (back substitution).
  \item \textbf{Factor once, reuse many times.} New right-hand sides need only the two cheap
        substitutions. This reuse is why LU is used so widely in practice.
  \item \textbf{Row exchanges give $\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$.} Pivoting handles missing
        pivots and prevents numerical errors from blowing up by keeping multipliers
        $\le 1$ in magnitude.
  \item \textbf{Cost.} Factoring is $\sim\tfrac13 n^3$ (cubic); each solve is $\sim n^2$
        (quadratic). One factorization is worth about $n/3$ solves.
  \item \textbf{Never invert; always factor.} To solve a system, do not compute $\mat{A}\inv$;
        factor and substitute. It is faster and more accurate.
\end{itemize}

This is the algorithm that numerical libraries run when you ask them to solve a linear
system.

Two tools appeared in this chapter without a full treatment. Whenever a zero blocked a pivot,
we swapped rows using a permutation matrix $\mat{P}$, arriving at
$\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$. And when $\mat{A}$ was symmetric, we factored it into
$\mat{L}\mat{D}\mat{L}\T$, using the transpose. What \emph{is} a transpose geometrically? And
how do permutation matrices behave under multiplication and inversion? The next chapter,
\emph{Transposes and Permutations}, answers these questions: flipping matrices across their
main diagonals, reordering coordinates, and setting out the rules for transposes that the
later chapters on orthogonality and symmetric matrices depend on.

\begin{historybox}
The modern formulation of elimination as matrix factorization ($\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{U}$) was established
by Alan Turing in his 1948 paper, \emph{Rounding-Off Errors in Matrix Processes}. Turing, working on the design
of the ACE (Automatic Computing Engine) at the British National Physical Laboratory, wanted to know whether
early digital computers could solve systems of one hundred linear equations without rounding error destroying
the results. In 1943 the statistician Harold Hotelling had published an analysis suggesting that rounding
errors might grow exponentially with the number of equations, and many concluded that solving large systems
on digital computers would produce nonsense. (John von Neumann and Herman Goldstine's 1947 analysis was more
hopeful, but only for symmetric positive definite matrices.)

Turing answered that pessimism directly. Working with slow electromechanical calculators and
early computer designs whose memory held only hundreds of numbers, Turing introduced the matrix condition number
(a single number measuring how sensitive a system's output is to tiny perturbations in its inputs) and proved
that Gaussian elimination is mathematically equivalent to factoring a matrix into triangular pieces:
$\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{U}$. He noted that with partial pivoting the multipliers stay bounded by $1$, and argued
that in practice the errors are far smaller than the pessimistic estimates. In 1961, James Wilkinson made this
rigorous with \emph{backward error bounds}: proofs that the computer's rounded answer is the exact solution
of a slightly changed problem.

An important precursor came from military geodesy (the science of measuring Earth's curvature to triangulate
positions and draft accurate maps). In 1910, the French artillery officer and surveyor André-Louis Cholesky
developed a method for solving the symmetric, positive-definite normal equations ($\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vec{x}=\mat{A}\T\vec{b}$,
which arise when finding best-fit lines through experimental data). Cholesky was killed in action in August 1918
during the closing months of World War~I, but his fellow officer Commandant Benoit published Cholesky's manuscript
posthumously in 1924. The Cholesky factorization ($\mat{A} = \mat{L}\mat{L}\T$) remains the gold standard for
symmetric positive-definite systems, requiring only half the arithmetic and half the memory of standard $\mat{L}\mat{U}$.

In early computers, memory was so scarce that storing a second $n\times n$ matrix during elimination was
impossible. Programmers used a simple fact: the diagonal of $\mat{L}$ is entirely $1$'s, so
it never needs to be stored. As elimination creates each zero below the pivot in column $j$, the multiplier
$\ell_{ij}$ can be written directly into the memory location where that zero was just created. At the end of the
sweep, the original array $\mat{A}$ holds both $\mat{L}$ (below the diagonal) and $\mat{U}$ (on and above the diagonal)
with no extra memory.

A puzzle remains open: Wilkinson showed that in the worst case, partial pivoting allows the entries (and
with them the errors) to grow by a factor of $2^{n-1}$, which would make a $100\times 100$ solve
completely inaccurate. Yet in over seventy years of practical computing, matrices from real problems that
trigger this growth have almost never appeared. Why remains a research topic; part of the explanation comes
from \emph{smoothed analysis}, an approach of Daniel Spielman and Shang-Hua Teng that asks how an algorithm
behaves on worst-case inputs after small random changes. A second open problem concerns speed: the fastest
known methods solve a general $n\times n$ system in about $n^{2.37}$ steps (the same as the fastest known
matrix multiplication), and whether about $n^2$ steps is possible is one of the major open questions in
theoretical computer science.

Even with pivoting, the triangular factors $\mat{L}$ and $\mat{U}$ can distort lengths and angles. Factorizations
that preserve them, and the tools for rectangular data, least squares, and symmetric systems, need transposes:
Chapter~10 (Definition~\ref{ch10:def-transpose}) treats transposes and permutations systematically, opening the
path to symmetric matrices, orthogonality, and the geometry of subspaces in Part~III.
\end{historybox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch09:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: already triangular]
\label{ch09:ex-mental-tri}
If a matrix $\mat{A}$ is already upper triangular, what are its factors $\mat{L}$ and $\mat{U}$ in the factorization $\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$? What if $\mat{A}$ is a diagonal matrix?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: multipliers at a glance]
\label{ch09:ex-mental-multipliers}
Without picking up a pen, state the multiplier $\ell_{21}$ needed to eliminate the bottom-left entry of $\begin{bmatrix}5 & 2 \\ 15 & 8\end{bmatrix}$, and write down the matrix $\mat{L}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Warmup factorization]
\label{ch09:ex-warmup}
Factor $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$ into $\mat{L}\mat{U}$ by hand.
State the multiplier you used, and verify your answer by multiplying $\mat{L}\mat{U}$ back
out.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Two slides]
\label{ch09:ex-twoslides}
Using your factorization from the previous exercise, solve $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\colvec{5\\21}$ by
forward substitution ($\mat{L}\vec{c}=\vec{b}$) followed by back substitution
($\mat{U}\vec{x}=\vec{c}$). Show $\vec{c}$ and $\vec{x}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A $3\times3$ by hand]
\label{ch09:ex-3x3}
Factor
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & 5 \\ 4 & 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix}
\]
into $\mat{L}\mat{U}$. Record all three multipliers $\ell_{21},\ell_{31},\ell_{32}$, and
confirm $\mat{L}\mat{U}=\mat{A}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[No minus signs]
\label{ch09:ex-signs}
A student factors $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&4\\6&3\end{bmatrix}$ and writes
$\mat{L}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\-3&1\end{bmatrix}$. Without recomputing from scratch, explain
how you know the sign is wrong, give the correct $\mat{L}$, and state the general rule for the
sign of the entries of $\mat{L}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Permutation needed]
\label{ch09:ex-perm}
Show that $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ has no plain $\mat{L}\mat{U}$
factorization. Then find a permutation $\mat{P}$ so that $\mat{P}\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{U}$, and
give $\mat{P}$, $\mat{L}$, and $\mat{U}$ explicitly.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Partial pivoting for stability]
\label{ch09:ex-pivot-stability}
Consider $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 0.001 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. (a) Factor it
\emph{without} pivoting and report the multiplier $\ell_{21}$ and the entry $u_{22}$.
(b) Now factor $\mat{P}\mat{A}$ with the rows swapped (partial pivoting) and report the new
multiplier. (c) In one sentence, say why the second factorization is the one a numerical
library prefers.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Pull out the pivots]
\label{ch09:ex-ldu}
For $\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 20 \end{bmatrix}$, find $\mat{L}\mat{U}$, then pull
the pivots out to write $\mat{A}=\mat{L}\mat{D}\mat{U}'$ with both triangular factors having
unit diagonal. Verify that $\mat{A}$ is symmetric and that, consequently, $\mat{U}'=\mat{L}\T$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Determinant from pivots]
\label{ch09:ex-det}
Using the factorization from Exercise~\ref{ch09:ex-3x3}, compute $\det\mat{A}$ as the product
of the pivots (the diagonal of $\mat{U}$). Then state how the answer would change if one row
exchange had been required.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Counting the cost]
\label{ch09:ex-cost}
You must solve $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ for an $n\times n$ matrix with $n=200$, across
$k=50$ different right-hand sides. (a) Estimate the total operation count if you re-run
elimination ($\sim\tfrac13 n^3$ each) every time. (b) Estimate it if you factor once
($\sim\tfrac13 n^3$) and then do $k$ substitutions ($\sim n^2$ each). (c) What is the
approximate speedup factor?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why not invert? (short proof/argument)]
\label{ch09:ex-noinvert}
Explain, in terms of operation counts, why solving $\mat{A}\vec{x}=\vec{b}$ by first
computing $\mat{A}\inv$ and forming $\mat{A}\inv\vec{b}$ is more expensive than factoring and
substituting. (Hint: how many right-hand sides does computing $\mat{A}\inv$ implicitly solve,
and how does that compare to the one right-hand side you actually care about?)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Uniqueness, a clean proof]
\label{ch09:ex-uniqueness}
Suppose a matrix has two unit-lower/upper factorizations
$\mat{L}_1\mat{U}_1=\mat{L}_2\mat{U}_2$, where $\mat{L}_1,\mat{L}_2$ are unit lower triangular
and $\mat{U}_1,\mat{U}_2$ are upper triangular with nonzero diagonals. Prove $\mat{L}_1=\mat{L}_2$
and $\mat{U}_1=\mat{U}_2$. (Hint: rearrange to $\mat{L}_2\inv\mat{L}_1=\mat{U}_2\mat{U}_1\inv$
and argue about what kind of matrix each side must be.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Block thinking, a challenge]
\label{ch09:ex-block}
Split a square matrix into four smaller matrices (\emph{blocks}) by cutting it with one
horizontal and one vertical line, the top-left block being square:
$\mat{A}=\begin{bmatrix} \mat{A}_{11} & \mat{A}_{12} \\ \mat{A}_{21} & \mat{A}_{22}
\end{bmatrix}$, with $\mat{A}_{11}$ invertible. Blocks multiply like ordinary entries, as
long as you keep the order of each product. Show that
\[
  \mat{A} = \begin{bmatrix} \mat{I} & \mat{0} \\ \mat{A}_{21}\mat{A}_{11}\inv & \mat{I} \end{bmatrix}
            \begin{bmatrix} \mat{A}_{11} & \mat{A}_{12} \\ \mat{0} & \mat{S} \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad \mat{S} = \mat{A}_{22} - \mat{A}_{21}\mat{A}_{11}\inv\mat{A}_{12}.
\]
This is one step of block elimination; $\mat{S}$ is the \emph{Schur complement}. Identify
which factor plays the role of $\mat{L}$ and which plays $\mat{U}$, and explain how this
generalizes the scalar multiplier $\ell_{21}=a_{21}/a_{11}$.
\end{exercise}
