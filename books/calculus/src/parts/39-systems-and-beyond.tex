\chapter{Systems of Differential Equations and Beyond}

In the previous chapter we studied second-order differential equations
like $y'' + py' + qy = 0$: a single mass on a spring, trading position for
velocity, settling or oscillating according to one equation for one variable. Up
to now, every differential equation has described one quantity changing in
time: a population, a temperature, the charge on a capacitor, the angle of a
swinging pendulum. One unknown function, one equation. Most real systems,
however, involve several quantities changing together. Foxes eat rabbits, so
the two populations rise and fall together, and neither population curve makes
sense without the other. Two masses joined
by springs pass energy back and forth. Three chemicals react, each one
consumed to make the next. In every one of these settings we have not one
unknown function but several, and the rate of change of each depends on
\emph{all} of them at once. We need a \emph{system} of differential equations.

This chapter joins two threads. One is the scalar ODE $y' = ky$
(``scalar'' meaning the unknown is a single number), whose solution is
$Ce^{kt}$. The other is linear algebra: vectors, matrices, and above all
\textbf{eigenvalues and eigenvectors}, which you met in the chapter on
gradients and Lagrange multipliers. An eigenvector of a matrix is a nonzero
vector that the matrix only stretches, keeping it on the same line through the
origin; the eigenvalue is the stretch factor. The main result is this: a system
of coupled linear equations, however tangled it looks, can be \emph{uncoupled}
by choosing the right coordinates, and in those coordinates each piece is
$y'=\lambda y$ again. The eigenvectors give the right coordinates, and the
eigenvalues are the growth rates.

The plan: write a system as one equation for a moving point, solve the linear
case with eigenvectors, draw pictures of all solutions at once, decide when a
resting state is one the system returns to after a small push, and then see
what changes when the equations are not linear. The last section points to the
subjects that continue from here.

\section{What a System Is, and Why You Already Have One}
\label{ch39:what}

A \textbf{first-order system} of differential equations is a collection of
equations, one for the derivative of each unknown function, where each
right-hand side may depend on all the unknowns (and possibly on time):
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    x_1'(t) &= f_1(t, x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n),\\
    x_2'(t) &= f_2(t, x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n),\\
            &\;\;\vdots\\
    x_n'(t) &= f_n(t, x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n).
  \end{aligned}
\]
The word \emph{first-order} means only first derivatives appear. The word
\emph{system} means the equations are bound together: in general you cannot
solve one without the others, because each $f_i$ can depend on every $x_j$.
Here $n$ is the number of unknowns, and $t$ is time.

It helps to bundle the unknowns into a single vector-valued
function
\[
  \vec{x}(t) = \big(x_1(t), x_2(t), \dots, x_n(t)\big),
\]
and the right-hand sides into a single vector field
$\vec{F}(t,\vec x) = (f_1, \dots, f_n)$. Then the entire system becomes one
line that looks like the scalar case:
\[
  \boxed{\;\vec{x}'(t) = \vec{F}\big(t, \vec{x}(t)\big).\;}
\]
Vector notation here expresses a geometric point of view. A single number
moving on a line obeyed $x' = f(x)$. A single \emph{point} moving through
$n$-dimensional space obeys $\vec x' = \vec F(\vec x)$. The state of the whole
world is one point; the differential equation tells that point, at every moment,
which direction and how fast to move.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Picture the unknowns as the coordinates of a single dot living in a space called
\textbf{phase space} (the space of all possible states). For the fox--rabbit
problem that space is a plane: the horizontal axis is the rabbit population
$R$, the vertical axis the fox population $F$.
The current state of the ecosystem is \emph{one dot} in that plane. The system of
ODEs is a rule that, at every point, plants a little arrow telling the dot where to
go next. Solving the system means following the arrows,
letting the dot drift, and watching the curve it traces. That curve is the
history of the ecosystem.
\end{intuitionbox}

both views. On the left is the curve the state dot traces in phase space; the
green dot $(R^*, F^*)$ is the balance point where both populations stay

\subsection{A first concrete example}

Here is a small system. Let $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ be two quantities
obeying
\[
  x' = x + 2y, \qquad y' = 3x + 2y.
\]
The equations are \textbf{coupled}: $x'$ depends on $y$, and $y'$ depends on $x$. You cannot
integrate the first equation by itself because the $2y$ on its right is an
unknown function you have not found yet. We will need a way to separate the
variables from each other; Section~\ref{ch39:worked} solves this system.

\begin{happeningbox}
Writing the system down is different from solving it. The
differential equations tell you the instantaneous velocity $(x', y')$ at each
point $(x, y)$, whereas a solution gives an explicit formula $(x(t), y(t))$
telling you where the state is at any time $t$ without having to trace the
step-by-step path in between.
\end{happeningbox}

\section{Reducing Higher-Order Equations to First-Order Systems}
\label{ch39:reduction}

The previous chapter studied second-order equations like $y'' + py' + qy = 0$,
and every higher-order ODE can be rewritten as a first-order system.
This is one of the most useful facts about differential equations.

Take the second-order equation
\[
  y'' = -y,
\]
the equation of simple harmonic motion, whose solutions you know are sines
and cosines. Introduce a \emph{second unknown} equal to the velocity:
\[
  x_1 = y, \qquad x_2 = y'.
\]
Now ask for the derivatives of these two new unknowns. The first is immediate:
$x_1' = y' = x_2$. The second uses the original equation: $x_2' = y'' = -y =
-x_1$. So the single second-order equation becomes the first-order system
\[
  x_1' = x_2, \qquad x_2' = -x_1.
\]
The physics is unchanged; we have only renamed ``position'' and
``velocity'' as two coordinates and written down how each one changes.
Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-shm-phase} shows the result: the solution
$y = \cos t$ becomes a point going round a circle in the
(position, velocity) plane. The same trick works for any order.

\begin{whybox}
Why does this always work? An $n$th-order equation
$y^{(n)} = G(t, y, y', \dots, y^{(n-1)})$ (where $y^{(k)}$ means the $k$th
derivative of $y$) gives the highest derivative in terms of
all the lower ones. So if we name the lower derivatives as new variables
\[
  x_1 = y,\quad x_2 = y',\quad x_3 = y'',\quad \dots,\quad x_n = y^{(n-1)},
\]
then each variable's derivative is the \emph{next} variable in the
list ($x_1' = x_2$, $x_2' = x_3$, and so on) until the last one, whose
derivative is the only place the original equation is used:
$x_n' = G(t, x_1, \dots, x_n)$. The chain $x_i' = x_{i+1}$ is
bookkeeping; the content of the equation sits in the final
line. That is why equations of any order
reduce to first-order systems.
\end{whybox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
This reduction is why we can spend the whole chapter on first-order
systems and lose nothing. A numerical solver written for $\vec x' = \vec F(\vec
x)$ can compute the orbit of a planet (second order), the vibration of a
bridge (fourth order), or the firing of a neuron (several coupled equations)
without modification. For the same reason, almost all general theorems and numerical
algorithms for differential equations are stated once, for first-order systems.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{example}[Damped oscillator as a system]
\label{ch39:ex-damped}
The equation $y'' + 2y' + 5y = 0$ models a mass on a spring with friction. Set
$x_1 = y$, $x_2 = y'$. Then $x_1' = x_2$, and from the equation
$y'' = -2y' - 5y$, so $x_2' = -5x_1 - 2x_2$. In matrix form (a matrix times a
column vector gives the column of row-times-column sums, so the top row here
reads $0\cdot x_1 + 1\cdot x_2$),
\[
  \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}'
  =
  \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -5 & -2 \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The matrix that appears is called the \textbf{companion matrix} of the
equation. Its rows above the bottom one have a single $1$ just to the right of
the main diagonal (encoding $x_1' = x_2$, $x_2' = x_3$, \dots), and its bottom
row holds the coefficients of the original ODE with their signs flipped. We
will solve systems of exactly this shape in a moment.
\end{example}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose you have the third-order equation $y''' - y = 0$.
If you set $x_1 = y$, $x_2 = y'$, and $x_3 = y''$, what does the $3\times 3$
companion matrix look like? The first row gives $x_1' = x_2$, so $(0, 1, 0)$;
the second gives $x_2' = x_3$, so $(0, 0, 1)$; and the bottom row reads the ODE
itself, $x_3' = y''' = y = x_1$, giving $(1, 0, 0)$:
\[
  \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Each row shifts one place to the right until the bottom row loops back.
\end{walkbox}

\section{The Matrix Form \texorpdfstring{$\vec{u}' = A\vec{u}$}{u' = Au}}
\label{ch39:matrixform}

From here on we focus on \textbf{linear} systems with constant coefficients:
each right-hand side is a sum of the unknowns times fixed numbers, with no
products, powers or other functions of the unknowns, and the numbers do not
change with time. We focus on them
because they are the ones we can solve completely and because what we learn
about them describes nonlinear systems near their resting points too (Section
\ref{ch39:nonlinear}). A linear constant-coefficient system in two unknowns
looks like
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    u_1' &= a u_1 + b u_2,\\
    u_2' &= c u_1 + d u_2,
  \end{aligned}
\]
with constants $a,b,c,d$, and fits into the single matrix equation
\[
  \boxed{\;\vec{u}' = A\,\vec{u}, \qquad
  \vec u = \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
  A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}.\;}
\]
Here $\vec u'$ means ``differentiate each component'': $\vec u' = (u_1', u_2')$.
The whole system is now a single object: a matrix $A$ acting on a moving vector.

Compare the scalar case. If $\vec u$ has one component, $A$ is
a number $\lambda$, and the equation is $u' = \lambda u$, whose solution we
have known since the chapter on exponentials:
\[
  u(t) = u(0)\,e^{\lambda t}.
\]
So we try the same shape for the system: we ask whether there are solutions of
the form $\vec u(t) = e^{\lambda t}\vec v$, with $\vec v$ a constant vector and
$\lambda$ a number.

\begin{walkbox}
Suppose that $\vec u(t) = e^{\lambda t}\vec v$ where $\vec v$ is a
constant nonzero vector. Differentiate the left side: since $\vec v$ is constant,
$\vec u'(t) = \lambda e^{\lambda t}\vec v$. Now compute the right side:
$A\vec u(t) = A(e^{\lambda t}\vec v) = e^{\lambda t}(A\vec v)$. For these to be
equal for all $t$, cancel the never-zero scalar $e^{\lambda t}$ and you are left
with
\[
  A\vec v = \lambda\vec v.
\]
\emph{That is the eigenvalue equation.} The requirement that $e^{\lambda t}\vec v$ solve
the differential equation is exactly the condition that $\vec v$ be an eigenvector of
$A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$. The differential equation has turned into a problem of linear algebra.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
This links this book to its companion, \emph{Linear Algebra From
the Ground Up}, and to the eigenvectors you met in the chapter on Lagrange
multipliers. An eigenvector $\vec v$ of $A$ is a nonzero vector
that $A$ only \emph{stretches} (or shrinks, or flips) along its own line: $A\vec v =
\lambda\vec v$, with the stretch factor $\lambda$ the eigenvalue.
Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-eigen-stretch} shows this for the matrix we will solve in
Section~\ref{ch39:worked}. Why should anyone care about
directions a matrix only stretches? The calculus side gives an answer:
those are exactly the directions in which a linear system of
ODEs behaves like a single scalar exponential. The chapters ``Eigenvalues and
Eigenvectors'' and ``Diagonalization'' in the companion volume develop
$\det(A-\lambda I)=0$ and the solving of $(A-\lambda I)\vec v = \vec 0$ in
full; below we recall enough to stay self-contained.
\end{connectionbox}

\subsection{Recipe: eigenvalues and eigenvectors}

For a $2\times 2$ matrix
$A=\begin{psmallmatrix} a & b\\ c & d\end{psmallmatrix}$, write
$I=\begin{psmallmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{psmallmatrix}$ for the
\textbf{identity matrix}, so that $A-\lambda I$ is $A$ with $\lambda$ subtracted
from each diagonal entry. The equation $A\vec v=\lambda\vec v$ says
$(A-\lambda I)\vec v=\vec 0$, and as in the Lagrange multiplier chapter, this
has a nonzero solution $\vec v$ exactly when the determinant of $A-\lambda I$
is zero. So the eigenvalues are the roots $\lambda$ of the
\textbf{characteristic equation}
\[
  \det(A - \lambda I) = 0
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \lambda^2 - (\underbrace{a+d}_{\text{trace }\tau})\,\lambda
  + (\underbrace{ad-bc}_{\det\,\Delta}) = 0.
\]
Here the \textbf{trace} $\tau = a+d$ is the sum of the main diagonal entries, and
the \textbf{determinant} $\Delta = ad-bc$ is the factor by which the matrix scales areas.
By the quadratic formula, the eigenvalues are
\[
  \lambda = \tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\tau \pm \sqrt{\tau^2 - 4\Delta}\Bigr).
\]
For each
eigenvalue $\lambda$, the eigenvectors are the nonzero solutions $\vec v$ of the
system $(A-\lambda I)\vec v = \vec 0$. Once we have two
eigenvalue--eigenvector pairs $(\lambda_1, \vec v_1)$ and $(\lambda_2, \vec
v_2)$, each gives a solution $e^{\lambda_i t}\vec v_i$, and, because the system
is linear, \emph{any combination of solutions is again a solution}.

In the theorem below, vectors $\vec v_1, \dots, \vec v_n$ are called
\textbf{linearly independent} if none of them is a combination of the others;
for two vectors in the plane, this means they do not lie on the same line
through the origin. Then every vector in $\R^n$ (the space of lists of $n$ real
numbers) can be written as $c_1\vec v_1+\cdots+c_n\vec v_n$ in exactly one
way; we say the vectors \textbf{span} $\R^n$ and form a \textbf{basis}.

\begin{theorem}[General solution by eigen-decomposition]
\label{ch39:thm-gensol}
Suppose the $n\times n$ matrix $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors
$\vec v_1, \dots, \vec v_n$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$. Then
\emph{every} solution of $\vec u' = A\vec u$ has the form
\[
  \vec u(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t}\vec v_1
            + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t}\vec v_2
            + \cdots
            + c_n e^{\lambda_n t}\vec v_n,
\]
with constants $c_1, \dots, c_n$ fixed by the initial condition $\vec u(0)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
Why does the combination cover \emph{every} solution, not just some? Two
reasons. First, \emph{superposition}: if $\vec p$ and
$\vec q$ each solve $\vec u' = A\vec u$, then so does $c_1\vec p + c_2\vec q$,
because differentiation and multiplication by $A$ are both linear:
\[
  (c_1\vec p + c_2\vec q)' = c_1\vec p\,' + c_2\vec q\,' = c_1 A\vec p + c_2 A\vec q =
  A(c_1\vec p + c_2\vec q).
\]
So the listed combinations really are solutions.
Second, \emph{counting}: the existence and uniqueness theorem
(Section~\ref{ch36:sec-exist}) also holds for linear systems, and
says a solution is pinned down completely by its value at one instant, $\vec
u(0)\in\R^n$: $n$ numbers, no more. Our family has exactly
$n$ free constants $c_1,\dots,c_n$, and because the $\vec v_i$ form a basis,
we can choose them to hit \emph{any} starting vector $\vec u(0) = \sum c_i\vec v_i$.
So the family matches every possible initial condition, and by uniqueness it
contains every solution.
\end{whybox}

\begin{happeningbox}
The eigenvectors \emph{uncouple} the
system. Write the starting state in the eigenvector basis, $\vec u(0) = \sum c_i
\vec v_i$. In those coordinates the matrix $A$ stops mixing the components:
along each eigen-direction it acts as multiplication by $\lambda_i$. So in
the right coordinates, the system $\vec u' = A\vec u$ falls apart into
$n$ \emph{independent} copies of the scalar equation $z_i' = \lambda_i z_i$, each
solved by $z_i(t) = c_i e^{\lambda_i t}$. The coupling we saw in Section
\ref{ch39:what} came from using the original coordinates; in eigenvector
coordinates it disappears.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Look at the general solution
$\vec u(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t}\vec v_1 + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t}\vec v_2$.
Suppose $\lambda_1 = -1$ and $\lambda_2 = -4$. As $t \to \infty$, both terms decay to
zero. But which direction does the trajectory follow as it approaches the origin?
The term $e^{-4t}$ decays four times as fast as $e^{-t}$ (their ratio
$e^{-3t}$ goes to zero). Long after $e^{-4t}$
has become negligible, the slower $e^{-t}$ term is still present,
so every trajectory with $c_1\neq 0$ enters the origin tangent to the
slower eigen-direction $\vec v_1$.
\end{walkbox}

\section{A Fully Worked \texorpdfstring{$2\times2$}{2x2} System}
\label{ch39:worked}

Let us solve the system from Section \ref{ch39:what} completely, step by step.
\[
  \vec u' = A\vec u, \qquad
  A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.
\]

\textbf{Step 1 -- eigenvalues.} The trace is $\tau = 1 + 2 = 3$ and the
determinant is $\Delta = (1)(2) - (2)(3) = 2 - 6 = -4$. The characteristic
equation is
\[
  \lambda^2 - 3\lambda - 4 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (\lambda - 4)(\lambda + 1) = 0,
\]
so $\lambda_1 = 4$ and $\lambda_2 = -1$. Two real eigenvalues of opposite sign
already tell us (Section \ref{ch39:phase}) that the origin will be a
\emph{saddle}: solutions approach it along one direction and are pushed away
along another.

\textbf{Step 2 -- eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 4$.} Solve $(A - 4I)\vec v = \vec
0$:
\[
  A - 4I = \begin{pmatrix} 1-4 & 2 \\ 3 & 2-4 \end{pmatrix}
         = \begin{pmatrix} -3 & 2 \\ 3 & -2 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The top row says $-3v_1 + 2v_2 = 0$, that is, $v_2 = \tfrac{3}{2}v_1$. Choosing
$v_1 = 2$ to clear the fraction gives
$\vec v_1 = \begin{psmallmatrix} 2 \\ 3\end{psmallmatrix}$. (The bottom row
$3v_1 - 2v_2 = 0$ is the same equation. It must be: $A-4I$ has determinant
zero, so its two rows carry only one condition between them.)

\textbf{Step 3 -- eigenvector for $\lambda_2 = -1$.} Solve $(A + I)\vec v = \vec
0$:
\[
  A + I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad 2v_1 + 2v_2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; v_2 = -v_1.
\]
Choosing $v_1 = 1$ gives
$\vec v_2 = \begin{psmallmatrix} 1 \\ -1\end{psmallmatrix}$.

\textbf{Step 4 -- assemble.} By Theorem \ref{ch39:thm-gensol},
\[
  \vec u(t)
   = c_1\,e^{4t}\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}
   + c_2\,e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}.
\]

\textbf{Step 5 -- fit an initial condition.} Suppose
$\vec u(0) = \begin{psmallmatrix} 3 \\ 2\end{psmallmatrix}$. At $t=0$ the
exponentials are $1$, so we need
\[
  c_1\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}
  + c_2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  \begin{cases} 2c_1 + c_2 = 3,\\ 3c_1 - c_2 = 2. \end{cases}
\]
Adding the two equations gives $5c_1 = 5$, so $c_1 = 1$, and then $c_2 = 3 - 2 = 1$.
The trajectory through $(3,2)$ is
\[
  \vec u(t) = e^{4t}\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}
            + e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}.
\]

\begin{intuitionbox}
Read the answer as two tendencies. The $e^{4t}$ piece grows
fast and points along $\vec v_1 = (2,3)$: this is the \emph{unstable}
direction, along which the state is pushed away from the origin. The $e^{-t}$ piece decays and
points along $\vec v_2 = (1,-1)$: the \emph{stable} direction, along which the
state is pulled toward the origin. Soon the decaying term is negligible and
the trajectory runs nearly parallel to $(2,3)$, heading outward. The two
eigenvectors act as slanted coordinate axes for the flow: trajectories come in
alongside the stable axis $(1,-1)$ and bend away to leave alongside the unstable
axis $(2,3)$. The red curve in Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-saddle} is this trajectory,
traced for times before and after $t=0$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{trapbox}
A frequent slip: writing
$\vec u(t) = e^{\lambda_1 t}\vec v_1 + e^{\lambda_2 t}\vec v_2$ \emph{without} the
constants $c_1, c_2$, then being unable to match the initial condition. The
constants are what let the
solution start at the right point. With two eigen-solutions you have two
dials, exactly enough to hit any point in the plane. Without them you
describe only one trajectory out of infinitely many.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Phase Portraits: Drawing the Flow}

Formulas give exact coordinates, but a picture shows the whole family of
solutions at once. The \textbf{phase
portrait} of a planar system is the $(u_1, u_2)$ plane drawn with the
arrows of the vector field $A\vec u$ and a sample of solution
curves (the \emph{trajectories}) following them. A phase
portrait shows where every possible initial state goes, at a single
glance.

lines; trajectories move away along the unstable direction $(2,3)$ and are
pulled in along the stable direction $(1,-1)$, tracing hyperbola-like
curves between them.

\subsection{The classification: trace and determinant decide everything}

The shape of a planar phase portrait depends only on the two
eigenvalues of $A$, and those depend only on the trace $\tau = a+d$ and
determinant $\Delta = ad-bc$. Here are the main cases. (We call the origin
\emph{stable} when nearby trajectories approach it and \emph{unstable} when
some move away; Section~\ref{ch39:stability} makes this precise.)

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Two real eigenvalues, same sign.} If both are negative, every
trajectory decays to the origin: a stable \textbf{node} (also called a sink), drawn in
Figure \ref{ch39:fig-node}. If both are
positive, every trajectory moves outward: an unstable node (a source). The slower
eigen-direction sets the shape near the origin.

\item \textbf{Two real eigenvalues, opposite signs.} A \textbf{saddle}, as in
Figure \ref{ch39:fig-saddle}. The origin attracts along one eigen-direction and
repels along the other. It is always unstable: every trajectory not on the
stable line eventually leaves.

\item \textbf{Complex eigenvalues $\lambda = \alpha \pm i\beta$, $\beta\ne0$.}
The solutions involve $e^{\alpha t}(\cos\beta t, \sin\beta t)$: rotation
(from the $\cos,\sin$) times scaling (from $e^{\alpha t}$). If $\alpha<0$,
trajectories spiral inward: a stable \textbf{spiral}. If $\alpha>0$, an unstable
spiral. If $\alpha = 0$ exactly, they neither grow nor shrink but circle
forever: a \textbf{center}, surrounded by closed orbits.
\end{itemize}
The boundary cases, a zero eigenvalue ($\Delta=0$) or a repeated eigenvalue
($\tau^2=4\Delta$), sit on the dividing lines between these regions and need
separate treatment, which we skip.

\begin{whybox}
Where do the rotation and the spiral come from, and how does a complex
eigenvector give a real trajectory? Take $\lambda = \alpha + i\beta$ with
$\beta\neq 0$, and split its eigenvector into real and imaginary parts,
$\vec v = \vec a + i\vec b$ with $\vec a, \vec b$ real. Euler's formula
$e^{i\beta t} = \cos\beta t + i\sin\beta t$ expands the complex solution:
\[
  e^{\alpha t}(\cos\beta t + i\sin\beta t)(\vec a + i\vec b)
  = e^{\alpha t}(\cos\beta t\,\vec a - \sin\beta t\,\vec b)
    + i\,e^{\alpha t}(\sin\beta t\,\vec a + \cos\beta t\,\vec b).
\]
Because $A$ is real, the real part and the imaginary part each solve
$\vec u\,' = A\vec u$ on their own (the same argument as for second-order
equations in the previous chapter), giving two independent \emph{real} solutions. Read the
structure: $e^{\alpha t}$ scales the size, while $\cos\beta t$ and $\sin\beta t$
turn the state around in the plane spanned by $\vec a$ and $\vec b$. That is why an
undamped spring ($\alpha = 0$, eigenvalues $\pm i\omega$ with $\omega$ the
angular frequency) circles forever in a
center, while a damped spring ($\alpha < 0$) spirals into rest.
\end{whybox}

The whole classification fits on one diagram in the $(\tau, \Delta)$ plane,
Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-tracedet}. The
parabola $\tau^2 = 4\Delta$ is the boundary where the eigenvalues switch from
real to complex, because $\tau^2-4\Delta$ is the number under the square root
in the eigenvalue formula.

\begin{aestheticbox}
Two numbers, the trace and determinant of a $2\times2$
matrix, predict the long-run behavior of every trajectory of the system.
You need not solve anything to know whether a bridge will settle or
shake apart, whether a population will reach equilibrium or oscillate, whether a
control system is stable. You compute $\tau$ and $\Delta$ and read off the
answer from one picture. The eigenvalue viewpoint
turns a question about motion over time into algebra on two numbers.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose you compute the determinant of a $2\times 2$
system matrix and get $\Delta = -6$. Can the origin ever be a stable equilibrium?
Can it be a spiral? No. The characteristic polynomial factors as
$\lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\Delta=(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2)$, and
comparing constant terms shows that the determinant is the product of the eigenvalues,
$\Delta = \lambda_1 \lambda_2$. If $\Delta < 0$, the eigenvalues cannot be
a complex-conjugate pair, because $(\alpha+i\beta)(\alpha-i\beta) = \alpha^2+\beta^2 \ge 0$.
So the eigenvalues must be real with opposite signs: one positive, one negative.
Trajectories always move outward along the eigen-direction with the positive
eigenvalue, so the origin is a saddle, and unstable, every time.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{A spiral, drawn}

For a concrete case, take
\[
  A = \begin{pmatrix} -0.3 & -1 \\ 1 & -0.3\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Here $\tau = -0.6$ and $\Delta = 0.09 + 1 = 1.09$, so
\[
  \lambda = \tfrac12\Bigl(-0.6 \pm \sqrt{0.36 - 4.36}\Bigr)
          = \tfrac12\bigl(-0.6 \pm \sqrt{-4}\bigr) = -0.3 \pm i,
\]
complex with negative real part: a stable spiral. For $\lambda = -0.3 + i$, the first row of
$(A-\lambda I)\vec v = \vec 0$ reads $-i v_1 - v_2 = 0$, so
\[
  \vec v = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} + i\begin{pmatrix}0\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The real and imaginary parts of $e^{-0.3t}(\cos t + i\sin t)\vec v$ are the two
real solutions
\[
  e^{-0.3t}\begin{pmatrix}\cos t\\ \sin t\end{pmatrix}
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  e^{-0.3t}\begin{pmatrix}\sin t\\ -\cos t\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Every trajectory winds counterclockwise and decays inward to the origin
(Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-spiral}).

\section{Stability: When Does the System Settle?}
\label{ch39:stability}

A point $\vec u^{*}$ where $\vec F(\vec u^{*}) = \vec 0$ is an
\textbf{equilibrium}: put the state dot there and it never moves, because its
velocity is zero. For the linear system $\vec u' = A\vec u$ the origin is always
an equilibrium (and the only one if $\det A\neq 0$, since then $A\vec u=\vec 0$
has only the solution $\vec u=\vec 0$). A basic practical question
in engineering is: if the system is pushed slightly away from equilibrium,
does it return, or does it move further away?

The eigenvalues answer it. A solution component behaves like
$e^{\lambda t}$. For complex $\lambda=\alpha+i\beta$,
$e^{\lambda t}=e^{\alpha t}(\cos\beta t+i\sin\beta t)$ has size $e^{\alpha t}$,
so it shrinks if the real part $\alpha$ is negative and
grows if it is positive. Hence:

\begin{theorem}[Linear stability]
\label{ch39:thm-stability}
For $\vec u' = A\vec u$, the origin is
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{asymptotically stable} (every trajectory $\to \vec 0$) if and only
if \emph{every} eigenvalue of $A$ has negative real part;
\item \textbf{unstable} if some eigenvalue has positive real part;
\item \textbf{marginally stable} (trajectories stay bounded but need not decay)
when no eigenvalue has positive real part and those with zero real part (on the
imaginary axis) are
not repeated, e.g.\ a center with $\lambda = \pm i\beta$, $\beta \neq 0$. (A
repeated eigenvalue on the axis can cause slow growth: $A =
\begin{psmallmatrix}0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{psmallmatrix}$ has the solution
$(t, 1)$.)
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
\emph{The system is stable exactly when every growth
rate is a decay rate.} A single eigenvalue in the right half of the
complex plane (positive real part), meaning one direction in which a
small push grows, is enough to
destroy stability: a typical trajectory has a nonzero component
along every eigen-direction, and the growing one eventually swamps the rest.
Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-eig-plane} shows the eigenvalues of this chapter's
examples on the complex plane.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Flight stability and eigenvalue analysis.} An aircraft in
steady flight is an equilibrium of a system of differential equations governing
its pitch, roll, and yaw (nose up or down, wings tilting, nose turning left or
right). Engineers replace these equations by their linear approximation near
steady flight (the method of Section~\ref{ch39:nonlinear}) and compute its
eigenvalues. If all have negative real part, the effect of a gust that tips the
plane dies away and the plane corrects itself. One such pattern, the
\emph{phugoid} mode, is a slow, lightly damped up-and-down oscillation (a stable
spiral) that passengers can sometimes feel as a gentle rise and fall. If an
eigenvalue moves into the right half-plane, the aircraft is unstable and needs a
control system to fly at all; the control system adds feedback terms to $A$
that shift the eigenvalues to the left. Some modern fighter jets are deliberately
built unstable for agility and fly only because a computer adjusts the control
surfaces many times a second, keeping the eigenvalues of the controlled aircraft
on the left. Engineers call these eigenvalues \emph{poles}, and choosing the
feedback to put them where you want, called ``pole placement,'' is a core tool
of control engineering.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{historybox}
The geometric and stability theory of differential equations was developed in the
late nineteenth century, when exact formulas proved insufficient for
complicated systems. For two hundred years after Newton, mathematicians sought exact
formulas for every moving system. They solved the two-body problem (a single
planet orbiting the Sun) with Kepler's ellipses, but the three-body problem
(the Sun, Earth, and Moon pulling on one another) defeated every attempt. At the same
time, the Industrial Revolution produced high-speed steam engines whose
mechanical speed regulators (governors) suffered from violent
oscillations called ``hunting'': machines that shook themselves apart because
their feedback overcorrected.

With only paper-and-pencil algebra, linear approximations, and
series expansions that often diverged, mathematicians had to
invent new tools. James Clerk Maxwell started stability theory in 1868 with ``On
Governors,'' analyzing when linear feedback stabilizes an engine. Edward Routh
(1876) and Adolf Hurwitz (1895) found algebraic tests for whether all
roots of a polynomial have negative real parts, without calculating the roots. In 1892,
the Russian mathematician Aleksandr Lyapunov published \emph{The General
Problem of the Stability of Motion}, introducing what we now call Lyapunov
functions: energy-like quantities that decrease along trajectories and so prove a
system settles back to rest without needing explicit solutions.

The biggest change came with Henri Poincar\'e. In 1885 King Oscar II
of Sweden announced an international prize for showing whether the solar system
is stable. Poincar\'e submitted a
memoir claiming a proof and won the prize in 1889. But while the paper was being
typeset for the journal \emph{Acta Mathematica}, the young editor Edvard
Phragm\'en spotted an unexplained gap. Investigating it, Poincar\'e found
that his proof had collapsed: the curves along which trajectories approach and
leave an unstable periodic orbit do not join up smoothly but cross each other
infinitely often, in a tangle of extraordinary complexity.
Poincar\'e paid, out of his own pocket, more than the prize was worth to recall
and destroy the printed copies of the journal, rewrote the memoir, and in doing
so discovered what we now call deterministic chaos (Section~\ref{ch39:nonlinear}).

Poincar\'e gave up the hunt for exact formulas and founded the modern
qualitative theory of dynamical systems: drawing trajectories directly in phase
space, classifying equilibria, and studying the shape of the flow. Many questions remain open.
The second half of Hilbert's 16th
Problem (one of 23 problems David Hilbert posed in 1900) asks for the maximum number and possible
arrangements of limit cycles (isolated periodic orbits, defined in
Section~\ref{ch39:nonlinear}) for polynomial systems
$x'=P(x,y)$, $y'=Q(x,y)$. Even for polynomials of degree two, it
is unsolved today.
\end{historybox}

\section{A Glimpse Beyond the Linear World}
\label{ch39:nonlinear}

Everything so far rested on linearity: the right-hand side was $A\vec u$, a
matrix times the state. Real systems are usually \emph{nonlinear}: the
right-hand side contains products, powers or other functions of the unknowns.
Examples are foxes eating rabbits (the rate of eating involves the product
$RF$), the swing of a pendulum at large angles, and the weather. We usually
cannot solve these with formulas. But two ideas take us a long way.

\subsection{Linearization: zoom in and the curve looks straight}

Take a system $\vec u' = \vec F(\vec u)$ whose right-hand side does not depend
on $t$, with components $F_1, F_2$, and an equilibrium $\vec u^{*}$. Write a
nearby state as $\vec u = \vec u^{*} + \vec h$, where $\vec h$ is the small
displacement from equilibrium. Approximate $\vec F$ near $\vec u^{*}$ by its
linear (tangent-plane) approximation from the partial-derivatives chapters. The constant term
vanishes because $\vec F(\vec u^{*})=\vec 0$, and to first order
\[
  \vec F(\vec u^{*} + \vec h) \approx J\,\vec h,
  \qquad
  J = \begin{pmatrix}
    \pdv{F_1}{u_1} & \pdv{F_1}{u_2} \\[4pt]
    \pdv{F_2}{u_1} & \pdv{F_2}{u_2}
  \end{pmatrix}_{\vec u^{*}},
\]
where $J$ is the \textbf{Jacobian matrix}: the grid of all first partial
derivatives, evaluated at $\vec u^{*}$. It is the
higher-dimensional version of the single derivative $f'(x)$. Since $\vec
u^{*}$ is constant, $\vec u' = \vec h'$, so \emph{near an
equilibrium, a nonlinear system looks like the linear system $\vec h' = J\vec h$},
and the eigenvalues of $J$ classify the equilibrium as before.

\begin{whybox}
This is the main reason the linear theory was worth the effort. A
nonlinear vector field, magnified enough around an
equilibrium, looks like its linearization, just as a smooth
curve, magnified near a point, looks like its tangent line. A theorem of
Hartman and Grobman makes this precise: provided no eigenvalue of $J$ has zero
real part, the nonlinear phase portrait near the equilibrium is a slightly
bent copy of the linear one. Saddles stay saddles, sinks stay sinks, sources stay sources.
So the trace--determinant classification applies to nonlinear systems too, near
each equilibrium, except in the excluded cases such as centers.
Calculus's first idea, ``replace the curve by its tangent,'' returns one last
time, now as replacing a vector field by its Jacobian.
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[The pendulum]
\label{ch39:ex-pendulum}
The undamped pendulum $\theta'' = -\sin\theta$ becomes the system $x_1' = x_2$,
$x_2' = -\sin x_1$ (with $x_1=\theta$ the angle and $x_2 = \theta'$ the angular
velocity). Its Jacobian is $J = \begin{psmallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\cos x_1 &
0\end{psmallmatrix}$. At the bottom-hanging rest state $x_1 = 0$:
$J = \begin{psmallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0\end{psmallmatrix}$, eigenvalues
$\pm i$, the eigenvalues of a \emph{center}: the pendulum swinging back and forth forever without
friction. (Eigenvalues with zero real part are the case the Hartman--Grobman
theorem excludes, so here linearization alone cannot settle the question; the
energy argument below confirms that the hanging state really is a center.)
At the inverted balance point $x_1 = \pi$:
$J = \begin{psmallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{psmallmatrix}$, eigenvalues
$\pm 1$, a \emph{saddle}, unstable. This matches the physical fact that
balancing a pendulum upright is hopeless: the tiniest nudge grows along the
unstable eigen-direction.

Linearization tells us what happens near each equilibrium. To see the whole
phase plane, Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-pendulum}, use the total mechanical energy
\[
  E = \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta')^2 - \cos\theta
\]
(kinetic energy plus height energy, in units where the constants are $1$). It
stays constant along every trajectory, because
$\frac{dE}{dt} = \theta'\theta'' + \sin\theta\,\theta' = \theta'(\theta''+\sin\theta) = 0$.
So each trajectory lies on one level curve $E = \text{const}$.
At low energy ($E < 1$), the pendulum cannot reach the top; it
traces closed blue loops around the center
$(0,0)$, swinging back and forth. At high energy ($E > 1$), the pendulum has
enough speed to pass over the top, whirling round and round along wavy purple tracks that
never reverse direction.

Dividing these two behaviors is the boundary curve called the
\textbf{separatrix} (from the Latin for ``divider''), shown in red. It
has energy $E=1$, so $(\theta')^2 = 2(1+\cos\theta) = 4\cos^2(\theta/2)$, that is,
$\theta' = \pm 2\cos(\theta/2)$, and it runs between the saddles
$(\pm\pi, 0)$. A trajectory started exactly
on the separatrix would coast upward, slowing down and taking an
infinite amount of time to come to rest balanced vertically on top.
\end{example}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} At the bottom rest point $\theta = 0$, the undamped
pendulum has eigenvalues $\pm i$ (a center). In a real room with friction, how
do the eigenvalues change, and what happens to the phase portrait? Air
resistance introduces a velocity drag term $-b\theta'$, which adds $-b$ to the
trace. For light damping ($0 < b < 2$) the eigenvalues become
$-b/2 \pm i\sqrt{1 - b^2/4}$, shifting their real
part into negative territory. The concentric closed orbits become inward spirals:
every swinging pendulum eventually winds down and comes to rest at the bottom.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{What linearization cannot see: limit cycles and chaos}

Linearization describes a nonlinear system only near an equilibrium. Farther
away, nonlinear systems can do things that linear systems never do.

A \textbf{limit cycle} is an \emph{isolated} closed loop (one with no other closed
loops right next to it) that nearby trajectories spiral
\emph{onto}, from inside and from outside. It is a self-sustaining oscillation with its own preferred size,
like a heartbeat or the steady sound of a bowed violin string.
Figure~\ref{ch39:fig-limit-cycle} shows one. The Van der Pol oscillator
$x'' - \mu(1-x^2)x' + x = 0$, with $\mu>0$ a constant, is the classic example from
electronics. No linear system can produce a limit cycle: a linear center has a
whole family of closed orbits of every size, while a limit cycle picks out
exactly one.

In three or more dimensions, nonlinear systems can be \textbf{chaotic}:
trajectories never repeat and never settle, and depend so sensitively on their
starting point that two nearby states move apart exponentially fast. The best
known example is the Lorenz system,
\[
  x' = \sigma(y-x), \qquad y' = x(\rho - z) - y, \qquad z' = xy - \beta z,
\]
where $\sigma$, $\rho$, $\beta$ are positive constants (Lorenz used $\sigma=10$,
$\rho=28$, $\beta=8/3$). Edward Lorenz derived it in 1963 from a simplified model of
rising and sinking air. Its trajectories are drawn toward a set called
an \textbf{attractor}: a region of phase space that pulls in nearby
trajectories and keeps them, while on it they wander forever without repeating.
This is the \emph{Lorenz attractor}, whose trajectories loop around two
lobes, switching from one to the other in an order that looks random.

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{The butterfly effect and the limits of forecasting.} Lorenz discovered
chaos by accident. Rerunning a weather simulation on his 1960s computer, he
typed in a midstream value rounded to three decimals ($0.506$) instead of the
full six the machine stored ($0.506127$), expecting the same forecast.
Before long the two runs had nothing in
common. The tiny rounding error, amplified by the system's exponential
sensitivity, changed the whole future. A 1972 lecture by Lorenz gave the lesson its
name, the \textbf{butterfly effect}: a butterfly flapping its wings in Brazil might set off a tornado in
Texas, not because the flap carries much energy, but because the atmosphere
keeps magnifying tiny differences. So long-range weather prediction is
limited in practice, however powerful the computer and however
dense the weather sensors.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
The Lorenz system separates \emph{determinism} from \emph{predictability}. Nothing in the
equations is random: each initial point determines one trajectory for all time.
Yet because nearby trajectories separate exponentially fast, any tiny measurement error
in the starting state grows until it is as large as the whole attractor. Exact laws do not guarantee
long-range forecasts when initial measurements have finite precision.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Where to Go Next}
\label{ch39:next}

This chapter is at the edge of what a first calculus book can cover. Three
subjects continue from here.

\textbf{Partial differential equations.} Every system here had a single
independent variable, time, so the unknowns were functions of $t$ alone:
\emph{ordinary} differential equations. But the temperature in a heated \emph{rod}
varies with both position and time; a drumhead vibrates across a \emph{surface};
a quantum wavefunction fills \emph{space}. These obey \textbf{partial}
differential equations (PDEs), where the unknown depends on several variables and
partial derivatives appear. An example is the heat equation
\[
  \pdv{u}{t} = k\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2},
\]
where $u(x,t)$ is the temperature at position $x$ and time $t$ and $k$ is a
constant measuring how fast heat spreads; others are the wave equation,
Laplace's equation and the Schr\"odinger equation of quantum mechanics. The
main tools are the partial derivatives of the multivariable
chapters and \emph{Fourier series}, which write a function as an infinite sum
of sines and cosines, much as a Taylor series writes it as a sum of powers.

\textbf{Numerical methods.} Most differential equations that come up in practice
cannot be solved by a formula. We solve them by stepping forward in time: from the state now,
the equation gives the velocity now, so we take a small time step $\Delta t$ and
repeat. The simplest version is Euler's method,
$\vec u_{n+1} = \vec u_n + \Delta t\,\vec F(\vec u_n)$, where $\vec u_n$ is the
approximate state after $n$ steps; the standard tool is the
fourth-order Runge--Kutta method, which evaluates the slope at four points within each step
and averages them, giving far smaller errors for the same step size. Weather forecasts, climate models, spacecraft trajectories, and
cloth in animated films are all differential equations stepped forward by
computers.

\textbf{Dynamical systems.} The phase-portrait, stability, and chaos ideas of
this chapter are the start of a large modern field. It studies
bifurcations (where an
equilibrium suddenly changes character as a parameter crosses a threshold),
strange attractors (attractors with an infinitely detailed, fractal shape, like
Lorenz's), the long-run averages of chaotic motion, and the geometry of flows.

\begin{connectionbox}
By the end of this book, linear algebra and calculus work as one.
We began with the slope of one curve; we end with the eigenvalues of a
matrix deciding where every trajectory of a system goes. The companion
\emph{Linear Algebra From the Ground Up} develops eigenvalues, diagonalization,
and the matrix exponential
$e^{At}$, which packages \emph{all} solutions of $\vec u'=A\vec u$ into the
single formula $\vec u(t) = e^{At}\vec u(0)$, just as $u(t)=e^{\lambda t}u(0)$
does in the scalar case.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
Looking back at the progression, we expressed coupled equations in matrix form $\vec u' = A\vec u$ and tested $e^{\lambda t}\vec v$, leading directly to the eigenvalue problem $A\vec v = \lambda \vec v$ whose eigenvectors decouple the system into scalar components. Next, we drew the flow as a phase portrait, classified it by trace and determinant, and read stability from the signs of the eigenvalue real parts. Finally, we saw how Jacobians linearize nonlinear systems locally before global phenomena such as limit cycles and chaos take over.
\end{recoverybox}

\textbf{Where to go next in this volume.}
Two appendices close the book. The first reviews the algebraic identities, inequalities, absolute-value geometry, functions, and proof techniques (direct proof, contradiction, and induction) used throughout the text. The second develops the completeness of the real numbers -- the least-upper-bound axiom that underpins every existence theorem in calculus.

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch39:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Reduce to a system]
Convert each higher-order equation to a first-order system $\vec u' = A\vec u$
(or $\vec x' = \vec F(\vec x)$ if nonlinear), naming your new variables
explicitly. (a) $y'' - 3y' + 2y = 0$. (b) $y''' = y$. (c) $y'' + \sin y = 0$.
For (a), write down the companion matrix and state its trace and determinant.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Eigen-solve a system]
For $A = \begin{psmallmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{psmallmatrix}$, find the
eigenvalues and eigenvectors, write the general solution of $\vec u' = A\vec u$,
and find the particular solution with $\vec u(0) = \begin{psmallmatrix} 1 \\
3\end{psmallmatrix}$. Classify the origin.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A saddle]
The matrix $A = \begin{psmallmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1\end{psmallmatrix}$ has
eigenvalues $4$ and $-2$. Find the eigenvectors and sketch the phase portrait by
hand, marking the stable and unstable directions and the flow along each.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Complex eigenvalues]
For $A = \begin{psmallmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 2 & 0\end{psmallmatrix}$, show the
eigenvalues are $\pm 2i$. What kind of equilibrium is the origin? Without solving,
describe the shape of every trajectory. Then verify that $u_1 = \cos 2t$, $u_2 =
\sin 2t$ is a solution.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trace--determinant reading]
For each matrix, compute $\tau$ and $\Delta$ and classify the origin
(stable/unstable node, saddle, stable/unstable spiral, or center) \emph{without}
finding the eigenvectors. (a) $\begin{psmallmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 &
-3\end{psmallmatrix}$. (b) $\begin{psmallmatrix} -1 & 4 \\ -1 &
-1\end{psmallmatrix}$. (c) $\begin{psmallmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 &
3\end{psmallmatrix}$. (d) $\begin{psmallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -4 &
0\end{psmallmatrix}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Stability hinges on a parameter]
Consider $A = \begin{psmallmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ -1 & k\end{psmallmatrix}$ with $k$
a real parameter. Compute the trace and determinant, and find the range of $k$
for which the origin is asymptotically stable. What happens at the value of $k$
where stability is lost?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Damped oscillator, both ways]
Return to $y'' + 2y' + 5y = 0$ from Example \ref{ch39:ex-damped}. (a) Find the
eigenvalues of the companion matrix. (b) Confirm they are complex with negative
real part, and say what kind of equilibrium and what physical behavior this
predicts. (c) Relate the real and imaginary parts of the eigenvalues to the decay
rate and oscillation frequency of the solution.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Linearize a nonlinear system]
The system $x' = x(3 - x - y)$, $y' = y(2 - x)$ models two competing species.
(a) Find all equilibria (there are three, including one in the interior with both
populations positive). (b) Compute the Jacobian $J$ at the interior equilibrium.
(c) Find its eigenvalues and classify that equilibrium. Is coexistence stable?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Pendulum equilibria]
Using Example \ref{ch39:ex-pendulum}, add light damping: $x_1' = x_2$, $x_2' =
-\sin x_1 - 0.2\,x_2$. Recompute the Jacobian at the hanging rest state $(0,0)$.
How do its eigenvalues differ from the undamped case, and what does the change
predict about the pendulum's motion?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual: why eigenvectors?]
In your own words, explain why guessing a solution of the form $\vec u(t) =
e^{\lambda t}\vec v$ forces $\vec v$ to be an eigenvector of $A$. Then explain
why a $2\times2$ system with two independent eigenvectors has \emph{exactly} the
right number of free constants to match any initial condition.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A taste of chaos, by hand]
You cannot solve the Lorenz system, but you can march it. With $\sigma=10$,
$\rho=28$, $\beta=8/3$, and step $\Delta t = 0.01$, do \emph{three} Euler steps
starting from $(x,y,z) = (1,1,1)$, reporting all coordinates each step. Then
repeat from $(1.001, 1, 1)$. Comment on how the two runs compare after three
steps -- and explain why, after a few thousand steps, they would have nothing in
common.
\end{exercise}
