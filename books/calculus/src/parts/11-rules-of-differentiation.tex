\chapter{The Rules of Differentiation, and Why They Work}

In the previous chapter you met the derivative as a \emph{limit}: the slope of a curve
at a point, the instantaneous rate of change, the number that the slopes of shrinking
secant lines close in on. But if you had to return to
the limit
\[
  f'(x) \;=\; \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}
\]
every time you wanted a derivative, calculus would be unusable. Differentiating
$x^7 - 4x^3 + \tfrac{1}{x}$ from scratch would take a page of algebra.

This chapter builds a small set of \emph{rules} that let you differentiate almost any
function you can write down, quickly and mechanically, without going back to the limit.
Each rule is derived from the limit definition, so you will see \emph{why} it has the
shape it has, and you will be able to rebuild any rule you forget.

\begin{focusbox}
Each rule gets (1) a picture or piece of intuition, (2) a derivation from the limit
definition, (3) a box explaining why it works, and (4) worked examples. The easy rules
come first (constants, sums, powers), then the rules for products and quotients, then
the chain rule for one function fed into another, which almost everything later in this
book depends on.
\end{focusbox}

\section{The Building Blocks: Two Derivatives We Already Know}
\label{ch11:building-blocks}

Two derivatives can be read straight off the limit definition, and everything else will
be assembled from them.

\subsection{The derivative of a constant is zero}

Let $f(x) = c$, a constant -- the graph is a flat horizontal line at height $c$. A flat
line has slope $0$ everywhere, so we already expect $f'(x)=0$. The limit confirms it:
\begin{align*}
  f'(x) &= \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}
         = \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{c - c}{h} \\
        &= \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{0}{h}
         = \lim_{h\To 0} 0 = 0.
\end{align*}
The numerator is exactly zero for \emph{every} $h$, because a constant function does not
change. There is nothing accumulating, so there is no rate.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A constant function is a thing that never moves. Its rate of change is the rate at which
a parked car covers distance: zero. This is why an added constant disappears when you
differentiate: $x^2+5$ and $x^2$ have the same derivative. (This fact returns with
integrals, as the ``$+C$'' added to every answer.)
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{The derivative of $x$ is one}

Let $f(x)=x$. The graph is the $45^\circ$ line through the origin, slope $1$ everywhere.
Again the limit agrees:
\[
  f'(x) = \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{(x+h)-x}{h}
        = \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{h}{h}
        = \lim_{h\To 0} 1 = 1.
\]
When $x$ increases by $h$, the output $f(x)=x$ increases by exactly $h$. Output change
divided by input change is $h/h = 1$. The function $f(x)=x$ is called the
\emph{identity function} because it returns its input unchanged; it passes change
through untouched, so its rate is $1$.

These two facts -- $\ddx{c}=0$ and $\ddx{x}=1$ -- are the building blocks. The rest of
the chapter shows how to combine them.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} What is $\ddx{\pi^3}$? And what is $\ddx{5x}$?
Answer: $\pi^3$ is a fixed number (roughly $31.006$), not a variable,
so its derivative is $0$, not $3\pi^2$. For $5x$, the constant factor $5$ scales the
derivative of $x$ (which is $1$), giving $5\cdot 1 = 5$. Distinguishing fixed numbers
from changing variables is the first habit of fluent differentiation.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Constant Multiples and Sums: Differentiation is Linear}
\label{ch11:linearity}

\subsection{The constant-multiple rule}

Suppose you already know $f'$, and you scale the whole function by a constant $k$, forming
$g(x)=k\,f(x)$. What happens to the slope? Stretching a graph vertically by a factor of
$k$ stretches all its \emph{rises} by $k$ while leaving the \emph{runs} alone -- so every
slope is multiplied by $k$. Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-constmult} shows this for $k=2$.

\begin{theorem}[Constant-multiple rule]\label{ch11:thm-constmult}
If $f$ is differentiable and $k$ is a constant, then $k f$ is differentiable and
\[
  \ddx{k\,f(x)} = k\,f'(x).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Go to the definition and watch the constant ride along outside the difference quotient:
\begin{align*}
  \big(kf\big)'(x)
  &= \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{k f(x+h)-k f(x)}{h}
   = \lim_{h\To 0} k\cdot\frac{f(x+h)- f(x)}{h} \\
  &= k\lim_{h\To 0}\frac{f(x+h)- f(x)}{h}
   = k\,f'(x).
\end{align*}
The only step that needs a reason is pulling $k$ out of the limit, which the limit
laws allow: a constant factor can be moved outside a limit.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The sum rule}

Now add two functions: $g(x)=f(x)+p(x)$. If $f$ is rising at rate $f'$ and $p$ is rising
at rate $p'$, then their sum rises at the combined rate $f'+p'$. Rates add, the
way two streams pouring into one tank add their flows (Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-sum}).

\begin{theorem}[Sum (and difference) rule]\label{ch11:thm-sum}
If $f$ and $p$ are differentiable, then so is $f\pm p$, and
\[
  \ddx{f(x)\pm p(x)} = f'(x)\pm p'(x).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Form the difference quotient and regroup the four terms so the $f$'s sit together and the
$p$'s sit together:
\begin{align*}
  \big(f+p\big)'(x)
  &= \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{[f(x+h)+p(x+h)] - [f(x)+p(x)]}{h} \\
  &= \lim_{h\To 0}\!\left(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} + \frac{p(x+h)-p(x)}{h}\right).
\end{align*}
The limit of a sum is the sum of the limits (each exists, by assumption), so this is
$f'(x)+p'(x)$. The subtraction case is identical with a minus sign.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
Together, the constant-multiple and sum rules say the derivative is a \emph{linear
operator}: an operation on functions that respects scaling and addition, so the
derivative of a combination is the same combination of the separate derivatives:
$\ddx{a f + b g} = a f' + b g'$ for any constants $a$ and $b$. This is what lets you
differentiate a long polynomial term by term. The reason is that the difference
quotient is built from subtraction and division by $h$, both of which respect sums and
constant multiples, and taking the limit respects them too.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Linearity covers sums and constant multiples \emph{only}. It does \emph{not} extend to
products or quotients:
\[
  \ddx{f\cdot g}\;\ne\; f'\cdot g',
  \qquad
  \ddx{\frac{f}{g}}\;\ne\;\frac{f'}{g'}.
\]
A great many wrong answers in calculus come from wishing products behaved as nicely as
sums. They do not. The next two sections show what happens instead.
\end{trapbox}

\section{The Power Rule}
\label{ch11:power-rule}

For any constant exponent $n$,
\[
  \boxed{\;\ddx{x^n} = n\,x^{\,n-1}.\;}
\]
``Bring the exponent down in front, then knock the exponent down by one.'' For example,
$\ddx{x^5} = 5x^4$. Where does it come from? We prove it in stages: for $x^2$ and
$x^3$ by hand, then for all positive integers $n$ using the binomial theorem, then for
one negative and one fractional exponent. The general case comes in the next chapter.

\subsection{Warm-up: $x^2$ and $x^3$ by hand}

\begin{example}[The derivative of $x^2$]\label{ch11:ex-x2}
Let $f(x)=x^2$. Then
\begin{align*}
  f'(x) &= \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h}
         = \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} \\
        &= \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{2xh + h^2}{h}
         = \lim_{h\To 0}(2x + h)
         = 2x.
\end{align*}
The $x^2$ cancels; the surviving terms all carry an $h$, so dividing by $h$ leaves
$2x+h$, and the leftover $h$ goes to $0$ in the limit. Result: $\ddx{x^2}=2x = 2x^{2-1}$,
which is $n x^{n-1}$ with $n=2$. Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-power} checks this slope formula
against two tangent lines.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The derivative of $x^3$]\label{ch11:ex-x3}
Now $f(x)=x^3$. Using $(x+h)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2 h + 3x h^2 + h^3$,
\begin{align*}
  f'(x) &= \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{3x^2 h + 3x h^2 + h^3}{h} \\
        &= \lim_{h\To 0}\big(3x^2 + 3x h + h^2\big)
         = 3x^2 = 3x^{3-1}.
\end{align*}
Again the lone $x^3$ cancels, every surviving term has at least one $h$, and only the term with
a \emph{single} $h$ --- namely $3x^2 h$ --- contributes after we divide by $h$ and take
$h\To 0$. That single term, the one with $h$ to the first power, sets the instantaneous
rate.
\end{example}

\begin{happeningbox}
Look at what survives. When you expand $(x+h)^n$ you get $x^n$, plus a term with exactly
one factor of $h$, plus terms in $h^2, h^3,\dots$ (called \emph{higher-order terms}; the
\emph{order} of a term is its power of $h$). The $x^n$ cancels the $-x^n$. After dividing
by $h$, the single-$h$ term loses its $h$ and no longer depends on $h$; every other term
still carries at least one factor of $h$ and therefore vanishes as $h\To 0$. So
\emph{the derivative is the coefficient of the term proportional to $h$}. Terms in $h^2$
and beyond are called \emph{second-order small}: when $h$ is tiny, $h^2$ is far tinier
(for $h=0.001$, $h^2=0.000001$), and even after dividing by $h$ they still go to $0$.
Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-orders} shows this for $x^3$ at $x=1$. The derivative measures the
part of the change in a function that is proportional to the size of the step; the rest
of differential calculus is built on this idea.
\end{happeningbox}

\subsection{All positive integers at once: the binomial theorem}

The hand calculations follow a pattern, and the \emph{binomial theorem} describes it.
For any positive integer $n$ it expands
\[
  (x+h)^n
  = x^n + n x^{\,n-1} h + \binom{n}{2} x^{\,n-2} h^2 + \cdots + h^n .
\]
Here $\binom{n}{2}$ (read ``$n$ choose $2$'') stands for the number $\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}$.
The expansion comes from multiplying out $n$ copies of $(x+h)$: each term picks either
$x$ or $h$ from every copy, and the coefficient counts the ways to do it. For instance
the coefficient $n$ of $x^{n-1}h$ counts the $n$ ways to choose which single copy
supplies the $h$. For $n=3$ the coefficients are $1, 3, 3, 1$, as in the expansion above.
The first term is $x^n$, the second is $n x^{n-1}h$ (this is the one that matters), and
every later term carries $h^2$ or higher.

\begin{theorem}[Power rule, positive integers]\label{ch11:thm-power-int}
For every positive integer $n$, $\ddx{x^n} = n x^{\,n-1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Subtract $x^n$, divide by $h$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{(x+h)^n - x^n}{h}
  &= \frac{n x^{n-1}h + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h^2 + \cdots + h^n}{h} \\
  &= n x^{n-1} + \underbrace{\binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h + \cdots + h^{n-1}}_{\text{every term has an } h}.
\end{align*}
As $h\To 0$ every term in the underbrace contains at least one factor of $h$ and so tends
to $0$. What remains is $n x^{n-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} What does the power rule give when $n=1$?
The formula yields $1\cdot x^{1-1} = 1\cdot x^0 = 1$, recovering our building block
$\ddx{x}=1$ from Section~\ref{ch11:building-blocks}. And when $n=0$ (for $x\ne 0$),
$x^0=1$ yields $0\cdot x^{-1} = 0$, matching $\ddx{c}=0$ (which itself holds even at
$x=0$). So both building blocks are special cases of the power rule.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Why it survives to negative and fractional exponents}

The binomial proof only handles $n=1,2,3,\dots$ Yet the rule is true far more generally:
$\ddx{x^{-2}} = -2x^{-3}$ and $\ddx{x^{1/2}} = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2}$ both obey
``$n x^{n-1}$.'' We cannot prove the full real-exponent case yet. The
cleanest proof waits until we have the chain rule and the logarithm (next chapter), where
writing $x^n = e^{n\ln x}$ reduces it to one line. Two special cases can be done now,
and each uses a useful algebra trick.

\begin{example}[A negative exponent: $1/x$]\label{ch11:ex-reciprocal}
Let $f(x)=x^{-1}=1/x$. Combine the two fractions over the common denominator $x(x+h)$:
\begin{align*}
  f'(x) &= \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{1}{h}\!\left(\frac{1}{x+h}-\frac{1}{x}\right)
         = \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{1}{h}\cdot\frac{x-(x+h)}{x(x+h)} \\
        &= \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{-h}{h\,x(x+h)}
         = \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{-1}{x(x+h)}
         = -\frac{1}{x^2}.
\end{align*}
That is $-x^{-2} = (-1)x^{-1-1}$: the power rule with $n=-1$. The minus sign says the
graph of $1/x$ slopes downward everywhere, and the $x^2$ below says it flattens quickly
as $x$ grows (Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-reciprocal}).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A fractional exponent: $\sqrt{x}$]\label{ch11:ex-sqrt}
Let $f(x)=\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}$ (for $x>0$). The trick is to rationalize: multiply top and
bottom by the \emph{conjugate} $\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x}$ (the same two terms with the
opposite sign between them), which clears the difference of square roots:
\begin{align*}
  f'(x) &= \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt{x}}{h} \\
        &= \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{(\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt{x})(\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x})}{h(\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x})}.
\end{align*}
The numerator collapses to $(x+h)-x = h$, which cancels the $h$ below:
\[
  f'(x)=\lim_{h\To 0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt{x}}
       = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}
       = \tfrac12\,x^{-1/2}.
\]
And $\tfrac12 x^{-1/2} = \tfrac12 x^{\,1/2 - 1}$: the power rule again, now with
$n=\tfrac12$.
\end{example}

\begin{whybox}
Why does ``bring down the exponent'' work? Think of $x^2$ as the area of a square of
side $x$, and $x^3$ as the volume of a cube of side $x$ (for larger $n$ the same algebra
describes an ``$n$-dimensional cube'', even though we cannot draw one). Nudge the side
from $x$ to $x+h$. The square gains a thin strip of area $x\cdot h$ along each of its
$2$ outer sides; the cube gains a thin slab of volume $x^2\cdot h$ on each of its $3$
outer faces (Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-square-cube}). In general there are $n$ such pieces,
each of size $x^{n-1}$ times thickness $h$, for a gain of $n\cdot x^{n-1}\cdot h$. (The
corners and edges where pieces meet involve $h^2$ and smaller, which is negligible at
an instant.) Divide that gain by $h$ and you get $n x^{n-1}$. The $n$ in front counts
the faces that grow. We will see this same ``thicken each face'' picture again, in two
dimensions, when we prove the product rule.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Polynomials: linearity plus the power rule}

The power rule plus linearity (Section~\ref{ch11:linearity}) differentiate \emph{any}
polynomial on sight, term by term.

\begin{example}[A polynomial]\label{ch11:ex-poly}
Differentiate $p(x) = 4x^5 - 7x^3 + 2x^2 - 9x + 6$.

Work term by term. The exponent comes down, drops by one, and constants multiply through;
the lone constant $6$ dies:
\begin{align*}
  p'(x) &= 4\cdot 5x^4 - 7\cdot 3x^2 + 2\cdot 2x - 9\cdot 1 + 0 \\
        &= 20x^4 - 21x^2 + 4x - 9.
\end{align*}
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Rewrite, then differentiate]\label{ch11:ex-rewrite}
Differentiate $g(x) = \dfrac{3}{x^2} + 5\sqrt{x} - \dfrac{x^4}{2}$.

First rewrite every piece as a power of $x$ so the power rule applies:
\[
  g(x) = 3x^{-2} + 5x^{1/2} - \tfrac12 x^4 .
\]
Now differentiate each power:
\begin{align*}
  g'(x) &= 3(-2)x^{-3} + 5\cdot\tfrac12 x^{-1/2} - \tfrac12\cdot 4x^3 \\
        &= -6x^{-3} + \tfrac{5}{2}x^{-1/2} - 2x^3 \\
        &= -\frac{6}{x^3} + \frac{5}{2\sqrt{x}} - 2x^3 .
\end{align*}
Rewriting roots and fractions as powers of $x$ is the first step.
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Differentiate $h(x) = x^7 - 3x^4 + \dfrac{1}{x^3} - \sqrt[3]{x}$ by hand. (Hint: rewrite
the last two terms as $x^{-3}$ and $x^{1/3}$.) You should get
\[
  h'(x) = 7x^6 - 12x^3 - 3x^{-4} - \tfrac13 x^{-2/3}.
\]
Then check one value, say $x=1$, by comparing to a numerical slope
\[
  \frac{h(1.001)-h(0.999)}{0.002}.
\]
\end{labbox}

\section{The Product Rule}
\label{ch11:product-rule}

The natural guess, ``the derivative of a product is the product of the derivatives'',
is \emph{wrong}, and one example shows it.

\begin{trapbox}
Take $f(x)=x$ and $g(x)=x$, so $f(x)g(x)=x^2$. The correct derivative is $2x$. But
$f'(x)g'(x) = 1\cdot 1 = 1 \ne 2x$. The product of the derivatives is not the derivative
of the product.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{The picture: a growing rectangle}

A picture explains the correct rule. Imagine a rectangle whose width is
$f(x)$ and whose height is $g(x)$. Its area is the product $A(x)=f(x)g(x)$. Now nudge $x$
forward by a tiny $h$. The width grows by $\Delta f = f(x+h)-f(x)$ and the height grows by
$\Delta g = g(x+h)-g(x)$. How much new area appears?

The new area comes in three pieces (Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-product}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \emph{tall strip} along the right edge, of height $g$ and width $\Delta f$:
        area $g\,\Delta f$;
  \item a \emph{wide strip} along the top edge, of width $f$ and height $\Delta g$:
        area $f\,\Delta g$;
  \item a \emph{tiny corner} rectangle of dimensions $\Delta f$ by $\Delta g$:
        area $\Delta f\,\Delta g$.
\end{itemize}
So the change in area is
\[
  \Delta A = g\,\Delta f + f\,\Delta g + \Delta f\,\Delta g .
\]
Divide by $h$ to get the rate, and watch the corner term:
\[
  \frac{\Delta A}{h} = g\,\frac{\Delta f}{h} + f\,\frac{\Delta g}{h}
                       + \Delta f\cdot\frac{\Delta g}{h}.
\]
As $h\To 0$, $\Delta f/h \To f'$ and $\Delta g/h \To g'$. The first two terms become
$g f'$ and $f g'$. The corner term is $\Delta f\cdot(\Delta g/h)$; the factor $\Delta g/h$
tends to $g'$ (finite), but $\Delta f \To 0$, so the whole corner vanishes.
The corner is the product of two small quantities, so it is second-order small (like
$h^2$) and contributes nothing to the instantaneous rate of change.

\begin{theorem}[Product rule]\label{ch11:thm-product}
If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable, then so is $fg$, and
\[
  \ddx{f(x)\,g(x)} = f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
To turn the picture into a proof, \emph{add and subtract the same mixed term}. Start
from the difference quotient and insert $-f(x+h)g(x)+f(x+h)g(x)$, which equals zero:
\[
  \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x)}{h}
  = \frac{f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x+h)g(x) + f(x+h)g(x) - f(x)g(x)}{h}.
\]
Group the first two and last two terms, factoring out the common piece in each:
\[
  = f(x+h)\,\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h} \;+\; g(x)\,\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.
\]
Now take $h\To 0$ term by term. In the first term, $f(x+h)\To f(x)$ (because $f$, being
differentiable, is continuous) and $\dfrac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}\To g'(x)$. In the second term,
$g(x)$ is constant in $h$ and $\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\To f'(x)$. Hence the limit is
\[
  f(x)\,g'(x) + g(x)\,f'(x),
\]
which is the product rule.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
Why \emph{two} terms? Because a product depends on \emph{both} of its factors, and at any
instant both factors are changing at once. In the rectangle picture, area
is gained on two sides at the same time. The term $f'g$ is the contribution from the width
growing while the height holds; $fg'$ is the contribution from the height growing while
the width holds. You can think of it as ``hold one factor still, differentiate the other,
then swap and add.'' The reason you \emph{add} them (rather than, say, multiply) is that
the two growths are separate pieces of area, and their overlap, the corner
$\Delta f\,\Delta g$, is second-order small and drops out. This ``vary one factor at a
time and sum'' pattern is how the product rule extends to more factors: for three
functions $u$, $v$, $w$,
\[
  (uvw)' = u'vw + uv'w + uvw',
\]
one term per factor, each differentiating a single factor while freezing the others.
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[A first product]\label{ch11:ex-prod1}
Differentiate $y = (x^2+1)(x^3 - 4x)$.

Let $f = x^2+1$ and $g = x^3 - 4x$, so $f' = 2x$ and $g' = 3x^2 - 4$. Then
\[
  y' = f'g + fg' = (2x)(x^3-4x) + (x^2+1)(3x^2-4).
\]
Expand to simplify:
\begin{align*}
  y' &= (2x^4 - 8x^2) + (3x^4 - 4x^2 + 3x^2 - 4) \\
     &= 5x^4 - 9x^2 - 4 .
\end{align*}
\emph{Sanity check.} We could instead multiply out first:
$y = x^5 - 4x^3 + x^3 - 4x = x^5 - 3x^3 - 4x$, whose derivative is
$5x^4 - 9x^2 - 4$. Same answer, as it must be. Here expanding was about as fast; the
product rule is needed when you cannot multiply out, as with $x^2\sin x$ or $e^x\ln x$
(their derivatives come in the next chapter).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[When you cannot expand]\label{ch11:ex-prod2}
Differentiate $y = x^2 \sqrt{x+1}$. First we need $g'(x)$ for $g(x)=\sqrt{x+1}$. Put
$t=x+1$; then the difference quotient of $g$ is
\[
  \frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h} = \frac{\sqrt{t+h}-\sqrt{t}}{h},
\]
which is the difference quotient of $\sqrt{t}$. By
Example~\ref{ch11:ex-sqrt} its limit is $\frac{1}{2\sqrt t}$, so
$g'(x) = \tfrac12 (x+1)^{-1/2}$ (the chain rule in the next section gives the same
result). With $f=x^2$, $f'=2x$:
\[
  y' = 2x\,\sqrt{x+1} + x^2\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x+1}}
     = 2x\sqrt{x+1} + \frac{x^2}{2\sqrt{x+1}} .
\]
This product cannot be multiplied out into powers of $x$, so the product rule is needed.
\end{example}

\section{The Quotient Rule}
\label{ch11:quotient-rule}

Because division is the inverse of multiplication, we can derive the quotient rule directly from
the product rule. Suppose $q(x) = \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ (with $g(x)\ne 0$). The cleanest
derivation clears the denominator first.

\subsection{Deriving it from the product rule}

Write $f = q\cdot g$. Differentiate both sides with the product rule:
\[
  f' = q' g + q g'.
\]
Now solve for the unknown $q'$:
\begin{align*}
  q' &= \frac{f' - q g'}{g} && \text{(subtract $qg'$, divide by $g$)} \\
     &= \frac{f' - \dfrac{f}{g}\,g'}{g} && \text{(substitute $q=f/g$)} \\
     &= \frac{f' g - f g'}{g^2} && \text{(multiply top and bottom by $g$).}
\end{align*}

\begin{theorem}[Quotient rule]\label{ch11:thm-quotient}
If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable and $g(x)\ne 0$, then $f/g$ is differentiable and
\[
  \ddx{\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}}
  = \frac{f'(x)\,g(x) - f(x)\,g'(x)}{\big(g(x)\big)^2}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
The derivation above assumed $q$ was differentiable so that we could apply the
product rule to $f=qg$. To certify from scratch that $q$ is differentiable whenever
$g(x)\ne 0$, put the difference quotient over a common denominator and insert
$-f(x)g(x)+f(x)g(x)$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\frac{f(x+h)}{g(x+h)} - \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}}{h}
  &= \frac{\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\,g(x) - f(x)\,\dfrac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}{g(x+h)\,g(x)} \\
  &\xrightarrow{\;h\To 0\;} \frac{f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{\big(g(x)\big)^2},
\end{align*}
since $g(x+h)\To g(x)\ne 0$ by continuity of $g$. The shortcut via $f=qg$ is the easiest
way to \emph{remember} the rule; the limit identity is what \emph{certifies} it.
\end{remark}

\begin{whybox}
Where do the minus sign and the squared denominator come from?
Think of $f/g$ as $f$ times $1/g$. Increasing $f$ pushes the quotient \emph{up}: that is
the $+f' g$ part. Increasing $g$ pushes the quotient \emph{down} -- a bigger denominator
means a smaller fraction -- which is why $g'$ enters with a \emph{minus}
(Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-quotient}). The
$g^2$ in the denominator is the price of the reciprocal: as we computed back in
Example~\ref{ch11:ex-reciprocal}, $\ddx{1/g}$ carries a $1/g^2$, and the quotient rule
inherits it. Order matters in the numerator: it is $f'g - fg'$, ``derivative of top times
bottom, minus top times derivative of bottom.'' Swapping the two terms flips the sign and
gives the wrong answer.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
The numerator of the quotient rule is \emph{not} symmetric: $f'g - fg' \ne fg' - f'g$. A
mnemonic that prevents the classic sign error: ``\,low dee-high minus high dee-low, over
the square of what's below.\,'' Here ``high'' is the numerator $f$, ``low'' is the
denominator $g$, and ``dee'' means ``derivative of.'' So
\[
  \frac{\text{low}\cdot f' - \text{high}\cdot g'}{\text{low}^2}
  = \frac{g f' - f g'}{g^2},
\]
which is the same formula, written to keep the order straight.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{example}[A rational function]\label{ch11:ex-quot1}
Differentiate $q(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x^2 + 1}$.

Top $f = x^2-1$, $f' = 2x$. Bottom $g = x^2+1$, $g' = 2x$. Then
\[
  q'(x) = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}
        = \frac{2x(x^2+1) - (x^2-1)(2x)}{(x^2+1)^2}.
\]
Expand the numerator:
\[
  2x(x^2+1) - 2x(x^2-1) = 2x\big[(x^2+1)-(x^2-1)\big] = 2x\cdot 2 = 4x.
\]
So $q'(x) = \dfrac{4x}{(x^2+1)^2}$. The numerator simplified while the denominator
stays squared, which is typical of quotient-rule answers.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Deriving the reciprocal rule]\label{ch11:ex-quot2}
Take $f = 1$ (so $f' = 0$) in the quotient rule:
\[
  \ddx{\frac{1}{g}} = \frac{0\cdot g - 1\cdot g'}{g^2} = -\frac{g'}{g^2}.
\]
This \emph{reciprocal rule} is worth memorizing on its own. As a check, with $g=x$ it
gives $\ddx{1/x} = -1/x^2$, matching Example~\ref{ch11:ex-reciprocal}.
\end{example}

\begin{historybox}
The rules of this chapter were found between the 1660s and the 1680s by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, working separately. Both wanted a way to find tangent slopes and rates of change for any curve, instead of inventing a new geometric trick for each equation.

Newton, working at Cambridge during the plague years of 1665--1666, thought of calculus in terms of motion. For him a curve was the path of a moving point; a changing quantity was a \emph{fluent} (a quantity that flows over time, like the height of a cannonball), and its rate of change was its \emph{fluxion}, written $\dot{x}$. He found versions of the product and chain rules early, but kept his manuscripts private, sharing them only with a few English correspondents.

Leibniz, working in Paris and Hanover, thought of calculus as a set of rules for manipulating symbols. In October 1684 he published the first paper on the differential calculus, in the Leipzig journal \emph{Acta Eruditorum}, then the leading scholarly journal of continental Europe. Its title begins \emph{Nova Methodus pro Maximis et Minimis, itemque Tangentibus} (``A New Method for Maxima and Minima, and also Tangents''). In six pages it introduced the symbols $\dd x$ and $\dd y$ for tiny changes in $x$ and $y$, together with rules for working with them.

Leibniz treated the product $u \cdot v$ as the area of a rectangle with sides $u$ and $v$. Give each side an \emph{infinitesimal} increase $\dd u$ and $\dd v$: a change treated as nonzero while forming ratios, but small enough to be ignored afterwards. The new area is $(u + \dd u)(v + \dd v) = uv + u\,\dd v + v\,\dd u + \dd u\,\dd v$. Throwing away the corner $\dd u\,\dd v$ as far smaller than the two strips, he got $\dd(uv) = u\,\dd v + v\,\dd u$, the rectangle picture of Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-product}. For a quotient $q = u/v$ he wrote $u = qv$, applied the product rule to get $\dd u = q\,\dd v + v\,\dd q$, and solved for $\dd q = (\dd u - q\,\dd v)/v = (v\,\dd u - u\,\dd v)/v^2$, the same derivation we used.

Who deserved credit led to a bitter dispute between Newton's supporters at the Royal Society in London and mathematicians on the continent, which divided British and continental mathematics for generations. The method was also attacked on logical grounds: in his 1734 pamphlet \emph{The Analyst}, Bishop George Berkeley pointed out that treating increments as nonzero when forming ratios, and as zero when discarding terms like $\dd u\,\dd v$, is a contradiction.

The answer came in the 1820s, when Augustin-Louis Cauchy rebuilt calculus on \emph{limits}. In limit language the corner is not thrown away by decree: after dividing by $\Delta x$ it becomes $(\Delta u/ \Delta x)\,\Delta v$, which tends to $0$ because $\Delta v \To 0$. That is the argument we used for the product rule. Leibniz's picture was right; Cauchy supplied the proof.
\end{historybox}

\section{The Chain Rule}
\label{ch11:chain-rule}

The power, product, and quotient rules handle functions \emph{built by} arithmetic; the
chain rule handles functions built by \emph{composition} (Section~\ref{ch02:composition}) -- one function
fed into another, like $(3x+1)^{10}$, $\sqrt{x^2+1}$, $\sin(x^2)$, or $e^{-t/2}$. Most
functions used in science are compositions, so most derivatives you compute will use
this rule.

\subsection{The intuition: rates multiply}

Start with linked rates of change, before any formulas. Suppose a
car's fuel use depends on its speed, and its speed depends on time. Ask: how fast is fuel
consumption changing with time?

Suppose that for each $1$ km/h of extra speed the car burns $0.2$ extra litres per hour,
and that right now the car is accelerating at $3$ km/h per second. In Leibniz notation,
$\dd(\text{fuel})/\dd(\text{speed}) = 0.2$ and $\dd(\text{speed})/\dd t = 3$. Then per
second the speed rises by $3$ km/h, and each of
those raises fuel use by $0.2$ litre/h, so fuel use climbs by $0.2 \times 3 = 0.6$ litre/h
per second. \emph{The rates multiply.}

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Gears.} Picture two meshed gears. If the first turns $3$ times for each turn of
the input shaft, and the second turns $2$ times for each turn of the first, then the
second turns $3\times 2 = 6$ times per turn of the input. Link the gears and you
multiply the gear ratios. A derivative is a ``how-much-output-per-unit-input'' ratio,
and composing functions links them like gears, so you multiply the derivatives.
\end{intuitionbox}

In Leibniz notation the chain rule is easy to remember. If $y$ depends on $u$ and $u$
depends on $x$, then
\[
  \dv{y}{x} = \dv{y}{u}\cdot\dv{u}{x},
\]
and it \emph{looks} as if the $\dd u$'s cancel like fractions. This is one reason
Leibniz's notation has lasted three centuries. (It is not real fraction cancellation,
because $\dd y/\dd x$ is a limit, not a quotient of numbers; but as a memory aid it
gives the right answer here.)

\subsection{Stating it in function notation}

Let $y = f(g(x))$ -- the ``outer'' function $f$ applied to the ``inner'' function $g$.
Setting $u = g(x)$, we have $y = f(u)$, and the chain rule reads:

\begin{theorem}[Chain rule]\label{ch11:thm-chain}
If $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x)$, then the composition
$f\circ g$ (``$f$ after $g$'', the function $x\mapsto f(g(x))$) is differentiable at
$x$, and
\[
  \ddx{f\big(g(x)\big)} = f'\big(g(x)\big)\cdot g'(x).
\]
In words: \emph{derivative of the outside (evaluated at the inside) times derivative of
the inside.}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Why the naive proof almost works -- and how to fix it}

The natural proof writes the difference quotient and multiplies it by
$\Delta u/\Delta u = 1$. Let $u=g(x)$ and let $\Delta u = g(x+h)-g(x)$ be the change in the inner function.
Then the change in $y$ is $\Delta y = f(g(x+h)) - f(g(x)) = f(u+\Delta u) - f(u)$, and we
write
\[
  \frac{\Delta y}{h}
  = \frac{f(u+\Delta u) - f(u)}{h}
  = \underbrace{\frac{f(u+\Delta u) - f(u)}{\Delta u}}_{\To\, f'(u)}
    \cdot
    \underbrace{\frac{\Delta u}{h}}_{\To\, g'(x)}.
\]
As $h\To 0$ we have $\Delta u \To 0$ (because $g$ is continuous), so the first factor
tends to $f'(u)$ and the second to $g'(x)$, giving $f'(u)\,g'(x)$. This is almost
correct.

\begin{trapbox}
The catch: we divided by $\Delta u$, but $\Delta u$ might be \emph{zero} for some small
$h$ (imagine $g$ momentarily flat, as in the left panel of
Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-chain-gap}). Then the fraction $\dfrac{f(u+\Delta u)-f(u)}{\Delta
u}$ is $0/0$, undefined, and the cancellation is not allowed. This is a real gap, and a
complete proof must close it.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{A rigorous proof}

The standard repair is based on an idea of the Greek-German mathematician Constantin
Carath\'eodory (1873--1950). It replaces the troublesome difference quotient by a single
function that is well behaved \emph{even when $\Delta u = 0$}. Define the slope
function of $f$ at $u$:
\[
  \phi(t) =
  \begin{cases}
    \dfrac{f(t)-f(u)}{t-u}, & t\ne u,\\[1.0em]
    f'(u), & t = u.
  \end{cases}
\]
For $t\ne u$, $\phi(t)$ is the slope of the secant line from $(u,f(u))$ to $(t,f(t))$;
at $t=u$, where that slope would be $0/0$, the gap is filled with the tangent slope
$f'(u)$ (right panel of Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-chain-gap}).
Because $f$ is differentiable at $u$, the first branch tends to $f'(u)$ as $t\To u$, which
is exactly the value we assigned in the second branch -- so \emph{$\phi$ is continuous at
$u$}. Also, for \emph{every} $t$ (including $t=u$), multiplying back across gives the
identity
\[
  f(t)-f(u) = \phi(t)\,(t-u).
\]
When $t\ne u$ this is clearing the fraction; when $t=u$ both sides are $0$.
Writing the change as a product rather than a fraction avoids dividing by zero altogether.

\begin{proof}[Proof of the chain rule]
Put $t = g(x+h)$ and recall $u = g(x)$, so $t - u = \Delta u = g(x+h)-g(x)$. The identity
$f(t)-f(u)=\phi(t)(t-u)$ becomes
\[
  f\big(g(x+h)\big) - f\big(g(x)\big) = \phi\big(g(x+h)\big)\cdot\big(g(x+h)-g(x)\big).
\]
Divide by $h$ (this is safe: we never divide by $\Delta u$):
\[
  \frac{f(g(x+h)) - f(g(x))}{h}
  = \phi\big(g(x+h)\big)\cdot\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}.
\]
Now let $h\To 0$. Since $g$ is differentiable, it is continuous, so $g(x+h)\To g(x)=u$;
and since $\phi$ is continuous at $u$, $\phi(g(x+h))\To \phi(u) = f'(u)$. The second factor
tends to $g'(x)$ by definition. Therefore the product tends to
\[
  f'(u)\cdot g'(x) = f'\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x),
\]
which is the chain rule.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
Both proofs reach the same conclusion, ``the two rates
multiply'', and differ only in how they handle $\Delta u = 0$. The naive version divides by $\Delta u$ and
assumes it is nonzero; the Carath\'eodory version replaces ``difference quotient of $f$''
with the continuous function $\phi$, which equals that quotient when $\Delta u
\ne 0$ but is also defined (as $f'(u)$) when $\Delta u = 0$. The product
$f(t)-f(u)=\phi(t)(t-u)$ holds with no exceptions, so we never divide by something that
might vanish. The moral is general: \emph{when a proof stumbles over a possible $0/0$,
look for a way to write the same quantity as a product instead of a quotient.}
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Using it: differentiating from the outside in}

In practice the chain rule is a peeling procedure. Identify the outermost operation,
differentiate it while leaving the inside untouched, then multiply by the derivative of
the inside, and repeat on the inside if it is itself a composition.
Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-chain} shows why the two derivatives multiply.

\begin{example}[A power of a polynomial]\label{ch11:ex-chain1}
Differentiate $y = (3x+1)^{10}$.

Outer function: ``something to the $10$th,'' with derivative $10(\,\cdot\,)^9$. Inner
function: $3x+1$, with derivative $3$. So
\[
  y' = 10(3x+1)^{9}\cdot 3 = 30(3x+1)^{9}.
\]
Expanding $(3x+1)^{10}$ to differentiate term by term would mean eleven terms and a
binomial expansion. The chain rule does it in one line.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A square root]\label{ch11:ex-chain2}
Differentiate $y = \sqrt{x^2+1} = (x^2+1)^{1/2}$.

Outer: $(\,\cdot\,)^{1/2}$, derivative $\tfrac12(\,\cdot\,)^{-1/2}$. Inner: $x^2+1$,
derivative $2x$. Thus
\[
  y' = \tfrac12 (x^2+1)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}} .
\]
Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-chain-graph} shows this slope as an actual tangent line.
This finally justifies the square-root step we borrowed in
Example~\ref{ch11:ex-prod2}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The general power-chain shortcut]\label{ch11:ex-chain-genpow}
Combining the power rule with the chain rule gives a pattern you will use constantly:
\[
  \ddx{\big(g(x)\big)^n} = n\big(g(x)\big)^{n-1}\,g'(x).
\]
For instance $\ddx{(x^3 - 2x)^4} = 4(x^3-2x)^3(3x^2-2)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Two layers deep]\label{ch11:ex-chain3}
Differentiate $y = \big(\sqrt{x}+1\big)^{3}$ -- a composition inside a composition.

Work from the outside in. Outermost: $(\,\cdot\,)^3$, derivative $3(\,\cdot\,)^2$. Its inside
is $\sqrt{x}+1$, whose derivative is $\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ (the $+1$ contributes nothing).
So
\[
  y' = 3\big(\sqrt{x}+1\big)^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}
     = \frac{3\big(\sqrt{x}+1\big)^{2}}{2\sqrt{x}} .
\]
If there had been a third layer, we would have multiplied by one more inner
derivative. The chain rule composes as many times as you need: each nested layer
contributes one more factor to the product.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
The single most common chain-rule error is \emph{forgetting the inner derivative}. People
write $\ddx{(3x+1)^{10}} = 10(3x+1)^9$ and stop -- dropping the $\times 3$. A
check: if the inside is just $x$, the inner derivative is $1$ and you are back to the
plain power rule; whenever the inside is anything else, the factor $g'(x)$ changes the
answer.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Combining all the rules}

Most problems need several rules. The skill is recognizing the \emph{outermost}
structure first -- is the whole expression a sum, a product, a quotient, or a composition?
-- and applying the matching rule, then doing the same for each piece.

\begin{example}[Product and chain together]\label{ch11:ex-combo1}
Differentiate $y = x^2\,(2x+3)^5$.

The outermost structure is a \emph{product}: $f = x^2$ times $g = (2x+3)^5$
(Figure~\ref{ch11:fig-tree} shows the full structure). Product rule
first:
\[
  y' = f'g + fg' = 2x\,(2x+3)^5 + x^2\cdot\ddx{(2x+3)^5}.
\]
The remaining derivative $\ddx{(2x+3)^5}$ needs the \emph{chain} rule:
$5(2x+3)^4\cdot 2 = 10(2x+3)^4$. Substitute back:
\[
  y' = 2x(2x+3)^5 + 10x^2(2x+3)^4 .
\]
You can factor out the common $2x(2x+3)^4$ to tidy up:
\[
  y' = 2x(2x+3)^4\big[(2x+3) + 5x\big] = 2x(2x+3)^4(7x+3).
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Quotient and chain together]\label{ch11:ex-combo2}
Differentiate $y = \dfrac{(x+1)^3}{x^2+1}$.

Outermost structure: a \emph{quotient}. Top $f = (x+1)^3$ with $f' = 3(x+1)^2$ (chain
rule, inner derivative $1$); bottom $g = x^2+1$ with $g' = 2x$. Quotient rule:
\[
  y' = \frac{3(x+1)^2(x^2+1) - (x+1)^3(2x)}{(x^2+1)^2}.
\]
Factor $(x+1)^2$ from the numerator:
\[
  y' = \frac{(x+1)^2\big[3(x^2+1) - 2x(x+1)\big]}{(x^2+1)^2}
     = \frac{(x+1)^2\,(x^2 - 2x + 3)}{(x^2+1)^2}.
\]
(The bracket simplifies as $3x^2+3-2x^2-2x = x^2-2x+3$.)
\end{example}

\begin{walkbox}
On your next walk, try differentiating $(x^2+1)^3$ \emph{two ways} in your head:
once by the chain rule directly, and once by first imagining it as the product
$(x^2+1)(x^2+1)(x^2+1)$ and using the three-factor product rule. Convince yourself both
give $6x(x^2+1)^2$. Then ask: why \emph{must} they agree? (Because they are the same
function, and a function has only one derivative.)
\end{walkbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Backpropagation and the chain rule.} In machine learning, training a neural
network means minimizing a loss function (a number measuring how wrong the network's
answers are). The loss is a deep composition: layer after layer of functions stacked
on top of one another. \emph{Backpropagation}, the algorithm used to train neural
networks, is the chain rule applied mechanically through that stack. It multiplies
the local rates of change, layer by layer, to get the rate of change of the loss with
respect to each weight (the adjustable numbers inside the network). When you read that
a network ``propagates gradients backward,'' it is computing $f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$ over
and over: the rule proved in this chapter.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The chain rule reappears in calculus with several variables. When $y$ depends on
several intermediate quantities that each depend on $x$, the single product
$f'(g(x))g'(x)$ becomes a \emph{sum} of such products, one for each intermediate
quantity. This \emph{multivariable chain rule}, which is also what backpropagation
uses, comes back when we reach functions of several variables
(Chapter~\ref{ch33:gradient-directional-chain-rule}).
\end{connectionbox}

\section{A Summary You Can Trust}
\label{ch11:summary}

Every rule below was derived from the limit definition; the last column gives a
one-line reason for each.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Rule} & \textbf{Formula} & \textbf{Why} \\
\midrule
Constant & $\ddx{c} = 0$ & flat line, no change \\
Identity & $\ddx{x} = 1$ & change passes through \\
Power & $\ddx{x^n} = n x^{n-1}$ & the term in $h$ of $(x+h)^n$ survives \\
Constant multiple & $(kf)' = k f'$ & vertical stretch scales all slopes \\
Sum & $(f\pm g)' = f' \pm g'$ & rates add \\
Product & $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$ & rectangle grows on two sides \\
Quotient & $\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$ & top up, bottom down \\
Chain & $\big(f(g(x))\big)' = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$ & rates multiply \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Two basic derivatives ($\ddx{c}=0$ and $\ddx{x}=1$), one principle (linearity), and
three combination rules (product, quotient, chain) give the derivative of \emph{every}
function you can build from powers of $x$ using the four arithmetic operations and
composition. None of the rules is arbitrary: each follows from what ``rate of change''
\emph{means} once you take the limit seriously.
\end{aestheticbox}

\subsection{What the Rules Do Not Tell You}
\label{ch11:what-unlocked}

\begin{connectionbox}
Before these rules, finding the slope of a curve needed a fresh argument for almost every new equation. With them, any function built from powers, sums, products, quotients, and compositions can be differentiated on paper without going back to the limit definition.

But a derivative gives only \emph{local} information, meaning information about a single point and its immediate surroundings. The number $f'(x_0)$ is the slope of the tangent line at the one point $x_0$; by itself it says nothing about the rest of the graph. It does not find the highest and lowest points of a curve, tell you whether the function keeps increasing over a whole interval, or say how quickly the curve bends away from its tangent line as you move away from $x_0$.

Turning these local slopes into a picture of the whole graph needs more tools:
derivatives of exponentials, logarithms, and trigonometric functions (Chapter~12),
curves given by an equation in $x$ and $y$ rather than a formula $y=f(x)$
(Chapter~13), and then the Mean Value Theorem (which turns slope information into facts
about whole intervals) and the search for highest and lowest points (Chapters~15--16).
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch11:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental warm-ups]
Answer each in your head in ten seconds or less.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What is $\ddx{e^3}$? (Careful: is $e^3$ changing when $x$ changes?)
  \item What is $\ddx{\dfrac{1}{x^4}}$?
  \item A student claims that the derivative of $(2x+1)^3$ is $3(2x+1)^2$. What essential factor was dropped?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Power-rule drill]
Differentiate each, rewriting roots and reciprocals as powers first.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $f(x) = 6x^4 - 2x^3 + x - 11$.
  \item $g(x) = \dfrac{5}{x^3} - \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{x}} + 7$.
  \item $h(x) = \sqrt[3]{x^2} + 4x^{-5}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Product rule]
Differentiate using the product rule, then (where feasible) check by expanding first.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $y = (2x^2 - 1)(x^3 + x)$.
  \item $y = x^3\sqrt{x+4}$.
  \item $y = (x^2+1)(x^2+2)(x^2+3)$ \;(use the three-factor product rule).
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Quotient rule]
Differentiate and simplify.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $y = \dfrac{x}{x^2+1}$.
  \item $y = \dfrac{2x-3}{4x+5}$.
  \item $y = \dfrac{x^2}{(x-1)^2}$ \;(quotient rule, with the chain rule on the bottom).
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Chain rule]
Differentiate. Work from the outside in, and do not drop the inner derivative.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $y = (5x^2 - 3)^{7}$.
  \item $y = \sqrt{4 - x^2}$.
  \item $y = \big((x+1)^2 + 1\big)^{3}$ \;(two layers).
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[All rules braided together]
Differentiate. First identify the outermost structure (sum, product, quotient, or
composition).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $y = x^2\sqrt{x^2+1}$.
  \item $y = \dfrac{(3x-1)^4}{x^2+1}$.
  \item $y = \big(x + \sqrt{x}\big)^5$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual: spot the error]
A student writes $\ddx{\dfrac{x^2}{x+1}} = \dfrac{2x}{1} = 2x$ by ``differentiating top and
bottom separately.'' Explain in one or two sentences why this is wrong, name the rule that
should have been used, and give the correct derivative.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual: why two terms?]
In your own words, explain why $\ddx{fg}$ has \emph{two} terms rather than one, using the
growing-rectangle picture. What does the third piece (the corner $\Delta f\,\Delta g$)
represent, and why does it not appear in the final formula?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Reconstruct a rule]
Without looking back, derive the quotient rule from the product rule by writing
$f = q\,g$ where $q = f/g$, differentiating, and solving for $q'$. Then check your result
by computing $\ddx{1/x}$ two ways: from the quotient rule and from the limit definition.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: the power rule for negative integers]
Assume the power rule holds for positive integers and that the reciprocal rule holds. Let
$n$ be a positive integer and set $x^{-n} = 1/x^{n}$. Use the reciprocal rule
$\ddx{1/g} = -g'/g^2$ to prove
\[
  \ddx{x^{-n}} = -n\,x^{-n-1},
\]
thereby extending the power rule to negative integer exponents.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: a three-factor product rule]
Prove that $(uvw)' = u'vw + uv'w + uvw'$ by treating $uvw$ as the product of $u$ with the
single function $(vw)$ and applying the two-factor product rule twice. Then state, without
proof, the pattern for a product of $n$ factors.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: the chain rule cannot be skipped]
Let $F(x) = f(g(x))$ with $f(u) = u^2$ and $g(x) = x^2 + 1$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute $F'(x)$ by the chain rule.
  \item Compute $F'(x)$ instead by first expanding $F(x) = (x^2+1)^2 = x^4 + 2x^2 + 1$ and
        differentiating directly.
  \item Verify the answers agree, and explain what would have gone wrong had you mistakenly
        written $F'(x) = f'(g(x)) = 2(x^2+1)$ without the factor $g'(x)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A limit that hides a derivative]
Recognize each limit as a derivative $f'(a)$ for a suitable $f$ and $a$, and evaluate it
using the rules of this chapter rather than the limit directly.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\displaystyle\lim_{h\To 0}\frac{(2+h)^5 - 2^5}{h}$.
  \item $\displaystyle\lim_{h\To 0}\frac{\sqrt{9+h} - 3}{h}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
