\chapter{Maxima, Minima, and the Shape of a Graph}

How do you \emph{know} what a graph looks like? The usual method is to plot a
few points, connect the dots, and trust that the curve behaves between them. But
that is guessing. Between two sample points a function can dive down and come
back up, or bend sharply in a way your dots never show. To draw a graph
correctly --- to know where it rises, where it falls, where it turns around,
where it bulges up and where it sags down --- you need more than a handful of
values. You need the derivative, and the derivative of the derivative.

The first derivative $f'$ tells you the \emph{direction} the curve is heading,
uphill or downhill. The second derivative $f''$ tells you how that direction is
\emph{changing}: whether the curve bends upward or downward. Together they
determine the shape of a graph and locate its highest and lowest points.
Finding highest and lowest points is what \emph{optimization} means: the cheapest
design, the strongest beam, the fastest route, the maximum profit, the least
error. It all starts from one observation: \emph{at the top of a hill, the
ground is level}.

Every test in this chapter rests on the Mean Value Theorem (MVT) from the
previous chapter, which turns facts about $f'$ into facts about $f$. When we
claim ``$f'>0$ means $f$ is increasing,'' or that a sign change in $f'$ means a
genuine peak, the MVT is the reason it is true.

\section{The top of the hill is level}

Stand at the very top of a smooth hill. The ground right at your feet is flat.
Farther away the hill falls off on both sides, but at the summit itself it is
level. If the ground at the summit were tilted even slightly uphill in some
direction, you could walk that way and go higher, so you were not at the
summit after all. The only way to be at the very top is for every direction to be
level or downhill, and for a smooth curve in one variable that means the tangent
line is horizontal.

\begin{intuitionbox}
At a peak or a valley of a smooth graph, the tangent line is horizontal: the
slope is zero. To the left of a
peak the function is climbing ($f'>0$); to the right it is falling ($f'<0$). A
continuous slope cannot jump from positive to negative without passing through
zero. So right at the top, $f'=0$. The summit is where ``going up'' turns into
``going down,'' and the turning point is level.
\end{intuitionbox}

``Highest point'' needs a precise meaning: highest compared to \emph{what}?
Compared to the entire domain, or only to nearby points?

\begin{definition}[Local and global extrema]\label{ch16:def-extrema}
Let $f$ be defined on a set $D$ and let $c \in D$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $f$ has a \emph{local maximum} at $c$ if $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for all $x$ in some
open interval around $c$ on which $f$ is defined (a local minimum if
$f(c)\le f(x)$ nearby).
\item $f$ has a \emph{global} (or \emph{absolute}) \emph{maximum} at $c$ if
$f(c)\ge f(x)$ for \emph{all} $x \in D$ (global minimum if $f(c)\le f(x)$ for all
$x\in D$).
\end{itemize}
A \emph{local extremum} (plural: \emph{extrema}, from the Latin for outermost) is either a local max or a local min.
\end{definition}

A local maximum is a peak relative to its immediate surroundings --- the top of
\emph{a} hill, even if a taller mountain stands a mile away. A global maximum is
the highest point across the entire domain of the function. A global extremum
that is not at an end of the domain is automatically a local one (being highest
overall makes you highest locally), but not the reverse.
Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-extrema} shows a local maximum that is not a global one:
the curve later climbs higher.

\subsection{Fermat's theorem: where to look}

The flatness-at-the-summit idea has a name and a proof. It is one of the oldest
results in the subject, found by Pierre de Fermat decades before Newton and
Leibniz had a systematic calculus.

\begin{theorem}[Fermat's theorem on extrema]\label{ch16:thm-fermat}
If $f$ has a local extremum at $c$, and $f$ is differentiable at $c$, then
$f'(c)=0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
The derivative $f'(c)$ is the limit of the slopes of secant lines through
$\big(c,f(c)\big)$ and a nearby point $\big(c+h,f(c+h)\big)$. At a peak, no
nearby point is higher. So a secant to a point on the right goes downhill (slope
$\le 0$), and a secant to a point on the left goes uphill (slope $\ge 0$), as
Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-fermat-secants} shows. If $f$ is differentiable at $c$,
both families of slopes approach the same number $f'(c)$, which must therefore be
both $\le 0$ and $\ge 0$. The only such number is $0$. If $f$ is \emph{not}
differentiable at $c$, the two sides may approach different numbers, and the
argument breaks down; that is why differentiability is in the hypothesis.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $f$ has a local maximum at $c$ (the minimum case is identical with
inequalities flipped). Then there is an interval around $c$ on which
$f(x)\le f(c)$, i.e.\ $f(c+h)-f(c)\le 0$ for all small $h$.

For $h>0$,
\[
\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h} \le 0
\qquad\text{(nonpositive over positive)},
\]
so the right-hand limit satisfies
\[
f'(c)=\lim_{h\to 0^+}\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}\le 0.
\]

For $h<0$,
\[
\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h} \ge 0
\qquad\text{(nonpositive over negative)},
\]
so the left-hand limit satisfies
\[
f'(c)=\lim_{h\to 0^-}\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}\ge 0.
\]

Because $f$ is differentiable at $c$, these two one-sided limits are equal and both
equal $f'(c)$. The only way to have $f'(c)\le 0$ and $f'(c)\ge 0$ at once is
$f'(c)=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{historybox}
The idea that extrema occur where the rate of change is zero predates both Newton and Leibniz. In 1636, Pierre de Fermat circulated a short manuscript, \emph{Methodus ad disquirendam maximam et minimam} (``Method for finding the maximum and minimum''), presenting his method of \emph{adequality} (from the Latin for ``approximate equality''). Fermat was inspired by a remark of the ancient Greek geometer Pappus of Alexandria: at a maximum, a slight nudge in the input produces almost no change in the output. Fermat replaced $x$ with $x + e$ for a small nudge $e$, set $f(x+e)$ ``adequal'' to $f(x)$, divided through by $e$, and then threw away all terms still containing $e$. In modern language, Fermat was solving $\lim_{e\to 0}\frac{f(x+e)-f(x)}{e} = 0$, finding peaks and troughs as the points where the rate of change vanishes, decades before calculus was invented.

The procedure drew sharp criticism. In 1638, Ren\'e Descartes demanded to know how Fermat could treat $e$ as nonzero so that he could divide by it, and then discard it as if it were zero. Without a definition of a limit, Fermat could only reply that the method always produced the correct geometric answers.

The geometry of bending belongs to Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. In his 1684 paper and later articles in the journal \emph{Acta Eruditorum}, Leibniz studied what he called \emph{puncta flexus contrarii}: inflection points, where a curve switches between bending upward and bending downward. He saw that just as the first differential $\dd y$ (a small change in $y$) determines the slope, the second differential $\dd^2 y$ (the change in that change) determines the bending. At an inflection point $\dd^2 y = 0$, and the tangent line crosses the curve instead of touching it from one side.

Fermat and Leibniz had the geometric picture right but no limit concept to justify throwing away the vanishing terms. In the nineteenth century, Cauchy and Weierstrass supplied it with the $\eps$-$\delta$ limit and a rigorous proof of the Mean Value Theorem, which showed that Fermat's flat points and Leibniz's bending changes follow exactly from the definitions. For functions of one variable, the tests in this chapter settle the question in almost every practical case. Finding the lowest point of a function of millions of variables, such as the error measure minimized when training a large neural network, is a different matter: in general no fast method is known, and the geometry of such functions is an active research area.
\end{historybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Fermat's theorem is a one-way street, and reading it backwards is one of the most
common mistakes in this subject. $f'(c)=0$ does \emph{not} guarantee an
extremum. The graph of $f(x)=x^3$ has $f'(0)=0$, yet $0$ is neither a max nor a
min --- the curve flattens for an instant and keeps right on rising. A zero
derivative is a \emph{candidate}, a place worth checking, nothing more.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Critical points: candidate extrema}

Fermat's theorem says smooth extrema hide where $f'=0$. But there is a second way
a function can turn around: at a \emph{corner}, where the derivative does not
exist at all. The function $f(x)=\abs{x}$ has its minimum at $0$, and $f'(0)$
does not exist: the slope is $-1$ on the left and $+1$ on the right, with
no single tangent. Fermat's theorem said nothing here, because its hypothesis
(``differentiable at $c$'') failed. So our search must include both kinds of points.

\begin{definition}[Critical point]\label{ch16:def-critical}
A \emph{critical point} of $f$ is a number $c$ in the domain of $f$ at which either
$f'(c)=0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist. The value $f(c)$ is a \emph{critical value}.
\end{definition}

\begin{focusbox}
Combining Fermat's theorem with the corner case gives the rule behind the
whole search for extrema:
\[
\boxed{\text{Every local extremum of } f \text{ occurs at a critical point.}}
\]
This does not say critical points are extrema; many are not. It says you never
have to search the whole real line. Peaks and valleys can \emph{only} sit at
places where $f'$ is zero or undefined, and for the functions met in practice
there are only a few such places. Find them, and you have a short list of
candidates to check.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}[Listing critical points]\label{ch16:ex-list-crit}
Find the critical points of $f(x)=x^3-3x^2-9x+5$.

\smallskip
Differentiate: $f'(x)=3x^2-6x-9=3(x^2-2x-3)=3(x-3)(x+1)$. This is a polynomial,
defined everywhere, so the only critical points come from $f'=0$:
\[
3(x-3)(x+1)=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x=3 \ \text{ or } \ x=-1.
\]
Two suspects: $x=-1$ and $x=3$. We do not yet know which (if either) is a max or a
min --- that is the next section's job.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A critical point from a missing derivative]\label{ch16:ex-cusp}
Find the critical points of $g(x)=x^{2/3}$.

\smallskip
Here $g'(x)=\dfrac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}=\dfrac{2}{3\,x^{1/3}}$. There is no $x$ making
this zero (the numerator is a constant $2/3$). But at $x=0$ the formula blows up:
$g'(0)$ does not exist. Yet $0$ is in the domain of $g$, and $g(0)=0$ while
$g(x)>0$ for every other $x$. So $x=0$ is a critical point --- in fact a global
minimum --- found \emph{only} because we remembered to check where the derivative
fails to exist. The graph has a \emph{cusp} at the origin: a sharp point,
pointing down, where two branches meet with a vertical tangent line.
Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-corner-cusp} compares it with the corner of $\abs{x}$.
\end{example}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Suppose a function $f$ is continuous everywhere, climbs steadily on $(-\infty, 2)$, and falls steadily on $(2, \infty)$. Does $x=2$ have to be a local maximum? Yes: by definition, $f(2) \ge f(x)$ for all nearby $x$. But does $f'(2)$ have to equal $0$? Not at all: the crest could be a sharp corner like $f(x) = 3 - |x-2|$, where the two one-sided slopes disagree and the derivative does not exist. That is why Fermat's equation $f'=0$ is only half the net: critical points must include every point where $f'$ fails to exist.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{The first-derivative test: reading the sign of the slope}

We have a list of candidate points. Now we classify them. The sign of $f'$ tells
you which way the function is going: positive slope, going up; negative slope,
going down. So to decide whether a critical point is a peak, a valley, or
neither, watch what the slope does as you walk through it.

\begin{whybox}
Why does the sign of $f'$ control whether $f$ rises or falls? This is the
Monotonicity Theorem (Theorem~\ref{ch15:thm-mono} in the preceding chapter),
which rests on the Mean Value Theorem. For any two points $a<b$ in an interval,
the MVT guarantees a point $\xi$ (the Greek letter xi) in $(a,b)$ satisfying
\[
f(b)-f(a)=f'(\xi)\,(b-a).
\]
Because $b>a$, the factor $(b-a)$ is positive, so $f(b)-f(a)$ has the same sign
as the slope $f'(\xi)$.
If $f'>0$ across the entire interval, then $f'(\xi)>0$ for every pair of points,
forcing $f(b)>f(a)$ throughout: $f$ is strictly increasing. If $f'<0$ throughout,
$f(b)<f(a)$, and $f$ is strictly decreasing. The first-derivative test applies
this fact on each side of a critical point.
\end{whybox}

\begin{theorem}[First-derivative test]\label{ch16:thm-fdt}
Suppose $f$ is continuous at a critical point $c$ and differentiable on an open
interval around $c$ (except possibly at $c$ itself). Consider the sign of $f'$ just
to the left and just to the right of $c$:
\begin{itemize}
\item if $f'$ changes from $+$ to $-$ at $c$, then $f$ has a \emph{local maximum}
at $c$;
\item if $f'$ changes from $-$ to $+$ at $c$, then $f$ has a \emph{local minimum}
at $c$;
\item if $f'$ does \emph{not} change sign at $c$ (stays $+$ or stays $-$), then $c$
is \emph{not} a local extremum.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $f'>0$ on an interval $(a,c)$ to the left of $c$ and $f'<0$ on an interval
$(c,b)$ to the right. By the Monotonicity Theorem, $f$ is increasing on $(a,c)$ and
decreasing on $(c,b)$; by continuity at $c$ these extend to $f$ increasing on
$(a,c]$ and decreasing on $[c,b)$. So for any $x$ in $(a,c)$ we have $f(x)<f(c)$,
and for any $x$ in $(c,b)$ we have $f(x)<f(c)$. Thus $f(c)\ge f(x)$ for all $x$ in
$(a,b)$: a local maximum. The minimum case reverses the inequalities. If $f'$ keeps
the same sign on both sides, $f$ is increasing straight through $c$ (or
decreasing straight through it), so $c$ cannot be a turning point.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Picture a ball rolling along the graph. Where $f'>0$ the ground slopes up to the
right, so a ball would roll \emph{left}, downhill. Where $f'<0$ the ground slopes
down to the right, so the ball rolls \emph{right}. A local \emph{minimum} is a spot
both neighbors roll \emph{toward} (down from the left, down from the right) --- a
basin, $-$ then $+$. A local \emph{maximum} is a spot both neighbors roll
\emph{away} from --- a ridge, $+$ then $-$. And if the ball rolls the same
direction on both sides, it passes through; there is no resting place.
\end{intuitionbox}

The practical method is a \emph{sign chart}: a table recording the sign of $f'$
on each piece of the number line. Mark the critical points on a number
line; they cut it into intervals. On each interval, $f'$ has a constant sign:
it can change sign only where it is zero or undefined, that is, at a critical
point.
Test one convenient point per interval and record $+$ or $-$.
Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-sign-chart} shows the sign chart for the next example drawn
on a number line.

\begin{example}[First-derivative test in full]\label{ch16:ex-fdt}
Classify the critical points of $f(x)=x^3-3x^2-9x+5$ from
Example~\ref{ch16:ex-list-crit}.

\smallskip
We had $f'(x)=3(x-3)(x+1)$, with critical points $x=-1$ and $x=3$. These split the
line into three intervals. Pick a test point in each and read the sign of $f'$ from
the factored form:
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
\text{interval} & (-\infty,-1) & (-1,3) & (3,\infty)\\\hline
\text{test } x & -2 & 0 & 4\\
(x-3) & - & - & +\\
(x+1) & - & + & +\\
f'=3(x-3)(x+1) & + & - & +\\
f & \nearrow & \searrow & \nearrow
\end{array}
\]
At $x=-1$, $f'$ goes $+\to-$: a \textbf{local maximum}, value
$f(-1)=-1-3+9+5=10$. At $x=3$, $f'$ goes $-\to+$: a \textbf{local minimum}, value
$f(3)=27-27-27+5=-22$. The function climbs to a peak at $(-1,10)$, slides down into
a valley at $(3,-22)$, then climbs forever.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A critical point that is not an extremum]\label{ch16:ex-no-extremum}
Classify the critical point of $f(x)=x^3$.

\smallskip
$f'(x)=3x^2$, zero only at $x=0$. But $3x^2\ge 0$ everywhere: $f'>0$ for $x<0$
\emph{and} $f'>0$ for $x>0$. The slope does not change sign --- it dips to zero and
bounces back up. By the first-derivative test, $x=0$ is \emph{not} an extremum. The
curve flattens for one instant and keeps climbing. This is the trap from earlier
made concrete: $f'(0)=0$ did not deliver an extremum.
\end{example}

\section{Concavity: which way the curve bends}

Knowing where a graph rises and falls still leaves its \emph{shape} ambiguous. A
curve can climb from $A$ to $B$ in two different ways: bulging up like the top
of a dome, or sagging down like the inside of a bowl
(Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-two-climbs}). Both go up, but they bend in opposite
directions. To capture the bending, we ask not how $f$ is changing, but how
$f'$ is changing --- and the rate of change of $f'$ is $f''$.

\begin{definition}[Concavity]\label{ch16:def-concave}
On an interval where $f$ is differentiable:
\begin{itemize}
\item $f$ is \emph{concave up} if $f'$ is increasing on the interval (the slope
gets ever larger --- more positive or less negative as you move right);
\item $f$ is \emph{concave down} if $f'$ is decreasing on the interval.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Apply the Monotonicity Theorem to the function $f'$ itself: $f'$ is increasing
exactly where \emph{its} derivative $f''$ is positive. That gives the working test.

\begin{focusbox}
\[
f''>0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{concave up (cup-shaped, }\smile\text{)},
\qquad
f''<0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{concave down (cap-shaped, }\frown\text{)}.
\]
A memory hook: $f''>0$ holds \emph{water}, like a cup. $f''<0$ spills it, like a
frown.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
There are three ways to picture concavity; Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-concavity}
shows the second and third.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The slope is turning.} Concave up means the tangent line is rotating
counterclockwise as you move right --- the slope keeps increasing. Concave down
means it rotates clockwise.
\item \textbf{The curve vs.\ its tangent.} A concave-up curve lies \emph{above}
each of its tangent lines (the tangent underestimates it). A concave-down curve
lies \emph{below} its tangents. This is why the linear approximation from the
linear-approximation chapter always undershoots a concave-up function and
overshoots a concave-down one.
\item \textbf{The chord.} A \emph{chord} is the straight line segment joining two
points of the curve. A concave-up curve lies \emph{below} its chords (think of the
bottom of a valley); a concave-down curve lies above its chords.
\end{enumerate}
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{whybox}
Why does a concave-up curve ($f''>0$) lie above its tangent lines?
Fix a point $a$, and let
\[
T(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)
\]
be the tangent line at $a$. Define the gap $g(x)=f(x)-T(x)$, measuring how far the
curve sits above the tangent line. Note that $g(a)=0$ and $g'(x)=f'(x)-f'(a)$, so
$g'(a)=0$.

When $f''>0$ on the interval, $f'$ is strictly increasing:
\begin{itemize}
\item For $x>a$: $f'(x)>f'(a)$, so $g'(x)>0$. Since $g(a)=0$ and $g$ is increasing
to the right of $a$, we have $g(x)>0$.
\item For $x<a$: $f'(x)<f'(a)$, so $g'(x)<0$. Since $g$ is decreasing toward $a$
where $g(a)=0$, moving leftward from $a$ means $g(x)>0$.
\end{itemize}
On either side of $a$, $g(x)>0$: the curve sits strictly above its tangent line
except at the point of tangency itself. The argument uses the Mean Value Theorem
twice: first to show that $f''>0$ makes $f'$ increase,
then to show that the sign of $g'$ makes $g$ increase or decrease.
\end{whybox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Could a curve be concave up everywhere ($f''>0$ on all of $\R$) and still possess a local maximum? Picture what that would demand: at a local maximum the tangent line is horizontal, and concave up forces the curve to lie \emph{above} that tangent line on both sides. But lying above a horizontal tangent means climbing even higher, which destroys the peak. So a function with $f''>0$ everywhere can never have a local maximum.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Inflection points: where the bend reverses}

A curve does not have to keep the same concavity forever. Many curves that
model growth, such as the S-shaped curve below, change their bending partway
through. An inflection point is where that switch happens.

\begin{definition}[Inflection point]\label{ch16:def-inflection}
A point $\big(c,f(c)\big)$ on the graph of a continuous function is an
\emph{inflection point} if the concavity of $f$ \emph{changes} there --- from up to
down, or down to up.
\end{definition}

Since concavity is governed by the sign of $f''$, an inflection point is a place
where $f''$ changes sign. And just as $f'$ can change sign only where it is zero
or undefined,
$f''$ can only change sign where $f''=0$ or $f''$ fails to exist. Those are the
\emph{candidate} inflection points --- and, exactly as with Fermat's theorem,
each candidate still has to be tested.

\begin{trapbox}
$f''(c)=0$ does \emph{not} by itself make $\big(c,f(c)\big)$ an inflection point.
You must check that $f''$ \emph{changes sign}. Classic counterexample:
$f(x)=x^4$ has $f''(x)=12x^2$, which is zero at $x=0$ but $\ge 0$ on both sides ---
no sign change, no inflection. The curve is concave up throughout; it merely
flattens momentarily at the bottom. Always confirm the sign flip, never read it off
from $f''=0$ alone.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{example}[The S-curve and its inflection]\label{ch16:ex-logistic}
Consider $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$, the logistic curve, used in
population models, neural networks, and other models of growth that levels off
at a ceiling. Find
where it is concave up, concave down, and its inflection point.

\smallskip
Write $s=f(x)=(1+e^{-x})^{-1}$, noting that $e^{-x} = \frac{1-s}{s}$. By the chain rule,
\[
f'(x) = -(1+e^{-x})^{-2}(-e^{-x}) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^2}
      = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}\cdot\frac{e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}} = s(1-s).
\]
Differentiating $f'(x) = s - s^2$ with respect to $x$ and substituting $s' = s(1-s)$
once more gives
\[
f''(x) = s'(1-2s) = s(1-s)(1-2s).
\]
Now $s$ ranges strictly between $0$ and $1$, so $s>0$ and $(1-s)>0$ always; the
sign of $f''$ is determined purely by the factor $(1-2s)$. That is positive when
$s<\tfrac12$ and negative when $s>\tfrac12$. And $s=\tfrac12$ happens exactly at
$x=0$. So:
\[
f''>0 \text{ for } x<0 \ (\text{concave up}),\qquad
f''<0 \text{ for } x>0 \ (\text{concave down}).
\]
The concavity flips at $x=0$: an inflection point at $\big(0,\tfrac12\big)$
(Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-logistic}). This
is the ``tipping point'' of the S-curve: the moment of fastest growth,
where the curve stops accelerating and begins to level off toward its ceiling
$y=1$.
\end{example}

\begin{applicationbox}
In applications, the inflection point is often \emph{the} quantity of
interest. If the total number of cases in an epidemic follows a logistic curve,
the inflection point is the day new cases per day \emph{peak}. After it, the
total is still growing, but more slowly each day. In a product-adoption curve it
is the moment when sales per day are highest. In both cases
$f$ (the cumulative total) is still increasing the whole time; it is $f''$ changing
sign that signals the turn (Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-logistic-rate}). The first derivative tells you how fast things are
getting worse; the second derivative tells you whether that rate is itself rising
or falling, and its sign change is the first good news.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{happeningbox}
What is the second derivative measuring at an inflection point?
Think of $f$ as position, so $f'$ is velocity and $f''$ is acceleration. Concave up
means accelerating (speeding up in the positive direction); concave down means
decelerating. The inflection point is the instant the acceleration passes through
zero and reverses --- the moment you stop pressing the gas and start pressing the
brake. The car is still moving forward (maybe even still gaining ground for a
while, because velocity has not yet hit zero), but the \emph{trend has turned}.
So $f''$ changing sign does not mean the motion stops; it means the motion
switches from speeding up to slowing down. A peak in $f$ is where $f'=0$; an
inflection in $f$ is where $f'$ is at its own peak or valley. Inflection points of
$f$ are the extrema of $f'$.
\end{happeningbox}

\section{The second-derivative test: curvature as a shortcut}

The first-derivative test always works, but it asks you to examine $f'$ on
\emph{both} sides of a critical point. When $f''$ is easy to compute, there is a
faster route that looks at a single point. The idea is geometric:
at a critical point the tangent is horizontal, so the question ``peak or valley?''
becomes purely a question of \emph{which way the curve bends there}. Bends up
($\smile$) over a horizontal tangent: a valley. Bends down ($\frown$): a peak.

\begin{theorem}[Second-derivative test]\label{ch16:thm-sdt}
Suppose $f'(c)=0$ and $f''(c)$ exists.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $f''(c)>0$, then $f$ has a \emph{local minimum} at $c$.
\item If $f''(c)<0$, then $f$ has a \emph{local maximum} at $c$.
\item If $f''(c)=0$, the test is \emph{inconclusive} --- $c$ could be a max, a min,
or neither.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
Why the test works: first a geometric argument for the case where $f''$ is
continuous, then an argument that needs less. If $f''$ is continuous and $f''(c)>0$, then $f''(x)>0$ throughout a
small interval around $c$, so the curve is concave up there and lies \emph{above} its
tangent line at $c$. Because $f'(c)=0$, that tangent line is the horizontal line
$y=f(c)$, giving $f(x)\ge f(c)$ nearby: a local minimum. Likewise $f''(c)<0$ bends the
curve below its horizontal tangent, giving a local maximum.

Even without assuming $f''$ exists anywhere except at $c$ itself, the limit
definition proves the theorem directly. For $f''(c)>0$,
\[
f''(c)=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f'(x)-f'(c)}{x-c}>0,
\]
and since $f'(c)=0$ this forces $f'(x)/(x-c)>0$ for all $x\ne c$ sufficiently
close to $c$. Thus $f'(x)$ and
$(x-c)$ share the same sign: $f'(x)<0$ just left of $c$ and $f'(x)>0$ just right of
$c$. By the first-derivative test on those two half-intervals, $c$ is a local minimum.
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[Second-derivative test, fast]\label{ch16:ex-sdt}
Reclassify the critical points of $f(x)=x^3-3x^2-9x+5$ using $f''$.

\smallskip
We have $f'(x)=3x^2-6x-9$, so $f''(x)=6x-6=6(x-1)$. Evaluate at the two critical
points:
\[
\begin{aligned}
f''(-1)&=6(-1-1)=-12<0 &&\Rightarrow\ \text{local max at }x=-1,\\
f''(3)&=6(3-1)=12>0 &&\Rightarrow\ \text{local min at }x=3.
\end{aligned}
\]
Same verdict as the sign chart in Example~\ref{ch16:ex-fdt}, with no intervals to
test: two evaluations and done. (Also, $f''=0$ at $x=1$, with
$f''$ changing sign there, so $x=1$ is an inflection point. It lies midway between
the peak and the valley, as it does for every cubic with a peak and a valley.)
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
When $f''(c)=0$, the second-derivative test tells you \emph{nothing} --- do not
conclude ``not an extremum.'' Compare three functions at $x=0$, all with $f'(0)=0$
and $f''(0)=0$ (Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-sdt-inconclusive}):
\[
x^4 \ (\text{min}),\qquad -x^4 \ (\text{max}),\qquad x^3 \ (\text{neither}).
\]
The second derivative cannot tell these apart. When it vanishes, fall back on the
first-derivative test (check the sign of $f'$ on both sides). For each of these
three functions it gives a clear answer.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Why have two tests for the same thing? Because each is better at something.
The first-derivative test works at corners and cusps where $f''$ does not even
exist, but it costs you a study of $f'$ on both sides of the point. The
second-derivative test is \emph{cheap} --- one number per critical point --- but it
needs a second derivative and says nothing when that derivative is zero. In
practice, use both: reach for $f''$ first because it is fast, and when it returns
zero, go back to the sign chart. The sign chart settles every \emph{isolated}
critical point (one with no other critical points close to it), because $f'$
then keeps one sign on each side.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Reading $f$, $f'$, and $f''$ together: curve sketching}

This section combines everything into one skill: drawing an accurate graph of a
function from its formula alone. You read three layers, $f$, $f'$ and $f''$, and
check that they agree.

\begin{focusbox}
The three layers of a graph:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Object} & \textbf{What it controls} & \textbf{What you read from it}\\
\midrule
$f$ & height & intercepts, domain, asymptotes, symmetry\\
$f'$ & slope & increasing/decreasing, local max/min\\
$f''$ & bending & concave up/down, inflection points\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
$f'$ governs whether the curve goes up or down. $f''$ governs whether it smiles or
frowns. Together they fix the shape between your plotted points, which is
the information dot-plotting was missing.
\end{focusbox}

A reliable checklist, in order:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Domain and intercepts.} Where is $f$ defined? Where does it cross the
axes ($f=0$, and $f(0)$)?
\item \textbf{Symmetry and ends.} Even ($f(-x)=f(x)$, mirror image across the
$y$-axis)? Odd ($f(-x)=-f(x)$, unchanged by a half-turn about the origin)? What
happens as $x\to\pm\infty$ --- any horizontal or slant asymptotes (straight lines
the curve gets ever closer to far out)? Any vertical asymptotes, where $f$ blows
up to $\pm\infty$ near an excluded point?
\item \textbf{First derivative.} Compute $f'$, find critical points, build the sign
chart: where increasing, where decreasing, and classify the extrema.
\item \textbf{Second derivative.} Compute $f''$, find where it is zero or
undefined, build its sign chart: concavity intervals and inflection points.
\item \textbf{Assemble.} Plot the special points (intercepts, extrema,
inflections, asymptotes), then connect them \emph{respecting} the slope and
concavity you found. The shape of the curve between the special points is now
fixed.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}[A full sketch: a quartic]\label{ch16:ex-sketch-quartic}
Sketch $f(x)=x^4-4x^3$. (A \emph{quartic} is a polynomial of degree $4$, just as
a cubic has degree $3$.)

\smallskip
\textbf{1. Domain, intercepts.} Defined for all $x$. Factor:
$f(x)=x^3(x-4)$, so $f=0$ at $x=0$ and at $x=4$. At $x=0$ the factor $x$
appears three times (a \emph{triple root}), and the curve flattens as it crosses
the axis there. Also $f(0)=0$.

\textbf{2. Ends.} As $x\to\pm\infty$, the $x^4$ term dominates and $f\to+\infty$ on
both sides. No symmetry ($f(-x)=x^4+4x^3\neq\pm f(x)$). No asymptotes.

\textbf{3. First derivative.} $f'(x)=4x^3-12x^2=4x^2(x-3)$. Critical points where
$f'=0$: $x=0$ (a double root, from the factor $x^2$) and $x=3$. Sign chart:
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
& (-\infty,0) & (0,3) & (3,\infty)\\\hline
4x^2 & + & + & +\\
(x-3) & - & - & +\\
f' & - & - & +\\
f & \searrow & \searrow & \nearrow
\end{array}
\]
At $x=3$, $f'$ goes $-\to+$: \textbf{local min}, $f(3)=81-108=-27$. At $x=0$, $f'$
does \emph{not} change sign ($-$ to $-$): \emph{not} an extremum, only a momentary
flat spot on the way down.

\textbf{4. Second derivative.} $f''(x)=12x^2-24x=12x(x-2)$. Zero at $x=0$ and
$x=2$. Sign chart:
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
& (-\infty,0) & (0,2) & (2,\infty)\\\hline
12x & - & + & +\\
(x-2) & - & - & +\\
f'' & + & - & +\\
\text{shape} & \smile & \frown & \smile
\end{array}
\]
Concavity flips at \emph{both} $x=0$ and $x=2$ --- two inflection points:
$\big(0,0\big)$ and $\big(2,f(2)\big)=\big(2,-16\big)$.

\textbf{5. Assemble} (Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-quartic}). Coming from the upper left,
the curve falls concave up until
$x=0$, where it momentarily flattens (slope $0$) and switches to concave down.
It keeps falling, now concave down, until $x=2$, where it turns
concave up again while still descending, reaches the bottom of the valley at
$(3,-27)$, and finally climbs away toward $+\infty$. The flat spot at the origin (slope zero
but \emph{not} an extremum) and the deep minimum at $x=3$ are the two features dot
plotting would most likely get wrong.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A sketch with asymptotes: a rational function]\label{ch16:ex-sketch-rational}
Sketch $f(x)=\dfrac{x^2}{x^2-1}$.

\smallskip
\textbf{1. Domain, intercepts.} The denominator $x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)$ vanishes at
$x=\pm1$, so those are excluded: vertical asymptotes there. $f=0$ only when the
numerator $x^2=0$, i.e.\ $x=0$, where also $f(0)=0$. So the only intercept is the
origin.

\textbf{2. Symmetry, ends.} $f(-x)=\dfrac{x^2}{x^2-1}=f(x)$: \emph{even}, symmetric
across the $y$-axis. As $x\to\pm\infty$, $f=\dfrac{x^2}{x^2-1}\to 1$: a horizontal
asymptote $y=1$. Near the vertical asymptotes the sign of the denominator tells the
story: for $\abs{x}$ slightly less than $1$, $x^2-1<0$ so $f\to-\infty$; for
$\abs{x}$ slightly more than $1$, $x^2-1>0$ so $f\to+\infty$.

\textbf{3. First derivative.} By the quotient rule,
\[
\begin{aligned}
f'(x)&=\frac{(2x)(x^2-1)-x^2(2x)}{(x^2-1)^2}\\
&=\frac{2x^3-2x-2x^3}{(x^2-1)^2}
=\frac{-2x}{(x^2-1)^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The denominator is positive wherever defined, so the sign of $f'$ is the sign of
$-2x$: positive for $x<0$, negative for $x>0$. The only critical point is $x=0$,
and $f'$ goes $+\to-$ there: a \textbf{local maximum} at $(0,0)$. (It is a maximum
of the central branch, even though the outer branches take much larger values:
``local'' compares only with points in a small open interval around $0$.)

\textbf{4. Second derivative.} Differentiating $f'(x)=\dfrac{-2x}{(x^2-1)^2}$ with
the quotient rule, then cancelling one factor of $(x^2-1)$ from top and bottom:
\[
\begin{aligned}
f''(x)&=\frac{-2(x^2-1)^2 - (-2x)\cdot 2(x^2-1)(2x)}{(x^2-1)^4}\\
&=\frac{-2(x^2-1) + 8x^2}{(x^2-1)^3}
=\frac{2(3x^2+1)}{(x^2-1)^3}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The numerator $2(3x^2+1)$ is always positive, so the sign of $f''$ matches the sign
of $(x^2-1)^3$, which matches the sign of $(x^2-1)$:
\[
f''>0 \text{ when } \abs{x}>1 \ (\text{concave up}),\qquad
f''<0 \text{ when } \abs{x}<1 \ (\text{concave down}).
\]
There is no inflection point, because the sign change of $f''$ happens only at
$x=\pm1$, where $f$ is not defined.

\textbf{5. Assemble} (Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-rational}). Three branches. The middle
branch (between the asymptotes) is a concave-down arch peaking at the origin and
diving to $-\infty$ at both $x=\pm1$. Each outer branch is concave up and runs
from $+\infty$ next to its vertical asymptote down toward the horizontal line
$y=1$ far out; the two are mirror images of each other.
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Take $f(x)=x e^{-x}$, a linear rise multiplied by an exponential decay. This
shape appears in physics, in pharmacology (the concentration of a drug in the
blood over time), and in probability. Work out the shape entirely by hand, then check your sketch against a
plot.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Show $f'(x)=e^{-x}(1-x)$. Find the one critical point and classify it with
the first-derivative test. (Answer: max at $x=1$, value $1/e\approx0.368$.)
\item Show $f''(x)=e^{-x}(x-2)$. Find the inflection point. (Answer: at $x=2$,
where the decay's concavity flips from down to up.)
\item As $x\to+\infty$, does $f\to0$ or $\to\infty$? (The exponential
$e^{-x}$ beats the linear $x$, so $f\to0^+$ --- a horizontal asymptote $y=0$.) As
$x\to-\infty$? ($f\to-\infty$, because $e^{-x}$ explodes.)
\item Assemble: a curve rising from below, crossing the origin, climbing to a single
peak at $x=1$, then decaying back toward zero, with the decay easing off (inflection)
at $x=2$. Sketch it, then plot it and compare.
\end{enumerate}
\end{labbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Concavity is the one-variable case of a much bigger idea, \emph{convexity}, which
runs through optimization, economics, and machine learning. A function with
$f''\ge0$ everywhere is called \emph{convex}; lying below its chords is its
defining property. Convex functions have a property you can already see from this
chapter: \emph{any local minimum is automatically a global minimum}. There are no
other valleys to get stuck in, because the curve never turns back down once it has
started up. This matters in machine learning, where a model is trained by
minimizing a \emph{loss function} (a number measuring how wrong the model's
predictions are). The usual method, \emph{gradient descent}, repeatedly takes a
small step downhill. If the loss is convex, every downhill path leads to the global
minimum; if it is not, the steps can end in a valley that is not the lowest one.
We will meet the version for functions of several variables later, where $f''$ is
replaced by a table of second derivatives (a \emph{matrix}) and ``concave up''
becomes a condition on that table.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Finding the global extremes on an interval}

Local extrema compare a point only with its neighbors. But the questions that
matter in practice are usually \emph{global}: over this whole range of choices,
what is the best? On an unbounded domain a function may have no global max at all
(think $f(x)=x$, climbing forever). The case that can always be settled is a
function that is continuous on a \emph{closed, bounded} interval $[a,b]$. There the
Extreme Value Theorem (EVT) from the continuity chapter guarantees that both a
global max and a global min exist. The only question is \emph{where}.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The closed-interval method.} To find the absolute max and min of a
continuous $f$ on $[a,b]$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Find all critical points of $f$ \emph{inside} $(a,b)$.
\item Make a list: those critical points, plus the two endpoints $a$ and $b$.
\item Evaluate $f$ at every point on the list.
\item The largest value is the global max; the smallest is the global min.
\end{enumerate}
No sign charts and no second derivatives: evaluate and compare. Why this works: a
global extremum that is not at an endpoint is also a local one, and local extrema
inside the interval occur at critical points (Fermat). The only other possible
place is an endpoint. So the global max and min are both somewhere on the list.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}[Closed-interval optimization]\label{ch16:ex-closed}
Find the absolute maximum and minimum of $f(x)=x^3-3x^2-9x+5$ on $[-2,4]$.

\smallskip
We already found the critical points $x=-1$ and $x=3$ (both inside $(-2,4)$). The
list is $\{-2,-1,3,4\}$. Evaluate:
\[
\begin{aligned}
f(-2)&=-8-12+18+5=3,\\
f(-1)&=-1-3+9+5=10,\\
f(3)&=27-27-27+5=-22,\\
f(4)&=64-48-36+5=-15.
\end{aligned}
\]
The largest is $f(-1)=10$: \textbf{global maximum}. The smallest is $f(3)=-22$:
\textbf{global minimum} (Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-closed-interval}). Here the winners
happen to be interior critical points, but endpoints must always be on the list.
On the longer interval $[-2,6]$, for example, the right endpoint gives
$f(6)=216-108-54+5=59$, which beats the critical point's $10$: the global maximum
would then be at the endpoint $x=6$.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
The closed-interval method needs both hypotheses of the Extreme Value Theorem:
a \emph{continuous} function on a \emph{closed, bounded} interval. Drop either and
the guarantee is lost. On the open interval $(0,1)$ the function $f(x)=x$ has no
maximum --- it gets arbitrarily close to $1$ but never reaches it, and $1$ is not in
the domain. On $[0,\infty)$ the function $f(x)=x$ has no maximum either. And a
function with a vertical asymptote inside the interval (a discontinuity) can shoot
to $+\infty$ with no max at all. When the EVT does not apply, you must examine the
behavior near the open ends or at infinity by hand.
\end{trapbox}

\section{What we built}

The whole chapter is one idea: \emph{the derivatives of $f$ encode the
shape of $f$}. The first derivative records direction. The points where it is zero
or undefined (critical points) include every local extremum, and its sign on
either side (the first-derivative test) classifies them. The second derivative
records bending. Its sign gives concavity, its sign \emph{changes} give
inflection points, and at a flat tangent its sign settles peak-versus-valley in
one step (the second-derivative test). Reading both at once, you can work out the
shape of a graph, and on a closed interval you can find the absolute best and
worst from a short list of values.

Underneath every one of these tests sits the Mean Value Theorem,
converting facts about $f'$ into facts about $f$. The rule ``positive derivative means
increasing'' is the MVT, and the first-derivative test applies that fact on each
side of a critical point. Concavity applies the same fact to $f'$, and the
second-derivative test applies concavity at a horizontal tangent.

In the next chapter we put these tests to work on real questions --- the largest
box, the cheapest can, the shortest path, the maximum profit --- where the skill is
no longer running the tests but \emph{setting up} the right function to optimize.

\subsection*{Summary: Curve Shape and Optimization}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Summary: Curve Shape and Optimization}

\begin{connectionbox}
With the first and second derivatives, you can systematically determine:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Critical points and extrema}: every candidate peak, valley, and flat spot ($f'=0$ or undefined), found from the formula rather than by plotting points.
  \item \emph{Increase, decrease and concavity}: the intervals where the graph rises, falls, bends up like a cup, or bends down like a dome.
  \item \emph{Inflection points and global extremes}: where the bending reverses, and, with the closed-interval method, the largest and smallest values on a closed interval.
\end{itemize}

These methods assume that the critical points are \emph{isolated} (each has no other critical points close to it) and that $f'$ keeps one sign between them. A function that wiggles infinitely fast near a point breaks this assumption. The standard example is
\[
  f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 \sin\big(\tfrac{1}{x}\big), & x \ne 0, \\ 0, & x = 0. \end{cases}
\]
Here
\[
f'(0) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{h^2 \sin(1/h)}{h} = \lim_{h\to 0} h\sin(1/h) = 0,
\]
so $x=0$ is a critical point. Yet for $x \ne 0$,
\[
f'(x) = 2x\sin(1/x) - \cos(1/x),
\]
which switches between positive and negative infinitely often in every open interval around $0$. So $f$ has infinitely many local maxima and minima crowding in toward the origin (Figure~\ref{ch16:fig-wiggle}). The first-derivative test cannot be applied, because $f'$ does not keep one sign on either side of $0$, and $f''(0)$ does not exist. The functions met in most applications are not like this, and for them the tests of this chapter work.
\end{connectionbox}

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental warm-ups]\label{ch16:ex-warmup}
Answer each question mentally or with a one-line sketch before calculating:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $f'(x) = (x-2)^2(x-5)$, where does $f$ have a local minimum? Does $x=2$ yield an extremum?
\item Can a cubic polynomial $f(x)=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d$ ($a\ne0$) have two local maxima? Explain using the degree of $f'$.
\item If a differentiable function on $[1,5]$ satisfies $f'(x)>0$ for all $x\in(1,5)$, where do its global minimum and global maximum occur?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch16:exA}
For each function, find all critical points and classify each as a local maximum,
local minimum, or neither, using the first-derivative test.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f(x)=2x^3-9x^2+12x-3$
\item $f(x)=x^4-8x^2+3$
\item $f(x)=x^{2/3}(x-5)$ \quad(watch for a point where $f'$ does not exist)
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch16:exB}
Find the intervals of concavity and all inflection points of
$f(x)=x^4-6x^2+5$. Then confirm, using the second-derivative test, the nature of
each critical point.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch16:exC}
Find the absolute maximum and minimum of $f(x)=2x^3-3x^2-12x+1$ on the closed
interval $[-2,3]$. Identify whether each extreme occurs at an endpoint or at an
interior critical point.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch16:exD}
Carry out a full curve sketch of $f(x)=\dfrac{x}{x^2+1}$: domain, symmetry,
intercepts, end behavior and asymptotes, intervals of increase/decrease with
extrema, and intervals of concavity with inflection points. Then draw the graph.
(Hint: $f$ is odd, $f\to0$ at both ends, and you will find one max, one min, and
three inflection points.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch16:exE}
\textbf{(Conceptual.)} For each description, either sketch a function meeting it or
explain briefly why none can exist.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f'(2)=0$ but $f$ has neither a max nor a min at $x=2$.
\item $f''(2)=0$ but $f$ has no inflection point at $x=2$.
\item $f$ has a local minimum at $x=2$ but $f'(2)$ does not exist.
\item $f$ has a local maximum at $x=2$ and $f''(2)>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch16:exF}
\textbf{(Reading $f$ from $f'$.)} The derivative of a function $f$ is
$f'(x)=(x-1)^2(x-4)$. Without finding $f$ itself, determine: (a) the intervals
where $f$ is increasing and decreasing; (b) the locations and types of all local
extrema; (c) whether $x=1$ is special, and why the squared factor matters.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch16:exG}
\textbf{(Why the test works.)} Suppose $f''$ is continuous and $f''(c)>0$ at a point
where $f'(c)=0$. Using the definition $f''(c)=\lim_{x\to
c}\dfrac{f'(x)-f'(c)}{x-c}$, prove directly that $f'$ is negative just to the left
of $c$ and positive just to the right, and conclude that $f$ has a local minimum at
$c$. (This is the second-derivative test, derived from scratch.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch16:exH}
\textbf{(Proof, tangent line and concavity.)} Let $I$ be an interval and let $f$
be twice differentiable on $I$ (that is, $f''(x)$ exists for every $x$ in $I$),
with $f''(x)>0$ for all $x\in I$. Prove that for any fixed $a\in I$,
\[
f(x)\ \ge\ f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\qquad\text{for all } x\in I,
\]
with equality only at $x=a$. (That is: a curve with $f''>0$ lies strictly above
each of its tangent lines. Imitate the gap-function argument from the chapter:
let $g(x)=f(x)-f(a)-f'(a)(x-a)$ and study the sign of $g'$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch16:exI}
\textbf{(Inflections are extrema of $f'$.)} Explain carefully why an inflection
point of $f$ corresponds to a local maximum or minimum of $f'$. Use this to find
the inflection point of $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$ by maximizing $f'$ instead of
analyzing $f''$ directly, and confirm you get $x=0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}\label{ch16:exJ}
\textbf{(Modeling.)} A drug's concentration in the bloodstream $t$ hours after a
dose is modeled by $C(t)=12t\,e^{-t/2}$ for $t\ge0$. (a) When is the concentration
highest, and what is that peak concentration? (b) The concentration is ``falling
fastest'' at the inflection point of $C$; find that time. (c) Sketch $C$ for
$t\in[0,12]$, marking the peak and the inflection.
\end{exercise}
