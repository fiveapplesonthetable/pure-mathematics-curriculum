\chapter{Trigonometric and Inverse Functions}

In Chapter~3 we studied exponential growth: curves that climb ever faster, and the
base $e$ whose rate of change equals its height. Many things in the physical world
do not grow without limit. They repeat: they swing back and forth, cycle, and
return to where they started. This chapter builds the functions that describe them.

These functions were not invented by people doing calculus. Astronomers tracking the stars and surveyors dividing up land used them thousands of years earlier. Sine and cosine describe anything that repeats, swings, rotates, or vibrates. A swinging pendulum, an alternating current, a sound wave, the seasons, the orbit of a planet, the position of a point on a spinning wheel: measured carefully, each of them can be described with sines and cosines.

Before we can differentiate $\sin x$ or integrate $\cos x$, we need to know what these functions \emph{are}. We will define them as the coordinates of a point moving around a circle. (The right-triangle ratios you may remember from school come out of this picture too.) The circle picture explains the identities, the graphs and the symmetries, and it shows why measuring angles in \emph{radians} keeps the derivative formulas free of conversion constants.

We will also meet the \emph{inverse} trig functions, which answer the question ``what angle has this sine?'' Sine repeats its values over and over, so many angles share the same sine, and we must choose which one the inverse should report. Making that choice carefully shows how to invert any function that is not one-to-one.

\section{Angles: What Are We Measuring?}
\label{ch04:angles}

An angle measures \emph{how much you have turned}. Stand facing north, rotate to face east: you have turned through one quarter of a full turn. Rotate to face south: half a turn. The simplest way to describe a turn is as a \emph{fraction of a full revolution}, a pure number with no units attached.

To get a measuring scale we only have to choose a \emph{unit}, a number to call ``one full turn'', and the choice is a convention. The Babylonians, working in base 60 and tracking a sky that seemed to cycle once a year through roughly 360 days, declared one full turn to be $360$ \emph{degrees}. There is nothing sacred about $360$; it is a culturally inherited number that happens to divide nicely ($360 = 2^3\cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$, so halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths, eighths, ninths, tenths, twelfths all come out whole). For everyday geometry and navigation, degrees work fine; in calculus they bring an awkward factor of $\pi/180$ into every derivative.

\begin{intuitionbox}
An angle is not a length and not an area. It is a \emph{turn}: a fraction of a full rotation. Degrees and radians are two different rulers laid against that same turn. Degrees call a full turn $360$; radians call it $2\pi$. Same turn, different number. We want the ruler that makes the rest of mathematics simplest, and that ruler is radians.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Radians: Measuring an Angle by the Arc It Cuts}

Radians measure an angle without any arbitrary number. Draw a circle of radius $r$ and an angle at its center. That angle opens up onto an \emph{arc} -- a curved piece of the circle's outer rim. The bigger the angle, the longer the arc. In fact the arc length is exactly proportional to the angle. So why not let the arc \emph{be} the measure of the angle?

There is one wrinkle: a bigger circle gives a longer arc for the same angle, so raw arc length depends on $r$. We fix that by dividing out the radius. Define:
\[
  \theta \;=\; \frac{\text{arc length}}{\text{radius}} \;=\; \frac{s}{r}.
\]
This ratio is the same for every circle, because doubling $r$ doubles $s$. The number you get is the angle measured in \textbf{radians}.

\begin{definition}[Radian]
\label{ch04:def-radian}
The radian measure of a central angle (an angle whose vertex sits right at the center of the circle) is the length of the arc it subtends (the slice of the rim carved out between the two arms of the angle) divided by the radius: $\theta = s/r$. Equivalently, an angle of one radian subtends an arc whose length equals the radius.
\end{definition}

Because $s = r\theta$, an angle of one radian is the angle for which the arc is \emph{exactly as long as the radius}. Wrap one radius-length of string along the rim of the circle, and the angle it spans at the center is one radian, a little under $60^\circ$ ($57.2958\ldots^\circ$, to be precise).

How many radians in a full turn? The whole circumference has length $2\pi r$, so a full revolution measures $s/r = 2\pi r / r = 2\pi$ radians. That gives us the master conversion:
\[
  2\pi \text{ radians} \;=\; 360^\circ,
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  \pi \text{ radians} = 180^\circ,
  \qquad
  1 \text{ rad} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi}.
\]
A handful of values worth knowing cold:
\[
  30^\circ = \tfrac{\pi}{6},\quad
  45^\circ = \tfrac{\pi}{4},\quad
  60^\circ = \tfrac{\pi}{3},\quad
  90^\circ = \tfrac{\pi}{2},\quad
  180^\circ = \pi,\quad
  360^\circ = 2\pi.
\]

\begin{happeningbox}
When you write ``$\theta = \pi/3$'' you are also writing a \emph{length}. You are saying: on the unit circle (the circle of radius $1$), walk a distance of $\pi/3 \approx 1.047$ along the rim. When $r=1$ the angle and the arc length are the same number. This is why, throughout calculus, the variable inside $\sin$ and $\cos$ feels like a distance: in radians on the unit circle, it \emph{is} one. Degrees hide this by inserting the arbitrary scaling factor $\pi/180$, which is exactly the factor that will later clutter up every derivative if you let it.
\end{happeningbox}

The definition $s = r\theta$ gives two formulas at once.

\textbf{Arc length.} The length of an arc of radius $r$ spanning angle $\theta$ (in radians) is $s = r\theta$, with no factor of $\pi/180$.

\textbf{Sector area.} A \emph{sector} is the pie-slice region between two radii and the arc joining them (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-sector}). A full disk has area $\pi r^2$ and spans $2\pi$ radians. A sector of angle $\theta$ is the fraction $\theta/(2\pi)$ of the disk, so its area is
\[
  A \;=\; \frac{\theta}{2\pi}\cdot \pi r^2 \;=\; \tfrac12 r^2 \theta.
\]
Hold onto that formula $A = \tfrac12 r^2\theta$; later in this chapter (Section~\ref{ch04:smallangle}) we will use it to find the most important limit in trigonometry.

\section{Sine and Cosine as Coordinates on the Unit Circle}
\label{ch04:unit-circle}

Forget triangles for a moment. Draw the \textbf{unit circle}: the circle of radius $1$ centered at the origin, the set of points with $x^2 + y^2 = 1$.

Start at the point $(1,0)$ -- three o'clock -- and walk counterclockwise along the rim. After walking an arc-length $t$ (equivalently, after turning through an angle of $t$ radians, since $r=1$), you arrive at some point on the circle. \emph{That point's coordinates are the definition of cosine and sine.}

\begin{definition}[Sine and cosine, circle definition]
\label{ch04:def-sincos}
Let $t$ be a real number. Travel counterclockwise a distance $t$ along the unit circle, starting from $(1,0)$. The coordinates of the point you reach are denoted $(\cos t,\ \sin t)$. That is, $\cos t$ is the horizontal coordinate and $\sin t$ is the vertical coordinate of the point at arc-length $t$ on the unit circle.
\end{definition}

Defining cosine and sine on the unit circle beats the right-triangle ``opposite over hypotenuse'' recipe in three ways. It works for \emph{every} real number $t$, not just acute angles between $0$ and $90^\circ$: you can keep walking past the top of the circle, past the left side, all the way around and around. It makes the periodic symmetries transparent, as the next section shows. And it ties $\sin$ and $\cos$ directly to motion along a curve, which is what derivatives measure.

The connection to the right-triangle picture is immediate. Look at Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-unitcircle}: the radius to the point, the vertical segment, and the segment along the $x$-axis form a right triangle with hypotenuse $1$. The horizontal leg is $\cos t$, and the vertical leg is $\sin t$. The standard right-triangle definitions -- often remembered by the mnemonic SOH-CAH-TOA ($\sin = \text{Opposite}/\text{Hypotenuse}$, $\cos = \text{Adjacent}/\text{Hypotenuse}$, $\tan = \text{Opposite}/\text{Adjacent}$) -- are this picture scaled up or down: in any right triangle with hypotenuse $h$, the leg opposite angle $t$ is $h\sin t$ and the leg adjacent is $h\cos t$. The triangle inside the unit circle is the reference triangle with hypotenuse $1$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Picture a point riding counterclockwise around the unit circle at steady speed. Its \emph{shadow on the $x$-axis} (the horizontal coordinate) traces out $\cos t$ as $t$ increases. Its \emph{shadow on the $y$-axis} traces out $\sin t$. Both shadows slide back and forth between $-1$ and $1$ forever. Sine and cosine are the two shadows of steady circular motion.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Everything That Follows Immediately}

Several facts follow directly from this one definition.

\textbf{The Pythagorean identity.} The point $(\cos t, \sin t)$ lies \emph{on} the unit circle, and the unit circle is the set with $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. Therefore, for every real $t$,
\[
  \boxed{\;\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1.\;}
\]
No further proof is needed: this \emph{is} the equation of the circle, with $x$ renamed $\cos t$ and $y$ renamed $\sin t$. (The notation $\cos^2 t$ is shorthand for $(\cos t)^2$, an old convention.)

\textbf{Range.} Both coordinates of a point on the unit circle lie between $-1$ and $1$. So $-1 \le \sin t \le 1$ and $-1 \le \cos t \le 1$ for all $t$. The functions never escape that band.

\textbf{Periodicity.} Walking an extra full lap -- an extra $2\pi$ of arc -- brings you back to the same point. Hence
\[
  \sin(t + 2\pi) = \sin t,\qquad \cos(t + 2\pi) = \cos t.
\]
A function $f$ has \textbf{period} $p$ if $f(t+p) = f(t)$ for every $t$, with $p$ the smallest positive number that works: the graph repeats itself every $p$ units. Sine and cosine have period $2\pi$. This is why they model anything cyclic.

\textbf{Special values.} Read them straight off the circle. At $t=0$ you are at $(1,0)$, so $\cos 0 = 1,\ \sin 0 = 0$. A quarter turn $t = \pi/2$ lands you at the top $(0,1)$: $\cos(\pi/2) = 0,\ \sin(\pi/2) = 1$. Half a turn $t=\pi$ puts you at $(-1,0)$. Three-quarters $t = 3\pi/2$ at the bottom $(0,-1)$.

The two axes cut the plane into four \textbf{quadrants}, numbered I to IV counterclockwise starting from the top right, where $x>0$ and $y>0$. The quadrant a point lands in tells you the signs of $\cos t$ and $\sin t$ before you compute anything. Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-special-points} shows the quadrants, the signs, and the special angles in the table below.

The sine row follows a pattern: it reads $\tfrac{\sqrt0}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt1}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt4}{2}$, and the cosine row is the same thing backwards. The values at $\pi/6, \pi/4, \pi/3$ come from two special triangles, the $30$-$60$-$90$ and the $45$-$45$-$90$, shown in Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-special-triangles}.

\begin{labbox}
Find $\sin(\pi/4)$ and $\cos(\pi/4)$ from the circle. At $t = \pi/4$ (a $45^\circ$ turn) you are exactly on the diagonal line $y = x$, by symmetry: the point is equidistant from the two axes. So $\cos(\pi/4) = \sin(\pi/4)$; call the common value $c$. Pythagoras says $c^2 + c^2 = 1$, hence $2c^2 = 1$, so $c = 1/\sqrt2 = \sqrt2/2 \approx 0.707$. This table value came from the circle and Pythagoras alone, with no calculator.
\end{labbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: the $30^\circ$--$60^\circ$--$90^\circ$ triangle.}
Take an equilateral triangle with side lengths $1$, so each corner angle is $60^\circ = \pi/3$ radians. Drop a vertical altitude from the top vertex down to the base. That cuts the triangle in half, creating two identical right triangles with angles $30^\circ = \pi/6$ and $60^\circ = \pi/3$. The base of each half is $\tfrac12$. By the Pythagorean theorem, the vertical height $h$ satisfies $h^2 + (\tfrac12)^2 = 1^2$, which gives $h = \sqrt{1 - 1/4} = \sqrt3/2$. Look at that half-triangle: opposite the $30^\circ$ angle is $\tfrac12$ (so $\sin(\pi/6) = \tfrac12$), and adjacent is $\sqrt3/2$ (so $\cos(\pi/6) = \sqrt3/2$). Looking from the $60^\circ$ angle reverses their roles. The whole table of special values comes from these two triangles.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{The Other Four: Tangent and Friends}
\label{ch04:tangent}

Sine and cosine are the parents; the remaining four trig functions are built from them by division. The most important is the \textbf{tangent}:
\[
  \tan t \;=\; \frac{\sin t}{\cos t}.
\]
Geometrically, $\tan t$ is the \emph{slope} of the radius line to the point $(\cos t,\sin t)$: rise over run, $\sin t / \cos t$. It also has a direct geometric meaning, shown in Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-tangent-secant}. Draw the vertical line $x = 1$; it is a \emph{tangent line} to the circle, touching it at $(1,0)$ without crossing it. Extend the ray from the origin through $(\cos t,\sin t)$ until it hits this vertical line at $(1,\tan t)$. Because the horizontal base of that outer right triangle has length $1$, the vertical segment cut off along the tangent line is exactly $\tan t$ (since $\text{opposite}/\text{adjacent} = \tan t/1 = \tan t$). This is where the name \emph{tangent} comes from (Latin \emph{tangere}, to touch).

What about the length along that extended ray from the origin all the way to $(1,\tan t)$? That ray cuts through the circle, making it a \emph{secant} line (Latin \emph{secare}, to cut). The outer triangle is the inner triangle scaled up by the factor $1/\cos t$ (its horizontal side is $1$ instead of $\cos t$), so its hypotenuse has length $1/\cos t$. This length is called the \textbf{secant}, $\sec t$. Together with the cotangent and cosecant, the remaining three functions are reciprocals:
\[
  \cot t = \frac{\cos t}{\sin t} = \frac{1}{\tan t},\qquad
  \sec t = \frac{1}{\cos t},\qquad
  \csc t = \frac{1}{\sin t}.
\]
Each is undefined wherever its denominator hits zero. Tangent and secant blow up where $\cos t = 0$, i.e.\ at $t = \pm\pi/2, \pm 3\pi/2,\ldots$; cotangent and cosecant blow up where $\sin t = 0$, i.e.\ at $t = 0, \pm\pi, \pm 2\pi,\ldots$. Dividing the Pythagorean identity $\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$ through by $\cos^2 t$ gives a useful cousin:
\[
  1 + \tan^2 t = \sec^2 t,
\]
and dividing instead by $\sin^2 t$ gives $1 + \cot^2 t = \csc^2 t$. Both are the Pythagorean identity in another form. (Applying Pythagoras to the outer triangle, with legs $1$ and $\tan t$ and hypotenuse $\sec t$, gives $1 + \tan^2 t = \sec^2 t$ directly.)

\begin{trapbox}
$\tan t$ has period $\pi$, \emph{not} $2\pi$. After half a lap the point $(\cos t,\sin t)$ flips to its antipode (the diametrically opposite point on the other side of the circle, $(-\cos t, -\sin t)$), and the two sign flips cancel in the ratio $\sin t/\cos t$. So $\tan(t+\pi) = \tan t$: both points lie on the same line through the origin, so they give the same slope (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-tan-period}). If you assume every trig function repeats every $2\pi$, you will get tangent wrong. Reason from the circle, not from a memorized rule.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Symmetries: Reading Identities Off the Circle}
\label{ch04:identities}

Most trigonometric identities are symmetries of the circle, so you can read them off a picture instead of memorizing them. Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-reflections} shows the first two below; Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-cofunction} shows the third.

\textbf{Even and odd.} Replace $t$ by $-t$: instead of walking counterclockwise you walk clockwise the same distance. You land at the mirror image across the $x$-axis. Mirroring across the $x$-axis keeps the horizontal coordinate and flips the vertical one:
\[
  \cos(-t) = \cos t \quad(\text{even}),\qquad
  \sin(-t) = -\sin t \quad(\text{odd}).
\]
In the language of Chapter~2, cosine is an \emph{even} function ($f(-t) = f(t)$, graph symmetric across the vertical axis) and sine is an \emph{odd} function ($f(-t) = -f(t)$).

\textbf{Supplementary angle ($\pi - t$).} Two angles are \emph{supplementary} when they add up to $\pi$ (that is, $180^\circ$). The point at angle $\pi - t$ is the mirror image of the point at angle $t$ across the \emph{$y$-axis}. Mirroring across the $y$-axis flips $x$ and keeps $y$:
\[
  \cos(\pi - t) = -\cos t,\qquad \sin(\pi - t) = \sin t.
\]

\textbf{Co-function / quarter turn ($\tfrac{\pi}{2} - t$).} Two angles are \emph{complementary} when they add up to $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ (a right angle), and this identity is the source of the prefix ``co-'' in cosine. The point at angle $\tfrac{\pi}{2} - t$ is the reflection of the point at angle $t$ across the diagonal line $y = x$. Reflecting across $y=x$ \emph{swaps} the coordinates. Therefore
\[
  \sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - t\right) = \cos t,\qquad
  \cos\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - t\right) = \sin t.
\]
Cosine of an angle is the sine of its complement: ``cosine'' means ``\textbf{co}mplement's \textbf{sine}.''

\begin{whybox}
Why can we get away with \emph{reading} identities off pictures instead of grinding algebra? Because $\sin$ and $\cos$ were \emph{defined} as coordinates of a point on the circle, and a geometric transformation of the circle (a reflection, a rotation) does a predictable thing to coordinates. Reflect across the $x$-axis: $y \mapsto -y$, so $\sin \mapsto -\sin$. Rotate by a quarter turn, reflect across a diagonal -- each motion is a simple coordinate operation. So every symmetry of the circle is automatically an identity, and the identity records where the coordinates went.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{The Addition Formulas}

One family of identities cannot be read off a single reflection, and much of what follows depends on it: the addition formulas
\[
  \cos(a+b) = \cos a\cos b - \sin a \sin b,
  \qquad
  \sin(a+b) = \sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b.
\]
These say: if you know where angles $a$ and $b$ land, you can find where angle $a+b$ lands without walking the whole way. The derivative of sine is computed from them, so we derive them here, by rotating the plane (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-rotation}).

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Deriving the addition formula by rotation.} Rotating the entire plane counterclockwise by an angle $a$ sends the reference point $(1,0)$ to $(\cos a, \sin a)$ and sends $(0,1)$ to $(-\sin a, \cos a)$ (a quarter turn ahead of the first). Any point $(x,y)$ can be reached from the origin by going $x$ steps along $(1,0)$ and then $y$ steps along $(0,1)$. A rotation turns the whole plane rigidly, so it turns this path too: the rotated point is reached by going $x$ steps along $(\cos a,\sin a)$ and $y$ steps along $(-\sin a,\cos a)$. So $(x,y)$ is carried to
\[
  (x\cos a - y\sin a,\ \ x\sin a + y\cos a).
\]
Now take the point on the circle at angle $b$, namely $(x,y) = (\cos b, \sin b)$, and rotate it further by angle $a$. Geometrically that lands it at angle $a+b$, meaning its coordinates are $(\cos(a+b), \sin(a+b))$. Algebraically, substituting $x = \cos b$ and $y = \sin b$ into the rotation formula sends it to
\[
  (\cos b\cos a - \sin b\sin a,\ \ \cos b \sin a + \sin b\cos a).
\]
Matching the geometric coordinates with the algebraic ones, first coordinate with first and second with second, gives
\begin{align*}
  \cos(a+b) &= \cos a\cos b - \sin a\sin b,\\
  \sin(a+b) &= \sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b,
\end{align*}
which are exactly the cosine and sine addition formulas. Both come from one fact: rotating by $b$ and then by $a$ is the same as rotating by $a+b$.
\end{focusbox}

Two consequences we will lean on. Setting $b = a$ gives the \textbf{double-angle} formulas:
\begin{align*}
  \sin 2a &= 2\sin a\cos a,\\
  \cos 2a &= \cos^2 a - \sin^2 a\\
          &= 1 - 2\sin^2 a &&\text{(replace $\cos^2 a$ by $1-\sin^2 a$)}\\
          &= 2\cos^2 a - 1. &&\text{(replace $\sin^2 a$ by $1-\cos^2 a$)}
\end{align*}
The last two forms rearrange into the \textbf{half-angle} formulas, which are used constantly in integration:
\[
  \sin^2 a = \frac{1 - \cos 2a}{2},\qquad
  \cos^2 a = \frac{1 + \cos 2a}{2}.
\]
Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-half-angle} shows what the first one says about graphs: squaring sine gives a wave that stays between $0$ and $1$, centered on the height $\tfrac12$, and wiggling twice as fast. When you later integrate $\sin^2$ or $\cos^2$, these are the formulas you will use.

\begin{historybox}
The addition formulas are ancient. In effect they appear in Ptolemy's \emph{Almagest} (c.\ 150~CE) as ``Ptolemy's theorem'' about chords of a circle: Greek astronomers worked not with our modern sine but with the \emph{chord} of an angle, the straight-line distance across the arc. In fifth-century India, Aryabhata introduced the half-chord ($jya$), which is our modern sine. The word ``sine'' itself arose from a mistranslation: the Sanskrit \emph{jya} became Arabic \emph{jiba} (written as the consonants $jb$), which twelfth-century Latin translators misread as \emph{jaib} (a fold or bay) and translated as \emph{sinus}. In 1748, Leonhard Euler defined sine and cosine on the unit circle as functions of a real number, giving trigonometry its modern form.
\end{historybox}

Read the structure straight off the graph. The wave oscillates between $-1$ and $1$; its \textbf{amplitude}, the largest distance it reaches from its middle line, is $1$. It completes one full cycle every $2\pi$ (the \textbf{period}). Sine starts at $0$ climbing; cosine starts at $1$ already at its peak. And the two curves are \emph{the same shape shifted}: $\cos t = \sin(t + \tfrac\pi2)$. Shifting sine to the left by a quarter period lands it exactly on cosine. This is the co-function identity again; using that cosine is even,
\[
  \sin\!\left(t+\tfrac\pi2\right) = \sin\!\left(\tfrac\pi2-(-t)\right) = \cos(-t) = \cos t.
\]
The cosine wave is the sine wave with a head start of a quarter turn.

\begin{connectionbox}
A general sinusoid (a wave with the shape of the sine curve) is written $y = A\sin(\omega t + \varphi) + k$. Each parameter is a geometric knob: $A$ is the amplitude (vertical stretch), $\omega$ (Greek omega) is the angular frequency, the number of radians the inner angle advances per unit of $t$ (it squeezes the wave horizontally, and the period is $2\pi/\omega$), $\varphi$ (Greek phi) is the phase (horizontal shift, the head start), and $k$ is the vertical offset (the height of the middle line). Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-sinusoid} labels each one on a graph. Because sine and cosine differ only by a phase, \emph{any} sinusoid can be written with either one. This four-knob family describes \emph{simple harmonic motion} in physics, such as a mass bobbing on a spring, $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$. It is also the building block of Fourier analysis (met in Chapter~2), where a periodic signal, however jagged, is written as a sum of these smooth waves.
\end{connectionbox}

The tangent graph looks entirely different, and the circle tells us why. The result is a curve that races up to a vertical asymptote, vanishes off the top, and reappears from the bottom -- repeating every $\pi$, not $2\pi$.

\section{The Small-Angle Behavior: $\sin t \approx t$}
\label{ch04:smallangle}

This section proves the fact about trigonometry that the rest of the book uses most. Look again at the unit circle for a \emph{small} angle $t$, just above $(1,0)$.

Three lengths are racing to zero together as $t\to 0$: the vertical drop $\sin t$, the arc $t$ along the rim, and the tangent segment $\tan t$. Geometrically they nest:
\[
  \sin t \;<\; t \;<\; \tan t \qquad (0 < t < \tfrac{\pi}{2}).
\]
The first inequality holds because the vertical segment of length $\sin t$ is shorter than the straight line from $(1,0)$ to the point (that line rises by $\sin t$ and also moves sideways, so it is longer than $\sin t$), and that straight line is shorter than the curved arc joining the same two points. The second inequality comes from comparing areas, as the proof below shows. As the angle shrinks, the arc straightens out and these three lengths become indistinguishable. The most useful way to say this precisely is in terms of the ratio $\sin t / t$.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The fundamental trig limit.}
\[
  \boxed{\;\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\sin t}{t} = 1.\;}
\]
In words: for small $t$ (\emph{in radians}), $\sin t$ and $t$ are almost equal: their ratio approaches $1$. Equivalently, $\sin t \approx t$ when $t$ is small; Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-sint-approx-t} shows the curve and the line staying close together near the origin. The calculus of trig functions is built on this limit: the derivative of sine (worked out in Chapter~12, once limits and differentiation have been developed) reduces directly to it.
\end{focusbox}

We can already see \emph{why} it is true, and sketch the proof, using only the sector-area formula $A = \tfrac12 r^2\theta$ from the start of the chapter.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Why $\sin t / t \to 1$: the squeeze.} Work on the unit circle ($r=1$) with $0 < t < \pi/2$. Compare three areas, shaded in Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-squeeze-areas}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the inner triangle with vertices $(0,0),(1,0),(\cos t,\sin t)$ has base $1$ along the $x$-axis and vertical height $\sin t$, giving area $\tfrac12\sin t$;
  \item the circular sector of angle $t$ has area $\tfrac12 t$ (using $A=\tfrac12 r^2\theta$ with $r=1$);
  \item the outer right triangle with vertices $(0,0),(1,0),(1,\tan t)$ has base $1$ and vertical height $\tan t$, giving area $\tfrac12\tan t$.
\end{itemize}
The inner triangle sits inside the sector, which sits inside the outer triangle. Write that down, then rearrange:
\begin{align*}
  \tfrac12 \sin t &< \tfrac12 t < \tfrac12 \tan t
    &&\text{(compare the areas)}\\
  1 &< \frac{t}{\sin t} < \frac{1}{\cos t}
    &&\text{(multiply by $2$, divide by $\sin t > 0$)}\\
  \cos t &< \frac{\sin t}{t} < 1.
    &&\text{(take reciprocals, which reverses ${<}$)}
\end{align*}
As $t\to 0$, $\cos t \to 1$. The ratio $\sin t/t$ is trapped -- \emph{squeezed} -- between something heading to $1$ and the constant $1$ (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-squeeze-graph}), so $\lim_{t\to 0}\sin t/t = 1$. (The case $t<0$ follows because $\sin t/t$ is an even function.)
\end{focusbox}

\begin{trapbox}
This limit equals $1$ \textbf{only in radians}. If $t$ is measured in degrees, then walking ``$t$ degrees'' covers an arc of only $\tfrac{\pi}{180}t$, so $\sin(t^\circ)/t \to \pi/180 \approx 0.01745$, an unwanted constant. Every derivative formula for trig functions would carry that factor (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-degrees-slope}). \emph{This is the real reason calculus uses radians.} Radians are the unit in which arc length equals angle, which is the unit in which $\sin t \approx t$, which is the unit in which the derivatives come out clean.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: the $1^\circ$ test.}
A $1^\circ$ angle is $\pi/180 \approx 0.0174533$ radians. Because $\sin t \approx t$ for small angles in radians, what should $\sin(1^\circ)$ be? It should be approximately $0.0174533$. Tap $\sin(1^\circ)$ into a calculator set to degrees: it gives $0.0174524\ldots$. The two numbers agree to five decimal places. If instead you put the degree count $t = 1$ into the approximation, you would predict $\sin(1^\circ) \approx 1$, which is off by a factor of about $57$. The approximation $\sin t \approx t$ (replacing the sine curve by its tangent line at $0$) works only when $t$ is measured in radians.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
A great deal depends on $\sin t/t\to 1$. It says that, up close, a sine wave and a straight line of slope $1$ look the same: the circle, viewed under a microscope at $(1,0)$, looks flat and vertical, rising one unit of height per unit of arc. From this ``the curve is locally straight'' fact will come the derivative formula $\dv{}{t}\sin t = \cos t$, and with it the mathematics of waves, light and sound. Later (Chapter~30) it reappears as the first term of the Taylor series $\sin t = t - \tfrac{t^3}{6} + \cdots$, which writes $\sin t$ as a polynomial with infinitely many terms.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{The pendulum.} Let $\theta(t)$ be the angle of a pendulum of length $L$ from the vertical at time $t$ (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-pendulum}), and let $g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ be the acceleration due to gravity. Newton's laws give, exactly,
\[
  \ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta,
\]
where $\ddot\theta$ (read ``theta double-dot'') is the angular acceleration: the rate of change of the rate of change of $\theta$. This equation is hard: no formula built from the familiar functions solves it. But for small swings, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, and the equation becomes
\[
  \ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{L}\,\theta.
\]
This simpler equation is called \emph{linear}, because $\theta$ appears only to the first power, and replacing an equation by such a simpler one is called \emph{linearizing}. It is solved by a pure sinusoid,
\[
  \theta(t) = A\cos\!\left(\sqrt{g/L}\;t\right),
  \qquad\text{with period}\qquad
  T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}.
\]
The period does not depend on the size $A$ of the swing, as long as the swing is small, which is why a pendulum clock keeps near-constant time (Galileo's observation). The physics of small vibrations in springs, molecules, circuits and bridges relies on this same move of replacing a curve by its tangent line, and $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ is the most famous instance.
\end{applicationbox}

A companion limit, derived the same way and needed alongside the first, is
\[
  \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1 - \cos t}{t} = 0.
\]
Intuitively: near $t=0$ the height $\sin t$ is about $t$, but the horizontal gap $1-\cos t$ is about $t^2/2$, which is far smaller (for $t = 0.1$ it is about $0.005$). So even after dividing by $t$, what is left still heads to zero. Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-one-minus-cos} compares the two gaps. To prove it, multiply top and bottom by $1+\cos t$ (the same two terms with the sign flipped, called the \emph{conjugate}), which sets up a difference of squares $(1-\cos t)(1+\cos t) = 1-\cos^2 t = \sin^2 t$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{1-\cos t}{t}
  &= \frac{(1-\cos t)(1+\cos t)}{t(1+\cos t)}
    &&\text{(multiply top and bottom by $1+\cos t$)}\\
  &= \frac{1-\cos^2 t}{t(1+\cos t)}\\
  &= \frac{\sin^2 t}{t(1+\cos t)}
    &&\text{(since $1-\cos^2 t = \sin^2 t$)}\\
  &= \underbrace{\frac{\sin t}{t}}_{\to 1}\cdot \underbrace{\frac{\sin t}{1+\cos t}}_{\to\, 0/2\,=\,0}.
\end{align*}
The first factor tends to $1$ by our main limit; the second tends to $0/2 = 0$. Product: $0$. Both limits will reappear, fused, the moment we differentiate sine.

\section{Inverse Trigonometric Functions}
\label{ch04:inverse}

Now reverse the question: ``given the coordinate, find the angle.'' If you know that a point on the unit circle has height $\sin t = 0.5$, which $t$ produced it? This is the job of the inverse trig functions. First there is an obstacle to deal with.

\subsection{The Obstacle: Periodic Functions Are Not One-to-One}

A function has an inverse only if it is \textbf{one-to-one}: each output comes from exactly one input. Graphically, this is the horizontal line test from Chapter~2: no horizontal line may cross the graph more than once, or else that output traces back to several inputs and ``the inverse'' is ambiguous.

Sine fails this test badly. The horizontal line $y = 0.5$ slices through the sine graph infinitely often: $\sin t = 0.5$ at $t = \pi/6$, at $t = 5\pi/6$, at $t = -7\pi/6$, at $t = -11\pi/6$, and infinitely many other places (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-horizontal-line-test}). ``The angle whose sine is $0.5$'' has no single answer. A periodic function repeats, so by its nature it can never be globally invertible.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}

\begin{intuitionbox}
The solution is to restrict sine to a domain interval on which it never repeats a value -- a stretch where it is monotonic (moving in only one direction, strictly climbing or strictly falling, never reversing) and sweeps through its full range exactly once. On that restricted piece the inverse exists and is unique. We cannot invert all of sine; we invert one chosen slice of it (called the \emph{principal branch}), and keep track of which slice we chose.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Arcsine: Choosing the Principal Branch}

For sine, the agreed-upon slice is the interval $[-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}]$. Why this one? On it, sine climbs monotonically from $\sin(-\tfrac\pi2) = -1$ up to $\sin(\tfrac\pi2) = 1$, hitting every value in $[-1,1]$ exactly once. It contains $0$, it is symmetric about $0$, and it is the piece where sine rises through zero with slope $1$. This restricted, invertible sine has an inverse called the \textbf{arcsine}.

\begin{definition}[Arcsine]
\label{ch04:def-arcsin}
For $-1 \le x \le 1$, $\arcsin x$ (also written $\sin^{-1}x$) is the unique angle $\theta \in [-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}]$ with $\sin\theta = x$. Its domain is $[-1,1]$ (the possible sine values) and its range is $[-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}]$ (the chosen slice of angles). The name \emph{arc}sine reflects exactly what it returns: the \emph{arc} (angle) belonging to a given coordinate.
\end{definition}

\begin{trapbox}
The superscript notation $\sin^{-1}x$ causes many mistakes. It does \textbf{not} mean $1/\sin x$; that is $\csc x$ or $(\sin x)^{-1}$. Here the ``$-1$'' means \emph{the inverse function}, not a reciprocal power. The clash with $\sin^2 x = (\sin x)^2$ is a historical inconsistency in the notation. When in doubt, write $\arcsin x$; it cannot be misread.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: undoing sine.}
What is $\arcsin(\sin(\pi/6))$? Because $\pi/6$ lies inside the home interval $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$, the two operations cancel cleanly: $\arcsin(\sin(\pi/6)) = \pi/6$. Now what is $\arcsin(\sin(5\pi/6))$? Watch the range carefully: the sine of $5\pi/6$ is $0.5$, and arcsine can \emph{only} return angles in $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$. Looking at that interval, the unique angle with sine $0.5$ is $\pi/6$. So $\arcsin(\sin(5\pi/6)) = \pi/6 \neq 5\pi/6$. The inverse undoes sine only on its home branch.
\end{recoverybox}

The graph of an inverse function is the \emph{reflection} of the original across the line $y=x$ -- because inverting a function swaps the roles of input and output, which swaps the axes. So $\arcsin$ is the restricted sine curve flipped over the diagonal.

\subsection{Arccosine and Arctangent}

The same restriction principle defines the other inverses; only the chosen interval differs, each picked to be a monotonic stretch covering the full range once.

\textbf{Arccosine.} Cosine \emph{decreases} from $1$ to $-1$ on $[0,\pi]$, sweeping its whole range once, so that is the slice. Define $\arccos x$ for $x\in[-1,1]$ as the unique $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ with $\cos\theta = x$ (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-arccos}). Its range starts at $0$, not $-\tfrac\pi2$: a different function needs a different home interval. A clean identity ties the two together: if $\alpha = \arcsin x$, then $\alpha\in[-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2]$ and $\sin\alpha = x$, so $\tfrac\pi2 - \alpha$ lies in $[0,\pi]$ and satisfies $\cos(\tfrac\pi2 - \alpha) = \sin\alpha = x$. By definition of arccosine, $\arccos x = \tfrac\pi2 - \alpha$, which rearranges to
\[
  \arcsin x + \arccos x = \frac{\pi}{2}\qquad\text{for all }x\in[-1,1].
\]
The arcsine and arccosine of the same number are complementary angles: the ``co-'' relationship again, now read backwards through the inverses.

\textbf{Arctangent.} Tangent runs monotonically from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ on the open interval $(-\tfrac\pi2, \tfrac\pi2)$, so that is its slice. Define $\arctan x$ for \emph{every} real $x$ as the unique $\theta\in(-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2)$ with $\tan\theta = x$. Because tangent's range is all of $\R$, arctangent accepts every real input. Its graph has two horizontal asymptotes, at $y = \pm\tfrac\pi2$: feed in an enormous number and the angle approaches, but never reaches, a quarter turn.

\begin{happeningbox}
What an inverse trig function returns is a \emph{location on the circle}, reported as the arc length from $(1,0)$, but \emph{only the location within the chosen branch}. When your calculator says $\arcsin(0.5) = \pi/6$, it is making a choice: it reports the one answer in $[-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2]$ and discards the equally valid $5\pi/6$ and all the answers $\pi/6 + 2\pi k$ and $5\pi/6 + 2\pi k$, where $k$ is any integer (positive, negative or zero). So solving $\sin t = 0.5$ in full requires you to add back what the inverse threw away: $t = \pi/6 + 2\pi k$ \emph{and} $t = 5\pi/6 + 2\pi k$ (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-two-solutions}). The inverse gives you one root; the symmetries of the circle give you the rest.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{whybox}
Why must we restrict the domain at all, rather than ``allowing'' a multivalued inverse? Because in calculus we need a true \emph{function} -- one output per input -- so that we can graph it, differentiate it, and feed it into the chain rule. A function that returns infinitely many answers cannot have a single well-defined derivative at a point. By restricting sine to a single monotonic branch, we obtain a well-defined, differentiable, single-valued inverse. The cost is that the inverse only ever reports angles in one limited window; we accept that cost because differentiability is essential for calculus, and because the discarded solutions are easy to recover from periodicity and symmetry.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Computing With Inverses: the Triangle Trick}

A common task is to simplify a composition like $\cos(\arcsin x)$. The expression mixes an angle-finder with a coordinate-finder, and a right-triangle picture untangles it.

\begin{example}[Simplify $\cos(\arcsin x)$]
\label{ch04:ex-triangle}
Let $\theta = \arcsin x$, so $\sin\theta = x$ and, by definition of the arcsine branch, $\theta\in[-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2]$. We want $\cos\theta$. Build a right triangle in which $\sin\theta = x$: opposite side $x$, hypotenuse $1$ (so that opposite/hypotenuse $= x$). By Pythagoras the adjacent side is $\sqrt{1 - x^2}$ (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-triangle-trick}). Then
\[
  \cos(\arcsin x) = \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \sqrt{1 - x^2}.
\]
We take the \emph{positive} root because $\theta$ lies in $[-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2]$, where cosine is never negative. The branch we chose for arcsine is exactly what resolves the sign ambiguity -- another reason the restriction matters.
\end{example}

Try the same trick on $\tan(\arccos x)$. Set $\theta=\arccos x$, so $\cos\theta = x$ with adjacent $=x$, hypotenuse $=1$, hence opposite $=\sqrt{1-x^2}$. Then $\tan\theta = \text{opposite}/\text{adjacent} = \sqrt{1-x^2}/x$. Sketch the triangle yourself and confirm each side; the picture does all the work.
\end{labbox}

\section{Why Radians Make Calculus Clean: The Payoff}
\label{ch04:why-radians}

Why radians? Every step of the argument in this chapter leads back to that choice; Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-radian-chain} lays the steps out in order.

We \emph{defined} radians so that arc length equals angle ($s = r\theta$, and $\theta$ itself on the unit circle). That choice made the arc above $(1,0)$ have length exactly $t$. That, in turn, forced the squeeze $\cos t < \tfrac{\sin t}{t} < 1$, hence $\sin t/t \to 1$, hence $\sin t \approx t$ for small $t$. And \emph{that} approximation is, almost word for word, the statement that the derivative of $\sin t$ at $t=0$ equals $1$:
\[
  \left.\dv{}{t}\sin t\,\right|_{t=0} = \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\sin t - \sin 0}{t - 0} = \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\sin t}{t} = 1.
\]
With that, the central differentiation formulas $\dv{}{t}\sin t = \cos t$ and $\dv{}{t}\cos t = -\sin t$ (which we will prove rigorously in Chapter~12 once limits and derivative rules are established) fall out clean, with no stray constants. Had we used degrees, every one of these would carry a factor of $\pi/180 \approx 0.01745$, multiplying and compounding through every derivative, every integral, and every Taylor series. Radians are the \emph{unique} angle unit in which the calculus of trigonometry has no extra constant factors.

\begin{aestheticbox}
The right units or definitions can show that two things that looked different are the same quantity. Radians show that angle and arc length are one and the same quantity. In Chapter~3, you saw that Euler's number $e$ is the unique base that makes the starting slope of an exponential curve equal to $1$, so that the function equals its own rate of growth. Here, radians are the angle unit that makes the starting slope of $\sin t$ equal to $1$. In both cases, choosing the ``natural'' ruler makes a rate of change equal to $1$, and the formulas lose their stray constants.
\end{aestheticbox}

\subsection{Looking Ahead: The Limit}

Look again at the fundamental trig limit:
\[
  \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\sin t}{t} = 1.
\]
If you try to evaluate this expression at the destination itself, setting $t = 0$, you get
\[
  \frac{\sin 0}{0} = \frac{0}{0},
\]
which is undefined. Yet as $t \to 0$, the ratio heads
toward $1$. How can mathematics make rigorous sense of where a quantity is heading
when evaluating at the point is forbidden? In Chapter~5, we explore the idea and history of the
\emph{limit}.

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch04:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental checks: see it before you compute]
Test your geometric intuition on the circle before writing down calculations:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Without converting to degrees, in which quadrant does an angle of $4$ radians land? (Think: $\pi \approx 3.14$ and $3\pi/2 \approx 4.71$.)
  \item Which is larger: $\sin(1)$ or $\cos(1)$? (Hint: compare $1$ radian to $\pi/4 \approx 0.785$.)
  \item What is the sign of $\cos(3)$? Of $\sin(3)$?
  \item Can $\sin t = 1.05$ ever have a real solution? What about $\tan t = 105$?
  \item What is $\arcsin(\sin(0))$? What is $\arcsin(\sin(\pi))$? Explain why the second answer is not $\pi$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conversions and arc length]
(a) Convert to radians: $135^\circ$, $210^\circ$, $300^\circ$.
(b) Convert to degrees: $\tfrac{5\pi}{6}$, $\tfrac{7\pi}{4}$, $3$ radians (approximate).
(c) A circular arc has radius $4\,\text{cm}$ and subtends an angle of $\tfrac{2\pi}{3}$. Find its length and the area of the corresponding sector.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Reading the circle]
Without a calculator, give exact values of $\sin$, $\cos$, and $\tan$ at $t = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}$, $t = \tfrac{5\pi}{4}$, and $t = \tfrac{11\pi}{6}$. For each, state which quadrant the point lands in and use that to predict the signs \emph{before} computing.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Identities from symmetry]
Using only the unit-circle picture (reflections and rotations), explain why each holds:
(a) $\sin(\pi + t) = -\sin t$;\quad
(b) $\cos(\pi + t) = -\cos t$;\quad
(c) $\tan(\pi + t) = \tan t$;\quad
(d) $\sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + t) = \cos t$. Draw the relevant reflection or rotation for at least two of them.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Double and half angle]
(a) Starting from the addition formula for $\cos(a+b)$, derive $\cos 2a = 1 - 2\sin^2 a$.
(b) Use the half-angle formula to find the exact value of $\cos^2(\pi/8)$, and hence $\cos(\pi/8)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The fundamental limit at work]
Evaluate each limit, reducing it to $\lim_{t\to0}\sin t/t = 1$ (and its cousin $\tfrac{1-\cos t}{t}\to 0$):
(a) $\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\sin 5t}{t}$;\quad
(b) $\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\sin 3t}{\sin 7t}$;\quad
(c) $\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\tan t}{t}$;\quad
(d) $\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1 - \cos t}{t^2}$.
(Hint for (a): write $\tfrac{\sin 5t}{t} = 5\cdot\tfrac{\sin 5t}{5t}$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Inverse compositions]
Simplify to an algebraic expression in $x$ (state any restrictions on $x$):
(a) $\sin(\arccos x)$;\quad
(b) $\tan(\arcsin x)$;\quad
(c) $\cos(\arctan x)$;\quad
(d) $\sin(2\arctan x)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why the branch matters]
(a) Is it true that $\arcsin(\sin t) = t$ for \emph{every} real $t$? Test $t = \tfrac{3\pi}{4}$ and explain the result using the range of $\arcsin$.
(b) For which $t$ \emph{is} $\arcsin(\sin t) = t$ exactly true?
(c) Sketch the function $g(t) = \arcsin(\sin t)$ over $[-2\pi, 2\pi]$ and describe its shape.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Solving trig equations completely]
Find \emph{all} real solutions, using an inverse for one solution and the circle's symmetry for the rest:
(a) $2\sin t - 1 = 0$;\quad
(b) $\cos t = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$;\quad
(c) $\tan t = 1$. Express each answer as a family $t = (\ldots) + 2\pi k$ or $+\pi k$, $k\in\Z$ (here $\Z$ is the set of all integers $\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots$).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Prove the squeeze, carefully]
Reproduce the proof that $\lim_{t\to 0^+}\sin t/t = 1$ by comparing the areas of the inner triangle, the sector, and the outer triangle on the unit circle. Then explain why the two-sided limit follows, by arguing that $f(t) = \sin t/t$ is an even function.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A modeling problem]
The number of daylight hours at a mid-northern latitude is modeled by
\[
  D(t) = 12 + 3\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(t - 80)\right),
\]
where $t$ is the day of the year. (a) What are the amplitude, period, and midline, and what do they mean physically? (b) On which day is daylight longest? (c) For roughly how many days per year does $D(t) > 13.5$? (Use an inverse sine and the symmetry of the curve.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual: the missing constant]
Suppose a careless textbook \emph{defines} sine using degrees, so that $S(x) = \sin(x^\circ)$ where $x$ is a pure number of degrees. Using $\sin(x^\circ) = \sin\!\big(\tfrac{\pi}{180}x \text{ rad}\big)$ and the radian limit, compute $\lim_{x\to0} S(x)/x$. Explain in one or two sentences why this answer makes degrees a poor choice for calculus, connecting it to the eventual formula for the derivative of $S$.
\end{exercise}
