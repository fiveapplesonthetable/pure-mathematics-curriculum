\chapter{Partial Fractions and Trigonometric Integrals}

In the previous chapter, integration by parts let us trade a hard product for
an easier one by moving a derivative from one factor to the other. Together
with substitution, which runs the chain rule in reverse, it gave us two ways to
undo differentiation. Both rest on one idea, which this chapter makes into a
deliberate strategy: \emph{we rarely integrate the integrand exactly as it is
handed to us}. We rewrite it with algebra and trigonometry until it becomes
something we already recognize, such as a power, a logarithm or an arctangent,
and then we read off the answer.

We will meet two families of integrands that look hard at first and become
routine once you know how to rewrite them. The first is \emph{rational
functions}, ratios of polynomials like $\dfrac{3x+5}{x^2-x-2}$. For these we
break one complicated fraction into a sum of simple fractions, each of which
integrates to a logarithm, a power or an arctangent. The second is integrals
built from \emph{powers and products of sines and cosines}. After that comes a
method for square roots such as $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$: we replace $x$ by a
trigonometric function, chosen with the help of a right triangle, so that the
square root disappears.

Nothing in this chapter is new calculus: \textbf{every method in this chapter
rewrites the integrand into a known form.} As you work, keep asking:
\emph{which known form am I steering toward?}

\chapterline

\section{The Unifying Idea: Reshaping Into Known Forms}
\label{ch24:reshape}

These are the integrals we treat as ``known.'' Everything in this chapter
lands on one of them.

\begin{focusbox}
The standard forms we steer toward (omitting $+C$ for brevity):
\[
\int x^n\,\dd x = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\;(n\neq -1),
\qquad
\int \frac{\dd x}{x} = \ln\abs{x},
\]
\[
\int \frac{\dd x}{x^2 + a^2} = \frac{1}{a}\arctan\!\frac{x}{a},
\qquad
\int \frac{\dd x}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} = \arcsin\!\frac{x}{a},
\]
together with $\int e^x\,\dd x = e^x$, $\int \sin x\,\dd x = -\cos x$,
$\int \cos x\,\dd x = \sin x$, and whatever a single $u$-substitution can
reach from these. Here $n$ is any fixed number other than $-1$ and $a$ is a
positive constant. Each technique below rewrites an unfamiliar integrand in
terms of these few forms.
\end{focusbox}

Why these particular forms? Because each is a derivative you have already
computed and reversed. $\ln\abs{x}$ is there because its derivative is $1/x$;
the absolute value is there because $\ln$ is defined only for positive inputs, and
$\dfrac{\dd}{\dd x}\ln\abs{x} = 1/x$ on both sides of zero. The arctangent is
there because $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd x}\arctan x = \dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$, and the
version with $a$ is that rule run through the substitution $u = x/a$. The
arcsine is there for the same reason, with
$\dfrac{\dd}{\dd x}\arcsin x = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The target list is short: powers, $1/x$, $1/(x^2+a^2)$, $1/\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$,
exponentials, and basic sines and cosines. Faced with a complicated rational
function or square root, we do not invent a new rule. We use algebraic and
trigonometric identities to break the integrand into a sum of those familiar
pieces, then integrate term by term.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{Rational Functions and the Promise of Partial Fractions}
\label{ch24:rational-intro}

A \emph{rational function} is a quotient of polynomials, $R(x) = P(x)/Q(x)$.
We would like to integrate any such thing. Two of them are already easy:
\[
\int \frac{\dd x}{x - 3} = \ln\abs{x-3} + C,
\qquad
\int \frac{\dd x}{(x-3)^2} = -\frac{1}{x-3} + C.
\]
Both are one substitution ($u = x-3$) away from a standard form. The trouble
starts when the denominator is a product of \emph{several} different factors,
as in
\[
\int \frac{3x + 5}{x^2 - x - 2}\,\dd x .
\]
The integrand as written is not a standard form. But the denominator factors:
$x^2 - x - 2 = (x-2)(x+1)$. Suppose,
for a moment, that we could write
\[
\frac{3x+5}{(x-2)(x+1)} \;=\; \frac{A}{x-2} + \frac{B}{x+1}
\]
for some constants $A$ and $B$. Then the integral would split into two pieces
we \emph{can} do:
\[
\int \frac{A}{x-2}\,\dd x + \int \frac{B}{x+1}\,\dd x
= A\ln\abs{x-2} + B\ln\abs{x+1} + C.
\]
If every rational function can be split into a sum of pieces like
$\dfrac{A}{x-r}$ (and a few related pieces we will meet), then integrating
rational functions is a solved problem: each piece integrates to a logarithm,
a power or an arctangent.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Partial fraction decomposition} applies to any \emph{proper} rational
function $P(x)/Q(x)$: one where the degree of the numerator (its highest power
of $x$) is strictly smaller than the degree of the denominator, much as the
fraction $3/5$ has a top smaller than its bottom. It states that $P(x)/Q(x)$
can be written as a sum of simple fractions, one group for each factor of
$Q(x)$. These simple fractions are the \emph{partial fractions}. The
decomposition turns an integral we cannot do directly into a sum of integrals
we can.
\end{focusbox}

Two questions need answers before we rely on this. Can we always \emph{find}
the constants $A$, $B$? And does the decomposition always \emph{exist}? We find
the constants first, and then prove that the decomposition always exists.

\subsection{Finding the constants}

Start from the proposed identity and clear the denominator by multiplying both
sides by $(x-2)(x+1)$:
\[
3x + 5 \;=\; A(x+1) + B(x-2).
\]
This must hold for \emph{every} $x$, so we may plug in any $x$ we like and
solve for the constants. The best choices are the values of $x$ that make a
factor zero.

Set $x = 2$: the $B$ term vanishes, leaving $3(2)+5 = A(2+1)$, so $11 = 3A$ and
$A = \tfrac{11}{3}$.

Set $x = -1$: the $A$ term vanishes, leaving $3(-1)+5 = B(-1-2)$, so $2 = -3B$ and
$B = -\tfrac{2}{3}$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Plugging in the roots this way is called the \emph{cover-up method}, and the
name describes a shortcut. To find the coefficient sitting over
$(x-2)$, you cover up that factor in the original denominator and evaluate
what is left at $x = 2$:
\[
A = \left.\frac{3x+5}{x+1}\right|_{x=2} = \frac{11}{3},
\qquad
B = \left.\frac{3x+5}{x-2}\right|_{x=-1} = \frac{2}{-3} = -\frac{2}{3}.
\]
(The bar $\big|_{x=2}$ means ``evaluate at $x=2$.'') Why does covering up work? Multiplying by $(x-2)$ removes that factor from the
denominator, and then setting $x = 2$ makes every \emph{other} term vanish,
because each of them still carries a factor of $(x-2)$. You isolate one
coefficient by evaluating at the point where all the other terms vanish.
\end{intuitionbox}

So the decomposition is
\[
\frac{3x+5}{(x-2)(x+1)} = \frac{11/3}{x-2} - \frac{2/3}{x+1},
\]
and the integral follows:
\[
\int \frac{3x+5}{x^2-x-2}\,\dd x
= \frac{11}{3}\ln\abs{x-2} - \frac{2}{3}\ln\abs{x+1} + C.
\]

\begin{labbox}
Check the decomposition without integrating. Put $\dfrac{11/3}{x-2} -
\dfrac{2/3}{x+1}$ over the common denominator $(x-2)(x+1)$. The numerator is
\[
\tfrac{11}{3}(x+1) - \tfrac{2}{3}(x-2) = \tfrac{11}{3}x + \tfrac{11}{3}
- \tfrac{2}{3}x + \tfrac{4}{3} = 3x + 5.
\]
It matches. Partial fractions \emph{undoes} the algebra of putting fractions
over a common denominator, so the check is to redo that algebra forward.
\end{labbox}

\subsection{Why the decomposition must exist}
\label{ch24:why-pf-exists}

We found $A$ and $B$ by assuming they exist. We also need to know they
\emph{always} exist, for any denominator. The proof is short, and it also
explains why the cover-up method succeeds.

\begin{whybox}
We prove it in the main case, where the denominator
$Q(x) = (x-r_1)(x-r_2)\cdots(x-r_n)$ has $n$ \emph{distinct} real roots
$r_1, \ldots, r_n$ and the numerator $P$ has degree less than $n$. We claim
\[
\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = \frac{A_1}{x-r_1} + \frac{A_2}{x-r_2} + \cdots +
\frac{A_n}{x-r_n}
\]
for some constants $A_1, \ldots, A_n$. Why \emph{must} such constants exist?

It comes down to counting. Clearing denominators turns the claim into
\[
P(x) = A_1\,\frac{Q(x)}{x-r_1} + A_2\,\frac{Q(x)}{x-r_2} + \cdots +
A_n\,\frac{Q(x)}{x-r_n}.
\]
Each $\dfrac{Q(x)}{x-r_k}$ is $Q$ with one factor removed, a polynomial of
degree $n-1$; call these the \emph{building blocks}. A polynomial of degree at
most $n-1$ is fixed by $n$ numbers, its coefficients of $1, x, \ldots,
x^{n-1}$. So matching the two sides means solving $n$ equations (one per
coefficient) for $n$ unknowns $A_1, \ldots, A_n$. A system with as many
equations as unknowns can fail in only one way: the building blocks might be
redundant, with some combination of them (not all multipliers zero) adding up
to the zero polynomial. If no such combination exists, the blocks are called
\emph{linearly independent}: none of them can be built from the others, so each
adds a new direction of freedom. A basic fact of linear algebra (the study of
such systems of linear equations) is that $n$ independent blocks, each described
by $n$ numbers, combine to give every polynomial of degree at most $n-1$, each
in exactly one way. So \emph{every} allowed $P$ can be reached.

They are independent, and the cover-up trick proves it. Suppose a combination
$\sum_k c_k \dfrac{Q(x)}{x-r_k}$ (with constants $c_k$) is the zero polynomial.
Evaluate at $x = r_j$. Every term with $k \neq j$ still contains the factor
$(x - r_j)$ and so vanishes there. The surviving $k=j$ term equals
$c_j \prod_{m\neq j}(r_j - r_m)$, where $\prod$ means ``multiply together'' (here
over all $m$ other than $j$). This product of nonzero numbers is nonzero because
the roots are distinct. For the whole thing to be zero we need $c_j = 0$. This
holds for every $j$, so all the multipliers are zero: the building blocks are
independent. \textbf{The decomposition exists, and it is unique.}
\end{whybox}

The proof also explains the cover-up method: evaluating at the root $x = r_j$
isolates $A_j$ for the same reason it shows the building blocks cannot cancel
each other.

\begin{connectionbox}
This is an instance of a central fact of linear algebra: if every target is
described by $n$ numbers and you have $n$ independent building blocks, then
every target is a combination of the blocks in exactly one way. Calculus uses
this counting argument often, to guarantee that a representation exists before
computing it. Matching $n+1$ derivative values at a point determines a unique
Taylor polynomial of degree $n$ (the tangent line of Chapter~14 is the case
$n=1$), and $n+1$ data points with different $x$-values determine
a unique polynomial of degree at most $n$ passing through them.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{historybox}
In 1702, writing in \emph{Acta Eruditorum} (a scientific journal published in
Leipzig), Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz considered
whether every rational function could be integrated into logarithms and arctangents.
He believed he had found a counterexample in the fraction
\[
  \frac{1}{x^4 + 1}.
\]
Factoring over the complex numbers (numbers $a+bi$ with $i^2=-1$, met in
Chapter~12) gives $(x^2 + i)(x^2 - i)$. Because $\pm i$ are
imaginary, Leibniz argued that these factors could not be combined into real
quadratic polynomials, concluding that $x^4 + 1$ could not be factored over the
reals and that its integral could not be expressed with real logarithms or arctangents.

The flaw in Leibniz's reasoning is that the complex roots of a polynomial with
real coefficients come in \emph{conjugate pairs} $a+bi$ and $a-bi$ (same real
part, opposite sign on the $i$ part), and the two factors
$(x-(a+bi))(x-(a-bi)) = (x-a)^2+b^2$ multiply to a real quadratic. In 1719
Nicolaus Bernoulli exhibited the real factorization explicitly:
\[
  x^4 + 1 = (x^2 + 1)^2 - 2x^2 = (x^2 + \sqrt{2}x + 1)(x^2 - \sqrt{2}x + 1),
\]
and Leonhard Euler's 1748 textbook \emph{Introductio in analysin infinitorum}
developed the conjugate-pairing principle in general.
Both quadratic factors are real, and $\int \frac{\dd x}{x^4+1}$ splits into standard
partial fractions. The general principle was settled in 1799, when Carl Friedrich
Gauss proved the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (every polynomial has a complex
root). Combined with conjugate pairing, it implies that every real polynomial
factors completely into real linear factors and real \emph{irreducible}
quadratic factors, meaning quadratics with no real root.
\end{historybox}

\section{The Four Cases of Partial Fractions}
\label{ch24:four-cases}

To decompose \emph{any} proper rational function you handle the denominator's
factors one type at a time. As the box above says, a polynomial with real
coefficients factors completely into linear factors $(x-r)$ and irreducible
quadratics $(x^2+px+q)$ (those with no real root, such as $x^2+1$), each
possibly repeated. That gives exactly four kinds of pieces.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The setup rules.} First make the fraction \emph{proper} (numerator
degree $<$ denominator degree) by polynomial long division (Chapter~9) if
needed. Then for each factor of $Q(x)$, add terms:
\begin{itemize}
\item Distinct linear factor $(x-r)$: one term $\dfrac{A}{x-r}$.
\item Repeated linear factor $(x-r)^m$: a full tower
   $\dfrac{A_1}{x-r} + \dfrac{A_2}{(x-r)^2} + \cdots + \dfrac{A_m}{(x-r)^m}$.
\item Distinct irreducible quadratic $(x^2+px+q)$: one term
   $\dfrac{Bx+C}{x^2+px+q}$ (a linear numerator, because the denominator is
   degree two).
\item Repeated irreducible quadratic $(x^2+px+q)^m$: a tower
   $\dfrac{B_1x+C_1}{x^2+px+q} + \cdots + \dfrac{B_mx+C_m}{(x^2+px+q)^m}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

Students often forget the lower powers in the rule for repeated factors, so
here is why they are needed.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why must $(x-r)^3$ contribute three terms, $\dfrac{A_1}{x-r} +
\dfrac{A_2}{(x-r)^2} + \dfrac{A_3}{(x-r)^3}$, and not only the last one? Because
you need enough free constants. A numerator over $(x-r)^3$ can be \emph{any}
quadratic, and any quadratic can be rewritten in powers of $(x-r)$ as
$a + b(x-r) + c(x-r)^2$. Dividing that by $(x-r)^3$ gives
exactly $\dfrac{a}{(x-r)^3} + \dfrac{b}{(x-r)^2} + \dfrac{c}{x-r}$. The three
terms carry the three coefficients of that quadratic. Drop any one and you
lose a constant you might need to match the original numerator.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Why a quadratic factor gets a linear numerator}

Over an irreducible quadratic the numerator is $Bx + C$, not a constant.
The reason is the same counting: a piece $\dfrac{N(x)}{x^2+px+q}$ is
proper as long as $\deg N < 2$, so the most general allowed numerator has
\emph{two} free constants, $B$ and $C$. Using only a constant would throw away
one of them, and in general the equations for the constants would have no
solution.

\begin{trapbox}
The three classic errors:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Forgetting to do long division first when the fraction is
   \emph{improper} (not proper). $\dfrac{x^3}{x^2-1}$ is not proper; you must divide to get
   $x + \dfrac{x}{x^2-1}$ before decomposing.
\item Writing only $\dfrac{A}{(x-r)^m}$ for a repeated factor and omitting the
   lower powers. The tower must be complete.
\item Putting a constant $A$ over an irreducible quadratic instead of $Bx+C$.
\end{enumerate}
Each of these makes the equations for the constants unsolvable. That is a
useful early warning: if your constants come out inconsistent, suspect a
malformed setup.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and predict.} Look at $\dfrac{2x+5}{(x-3)(x^2+1)}$. Before touching any algebra:
how many unknown constants will you need to solve for, and what kinds of functions will appear
in the final antiderivative?
The denominator degree is $1 + 2 = 3$, so you need three constants ($A$ over $x-3$, and $Bx+C$
over $x^2+1$). The linear factor will produce a logarithm $\ln\abs{x-3}$; the irreducible quadratic
will split into a logarithm $\ln(x^2+1)$ and an arctangent $\arctan x$. Predicting the final shape
before starting keeps you oriented throughout the algebra.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Worked example: a repeated linear factor}

\begin{example}[Repeated root]
\label{ch24:ex-repeated}
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \frac{x + 4}{(x-1)^2(x+2)}\,\dd x.$
\end{example}

The denominator has a repeated factor $(x-1)^2$ and a single factor $(x+2)$,
so the setup is
\[
\frac{x+4}{(x-1)^2(x+2)} = \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{B}{(x-1)^2} + \frac{D}{x+2}.
\]
(We skip the letter $C$ to keep it free for the constant of integration.)
Clear denominators:
\[
x + 4 = A(x-1)(x+2) + B(x+2) + D(x-1)^2.
\]
Use the cover-up values for the easy constants. Set $x = 1$: $1 + 4 = B(3)$, so
$B = \tfrac{5}{3}$. Set $x = -2$: $-2 + 4 = D(-3)^2$, so $2 = 9D$ and $D =
\tfrac{2}{9}$. The constant $A$ has no root to cover up (it sits over the
\emph{first} power of a repeated factor), so match one more coefficient. The
$x^2$ coefficient on the left is $0$; on the right it is $A + D$. Hence $A =
-D = -\tfrac{2}{9}$.

So
\[
\frac{x+4}{(x-1)^2(x+2)} = \frac{-2/9}{x-1} + \frac{5/3}{(x-1)^2}
+ \frac{2/9}{x+2},
\]
and integrating term by term,
\[
\int \frac{x+4}{(x-1)^2(x+2)}\,\dd x
= -\frac{2}{9}\ln\abs{x-1} - \frac{5}{3(x-1)} + \frac{2}{9}\ln\abs{x+2} + C.
\]
The middle term integrates to a power, not a logarithm: the integral of
$1/(x-1)^2$ is $-1/(x-1)$. Repeated factors produce these non-logarithm
pieces, which is why their tower matters.

\subsection{Worked example: an irreducible quadratic}

\begin{example}[Quadratic factor]
\label{ch24:ex-quadratic}
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \frac{2x^2 - x + 4}{x(x^2+4)}\,\dd x.$
\end{example}

Set up with a linear numerator over the quadratic:
\[
\frac{2x^2 - x + 4}{x(x^2+4)} = \frac{A}{x} + \frac{Bx + C}{x^2 + 4}.
\]
Clear denominators:
\[
2x^2 - x + 4 = A(x^2+4) + (Bx + C)x.
\]
Set $x = 0$:
$4 = 4A$, so $A = 1$. Match $x^2$: $2 = A + B$, so $B = 1$. Match $x^1$:
$-1 = C$. Thus
\[
\frac{2x^2-x+4}{x(x^2+4)} = \frac{1}{x} + \frac{x - 1}{x^2+4}.
\]
Now integrate. The first piece is a logarithm. The second piece we
\emph{split} into a part whose numerator is the derivative of the denominator
and a leftover:
\[
\int \frac{x - 1}{x^2 + 4}\,\dd x
= \int \frac{x}{x^2+4}\,\dd x - \int \frac{1}{x^2+4}\,\dd x.
\]
For the first, $u = x^2 + 4$, $\dd u = 2x\,\dd x$, giving
$\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(x^2+4)$. The second is a standard arctangent with $a = 2$:
$\tfrac{1}{2}\arctan(x/2)$. Altogether,
\[
\int \frac{2x^2-x+4}{x(x^2+4)}\,\dd x
= \ln\abs{x} + \frac{1}{2}\ln(x^2+4) - \frac{1}{2}\arctan\!\frac{x}{2} + C.
\]

\begin{happeningbox}
The quadratic piece became \emph{a logarithm plus an arctangent}. This happens
to every irreducible-quadratic term. The $Bx$ part of the
numerator, after you write it as a multiple of the denominator's derivative
$2x$, integrates to $\ln(x^2+4)$ by substitution. The leftover constant part
has no $x$ on top, so there is nothing to substitute, and it matches the
arctangent form instead. So for any irreducible quadratic, split the numerator
into a ``derivative-of-denominator'' part (giving a logarithm) and a constant
part (giving an arctangent).
\end{happeningbox}

\subsection{Worked example: improper, needing division first}

\begin{example}[Improper fraction]
\label{ch24:ex-improper}
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \frac{x^3}{x^2 - 1}\,\dd x.$
\end{example}

The numerator degree ($3$) exceeds the denominator degree ($2$), so do
polynomial long division first: $x^3 = x(x^2-1) + x$, hence
\[
\frac{x^3}{x^2-1} = x + \frac{x}{x^2-1}.
\]
Here $x$ is the \emph{quotient} and the numerator $x$ of the fraction is the
\emph{remainder}. The leftover $\dfrac{x}{x^2-1} = \dfrac{x}{(x-1)(x+1)}$ decomposes as
$\dfrac{1/2}{x-1} + \dfrac{1/2}{x+1}$ (cover-up: at $x=1$, $\tfrac{1}{2}$; at
$x=-1$, $\tfrac{-1}{-2} = \tfrac{1}{2}$). So
\begin{align*}
\int \frac{x^3}{x^2-1}\,\dd x
&= \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\ln\abs{x-1} + \frac{1}{2}\ln\abs{x+1} + C\\
&= \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\ln\abs{x^2-1} + C.
\end{align*}

\begin{walkbox}
Why can long division not be skipped? A proper fraction
$P/Q$ with $\deg P < \deg Q$ shrinks toward $0$ as $x\to\infty$. But
$x^3/(x^2-1) \approx x$ for large $x$, so it grows without bound
(Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-improper}). No sum of pieces like $\dfrac{A}{x-r}$, each
shrinking to zero, could reproduce that growth. The division removes the
polynomial part $x$ that carries the growth, leaving a proper remainder (which
does shrink to zero) for the partial fractions to handle.
\end{walkbox}

\section{Trigonometric Integrals: Powers and Products}
\label{ch24:trig-integrals}

Next come integrals built from powers and products of sines and cosines, such as
$\int \sin^5 x\,\dd x$, $\int \sin^2 x\cos^2 x\,\dd x$, or
$\int \sin 3x\cos 5x\,\dd x$. None is a standard form as written, but each
gives way to the same move: \emph{use a trig identity to rewrite the
integrand into something a substitution can finish}. The only tools are the
Pythagorean identity $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$, the double-angle formulas, and
the product-to-sum formulas, all stated below where they are used.

\subsection{Odd powers: peel off one factor}

\begin{example}[Odd power of sine]
\label{ch24:ex-odd}
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \sin^5 x\,\dd x.$
\end{example}

There is an \emph{odd} number of sines, so peel one off
and convert the rest to cosines using $\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x$. The lone sine
becomes the $\dd u$ for $u = \cos x$.
\[
\int \sin^5 x\,\dd x
= \int (\sin^2 x)^2 \sin x\,\dd x
= \int (1 - \cos^2 x)^2 \sin x\,\dd x.
\]
Substitute $u = \cos x$, $\dd u = -\sin x\,\dd x$:
\begin{align*}
\int (1 - \cos^2 x)^2 \sin x\,\dd x
&= -\int (1 - u^2)^2\,\dd u\\
&= -\int (1 - 2u^2 + u^4)\,\dd u\\
&= -\Big(u - \tfrac{2}{3}u^3 + \tfrac{1}{5}u^5\Big) + C.
\end{align*}
Back-substitute $u = \cos x$:
\[
\int \sin^5 x\,\dd x = -\cos x + \frac{2}{3}\cos^3 x - \frac{1}{5}\cos^5 x + C.
\]

\begin{whybox}
Why does peeling one factor work, and why does it need an \emph{odd} power?
Substitution $u=\cos x$ needs $\dd u = -\sin x\,\dd x$, so it uses up exactly
\emph{one} sine. After spending that one, the remaining sines must be
written in terms of $u=\cos x$, and $\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x$ does that, but
only for sines in pairs (\emph{even} amounts). So you need one sine to
spend on $\dd u$ and an even number left over to convert: an odd total. If the
power were even, after spending one sine you would have an odd number left, and
the last lone sine could not be converted, so the substitution stalls. Odd powers
work because they split as ``one for $\dd u$, the rest even.''
\end{whybox}

The same trick handles odd powers of cosine (peel one cosine, set $u=\sin x$)
and mixed products $\int \sin^m x\cos^n x\,\dd x$ \emph{whenever at least one
of $m,n$ is odd}: peel one factor off the odd one, convert the rest, substitute
for the other function.

\begin{example}[Mixed product, one odd power]
$\displaystyle \int \sin^2 x\cos^3 x\,\dd x.$ The cosine power is odd. Peel one
cosine, convert the rest with $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$, and set $u = \sin x$,
$\dd u = \cos x\,\dd x$:
\begin{align*}
\int \sin^2 x\cos^3 x\,\dd x
&= \int \sin^2 x\,(1-\sin^2 x)\cos x\,\dd x\\
&= \int u^2(1-u^2)\,\dd u\\
&= \frac{u^3}{3} - \frac{u^5}{5} + C\\
&= \frac{\sin^3 x}{3} - \frac{\sin^5 x}{5} + C.
\end{align*}
\end{example}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and predict.} What is the definite integral $\int_0^{2\pi} \sin^7 x\,\dd x$?
Before reaching for pencil and paper: sine oscillates symmetrically above and below
zero. Every positive value on $[0, \pi]$ is matched by the same value with a
minus sign on $[\pi, 2\pi]$, because $\sin(x+\pi) = -\sin x$. Raising sine to an
odd power keeps the sign ($(-y)^7 = -y^7$), so the two halves cancel completely
(Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-sin7}): the integral is $0$ by symmetry alone.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Even powers: the half-angle identities}

When \emph{both} powers are even, the peel-one trick fails: there is no spare
factor to serve as $\dd u$. Instead we lower the exponent with the
power-reduction identities, also called half-angle identities because $x$ is
half of the angle $2x$ on the right:
\[
\sin^2 x = \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2},
\qquad
\cos^2 x = \frac{1 + \cos 2x}{2}.
\]
They come from the double-angle formula $\cos 2x = \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x$ of
Chapter~4: replace $\cos^2 x$ by $1 - \sin^2 x$ (or $\sin^2 x$ by
$1-\cos^2 x$) and solve. Each application trades a squared sine or cosine for a
first-power cosine of double the angle: the exponent drops, and the wave
oscillates twice as fast. Repeat until nothing is squared.

\begin{example}[Even power]
\label{ch24:ex-even}
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \sin^2 x\,\dd x.$
\begin{align*}
\int \sin^2 x\,\dd x
&= \int \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}\,\dd x\\
&= \frac{1}{2}\Big(x - \frac{\sin 2x}{2}\Big) + C\\
&= \frac{x}{2} - \frac{\sin 2x}{4} + C.
\end{align*}
\end{example}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Here is a check on this answer. The average value of $\sin^2 x$ over a full
period is $\tfrac{1}{2}$, because $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$ and, by symmetry,
$\sin^2$ and $\cos^2$ have the same average, so each must average
$\tfrac{1}{2}$ (Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-sin2} shows this for $\sin^2 2x$). Our
antiderivative $\tfrac{x}{2} - \tfrac{\sin 2x}{4}$ grows like $\tfrac{x}{2}$
with a small wobble on top, so over a period it accumulates exactly
$\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot(\text{length})$, as an average of $\tfrac12$ requires. This
``$\sin^2$ averages to a half'' fact is the reason the RMS (root-mean-square,
Chapter~20) value of an alternating current is the peak over $\sqrt 2$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{example}[Both even]
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \sin^2 x\cos^2 x\,\dd x.$ The quickest route uses the double-angle identity
$\sin x\cos x = \tfrac{1}{2}\sin 2x$, so $\sin^2 x\cos^2 x =
\tfrac{1}{4}\sin^2 2x$. Then half-angle again on $\sin^2 2x$:
\begin{align*}
\int \tfrac{1}{4}\sin^2 2x\,\dd x
&= \frac{1}{4}\int \frac{1 - \cos 4x}{2}\,\dd x\\
&= \frac{x}{8} - \frac{\sin 4x}{32} + C.
\end{align*}
\end{example}

\subsection{Products of different frequencies}

For $\int \sin(mx)\cos(nx)\,\dd x$ with $m \neq n$ there are no powers at all,
only a product of two waves at different frequencies (the \emph{frequency} $m$
counts how many full cycles $\sin(mx)$ makes on $[0,2\pi]$). The rewriting tool
is the \emph{product-to-sum} identities:
\begin{align*}
\sin A\cos B &= \tfrac{1}{2}\big[\sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B)\big],\\
\cos A\cos B &= \tfrac{1}{2}\big[\cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B)\big],\\
\sin A\sin B &= \tfrac{1}{2}\big[\cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B)\big].
\end{align*}
Each follows by adding or subtracting two addition formulas from Chapter~4;
for example, adding $\sin(A+B) = \sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B$ and
$\sin(A-B) = \sin A\cos B - \cos A\sin B$ gives the first. A product of two
waves becomes a \emph{sum} of two waves, and a sum is easy to integrate term by
term.

\begin{example}
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \sin 3x\cos 5x\,\dd x$. With $A = 3x$, $B = 5x$:
\begin{align*}
\int \sin 3x\cos 5x\,\dd x
&= \frac{1}{2}\int \big[\sin 8x + \sin(-2x)\big]\,\dd x\\
&= \frac{1}{2}\int \big[\sin 8x - \sin 2x\big]\,\dd x\\
&= -\frac{\cos 8x}{16} + \frac{\cos 2x}{4} + C.
\end{align*}
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
These product-to-sum integrals are the main computation behind \textbf{Fourier
series}, which write a repeating signal as a sum of sines and cosines of
different frequencies. You can extract the ``amount of frequency $n$'' inside a
signal because $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin(mx)\sin(nx)\,\dd x = 0$ whenever $m \neq n$
(for whole numbers $m,n\ge 1$) and equals $\pi$ when $m = n$
(Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-orthogonal}). Two functions are called
\emph{orthogonal} when their product integrates to zero over a full period;
the word is borrowed from geometry, where it means ``perpendicular.'' The
product-to-sum identity explains the result: for $m\ne n$,
$\sin(mx)\sin(nx)$ becomes two cosines that each complete whole cycles on
$[0,2\pi]$ and so integrate to zero, while for $m=n$ it becomes
$\tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos(2nx)$, whose average is $\tfrac12$, giving
$\tfrac12\cdot 2\pi = \pi$. Audio compression and signal filtering rely on
this calculation.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Completing the Square: Taming Any Quadratic}
\label{ch24:complete-square}

Before trig substitution we need one algebraic move. Many
integrals hide a quadratic in the denominator or under a root that is not
quite a standard form, like
\[
\int \frac{\dd x}{x^2 + 4x + 13}.
\]
The denominator is not $x^2 + a^2$, so the arctangent form does not apply
directly. But \emph{completing the square} rewrites any quadratic as a
perfect square plus (or minus) a constant. Take half the coefficient of $x$
(here $\tfrac42 = 2$), square it ($4$), and add and subtract that number:
\[
x^2 + 4x + 13 = (x^2 + 4x + 4) + 9 = (x+2)^2 + 9 = (x+2)^2 + 3^2.
\]
Now substitute $u = x + 2$ (a pure shift, $\dd u = \dd x$) and it \emph{is} the
arctangent form with $a = 3$:
\[
\int \frac{\dd x}{(x+2)^2 + 3^2}
= \frac{1}{3}\arctan\!\frac{x+2}{3} + C.
\]

\begin{happeningbox}
Geometrically, completing the square is a \emph{horizontal shift}. The graph of
$y = x^2 + 4x + 13$ is the parabola $y = x^2 + 9$ shifted two units to the left
(Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-complete-square}). The substitution $u = x+2$ moves the
origin of the horizontal axis to the lowest point of the parabola, where the
quadratic is symmetric: $u^2 + 9$. Every quadratic $x^2 + bx + c$ is the
parabola $y=x^2$ shifted horizontally and vertically; completing the square
finds the shift, and the substitution undoes it. Once centered, the integrand
matches the standard arctangent form.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{example}[Linear numerator over a completed square]
\label{ch24:ex-complete}
$\displaystyle \int \frac{x + 1}{x^2 + 4x + 13}\,\dd x.$ Split the numerator into
a multiple of the denominator's derivative $2x+4$ plus a leftover constant:
$x + 1 = \tfrac{1}{2}(2x+4) - 1$. Then
\[
\int \frac{x+1}{x^2+4x+13}\,\dd x
= \frac{1}{2}\int \frac{2x+4}{x^2+4x+13}\,\dd x - \int \frac{\dd x}{x^2+4x+13}.
\]
The first integral is $\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(x^2+4x+13)$ by substitution; the second
is the arctangent we just did. So the answer is
\[
\frac{1}{2}\ln(x^2+4x+13) - \frac{1}{3}\arctan\!\frac{x+2}{3} + C.
\]
\end{example}

This ``log half plus arctangent half'' split is the same one used for the
irreducible-quadratic pieces in the partial fractions section.

\section{Trigonometric Substitution: Algebra Becomes Geometry}
\label{ch24:trig-sub}

The last technique is the most geometric one in the chapter. Integrals
containing $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$, $\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}$, or $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}$ resist
ordinary substitution because the square root has no convenient derivative
hiding in the integrand. The way out is to \emph{change variables so that the
expression under the root becomes a perfect square}, using the Pythagorean
identities. A right triangle shows which substitution to use and how to get
back to $x$ afterwards.

\subsection{The idea on a triangle}

Consider $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$. The relation $a^2 = x^2 + (\sqrt{a^2-x^2})^2$ is
the Pythagorean theorem: $a$ is a hypotenuse, $x$ and $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ are the
two legs. So \emph{draw} that triangle (Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-triangle}(a)) and
let $\theta$ be the angle whose \emph{opposite leg} is $x$:
\[
\sin\theta = \frac{x}{a} \;\Longrightarrow\; x = a\sin\theta,
\qquad
\sqrt{a^2 - x^2} = a\cos\theta.
\]
To make the substitution reversible, we restrict $\theta$ to the interval
$[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$, the same window used to define $\arcsin$ in Chapter~4, so
that $\theta = \arcsin(x/a)$. On this interval cosine is never negative, so
$\sqrt{\cos^2\theta} = \abs{\cos\theta} = \cos\theta$ and no absolute-value sign
is needed.

Each is chosen so that the Pythagorean theorem turns the expression under the root into a perfect square: \textbf{(a)} $x=a\sin\theta$ gives $a^2-x^2 = a^2\cos^2\theta$; \textbf{(b)} $x=a\tan\theta$ gives $a^2+x^2 = a^2\sec^2\theta$; \textbf{(c)} $x=a\sec\theta$ gives $x^2-a^2 = a^2\tan^2\theta$. Reading ratios of sides off the triangle translates any trig function of $\theta$ back into $x$.}
\label{ch24:fig-triangle}
\end{figure}

The reference triangle tracks the relationships among the sides. It tells
you the substitution ($x = a\sin\theta$), it tells you $\dd x = a\cos\theta\,
\dd\theta$, it converts the root to $a\cos\theta$, and most importantly, it tells
you how to convert the \emph{answer} back to $x$ at the end, because every trig
function of $\theta$ is a ratio of the triangle's sides.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The three triangles.}
\begin{itemize}
\item $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$: set $x = a\sin\theta$, so the root becomes $a\cos\theta$
   (using $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$).
\item $\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}$: set $x = a\tan\theta$ with $\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$, so
   $\sec\theta > 0$ and the root becomes $a\sec\theta$ (using $1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta$).
\item $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}$: for $x \ge a$, set $x = a\sec\theta$ with
   $\theta\in[0,\pi/2)$, so $\tan\theta\ge 0$ and the root becomes $a\tan\theta$
   (using $\sec^2\theta - 1 = \tan^2\theta$; for $x\le -a$, check the sign of $\tan\theta$).
\end{itemize}
In each case the substitution is the one that makes a Pythagorean identity
collapse the root into a single trig function. Draw the triangle and you never
have to memorize which is which.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{whybox}
Why does turning $x$ into a trig function help at all? Because the obstacle is
the square root, and a square root vanishes only when its inside is a perfect
square. The Pythagorean identities are precisely the algebraic statements that
$a^2 - (a\sin\theta)^2$, $a^2 + (a\tan\theta)^2$, and $(a\sec\theta)^2 - a^2$
are each a perfect square ($a^2\cos^2\theta$, $a^2\sec^2\theta$,
$a^2\tan^2\theta$). Trig substitution is the deliberate choice of a new
variable that makes the thing under the root a square, so the root can be
taken exactly.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Worked example: the area of a circle, recovered}

\begin{example}[Root of $a^2 - x^2$]
\label{ch24:ex-circle}
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\,\dd x.$
\end{example}

This integrand is the height of a circle of radius $a$, so the integral is a
circular area (Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-semicircle}). Use the triangle:
$x = a\sin\theta$, $\dd x = a\cos\theta\,\dd\theta$, and
$\sqrt{a^2-x^2} = a\cos\theta$. The integral becomes pure trig:
\begin{align*}
\int \sqrt{a^2-x^2}\,\dd x
&= \int a\cos\theta\cdot a\cos\theta\,\dd\theta
= a^2\int \cos^2\theta\,\dd\theta\\
&= a^2\Big(\frac{\theta}{2} + \frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}\Big) + C,
\end{align*}
using the half-angle identity for $\cos^2\theta$ from before. Now convert back
via the triangle. From $\sin\theta = x/a$ we get $\theta = \arcsin(x/a)$, and
\[
\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta
= 2\cdot\frac{x}{a}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}{a}
= \frac{2x\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}{a^2}.
\]
Therefore
\[
\int \sqrt{a^2-x^2}\,\dd x
= \frac{a^2}{2}\arcsin\!\frac{x}{a} + \frac{x}{2}\sqrt{a^2 - x^2} + C.
\]

\begin{aestheticbox}
Test this on a whole circle. The area inside $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$ is four times
the area under $y = \sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$ from $0$ to $a$:
\[
4\int_0^a \sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\,\dd x
= 4\left[\frac{a^2}{2}\arcsin\!\frac{x}{a} + \frac{x}{2}\sqrt{a^2-x^2}
\right]_0^a.
\]
At $x = a$: $\arcsin 1 = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$ and the square-root term is $0$. At
$x = 0$: everything is $0$. So the area is
\[
4\cdot\frac{a^2}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = \pi a^2,
\]
the familiar area of a disc, now derived from a triangle substitution.

The two terms of the antiderivative also have a geometric meaning
(Figure~\ref{ch24:fig-sector}). For $0 \le x \le a$, the area under the circle
from $0$ to $x$ splits into a right triangle with legs $x$ and
$\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$, of area $\tfrac{x}{2}\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$, and a circular sector
(a pie slice) of radius $a$ and angle $\theta = \arcsin(x/a)$. A slice of angle
$\theta$ is the fraction $\theta/(2\pi)$ of the whole disc, so its area is
$\tfrac{\theta}{2\pi}\cdot\pi a^2 = \tfrac{a^2}{2}\theta$. The $\arcsin$ in the
answer is the angle of that slice.
\end{aestheticbox}

\subsection{Worked example: a secant integral via the tangent triangle}

\begin{example}[Root of $a^2 + x^2$]
\label{ch24:ex-tan}
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \frac{\dd x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 9}}.$
\end{example}

Here $a = 3$ and the root is $\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}$, so we use the tangent triangle
with $\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$: $x = 3\tan\theta$, $\dd x = 3\sec^2\theta\,\dd\theta$,
and $\sqrt{x^2+9} = 3\sec\theta$. The integral simplifies to
\[
\int \frac{3\sec^2\theta}{3\sec\theta}\,\dd\theta
= \int \sec\theta\,\dd\theta.
\]
To evaluate $\int\sec\theta\,\dd\theta$, multiply top and bottom by
$\sec\theta + \tan\theta$. The new numerator $\sec^2\theta + \sec\theta\tan\theta$ is
exactly the derivative of the new denominator $w = \sec\theta + \tan\theta$
(because $\tfrac{\dd}{\dd\theta}\sec\theta = \sec\theta\tan\theta$ and
$\tfrac{\dd}{\dd\theta}\tan\theta = \sec^2\theta$), so the
$\int\frac{\dd w}{w}$ pattern finishes it:
\[
\int \sec\theta\,\dd\theta
= \int \frac{\sec^2\theta + \sec\theta\tan\theta}{\sec\theta + \tan\theta}\,\dd\theta
= \ln\abs{\sec\theta + \tan\theta} + C.
\]
Convert back with the triangle. With opposite $= x$, adjacent $= 3$,
hypotenuse $= \sqrt{x^2+9}$, we have $\tan\theta = x/3$ and $\sec\theta =
\sqrt{x^2+9}/3$. So
\[
\int \frac{\dd x}{\sqrt{x^2+9}}
= \ln\left|\frac{\sqrt{x^2+9}}{3} + \frac{x}{3}\right| + C
= \ln\big|x + \sqrt{x^2+9}\big| + C',
\]
absorbing the constant $-\ln 3$ into a new constant $C'$. (Chapter~2 met the
hyperbolic sine $\sinh x = \tfrac12(e^x - e^{-x})$. Its inverse function,
written $\operatorname{arcsinh}$, works out to
$\operatorname{arcsinh} y = \ln\bigl(y + \sqrt{y^2+1}\bigr)$, so this answer
is also $\operatorname{arcsinh}(x/3)$ plus a constant. Hyperbolic functions
handle $\sqrt{x^2+a^2}$ the way sine and cosine handle $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$.)

\begin{trapbox}
The single most forgotten step in trig substitution is \textbf{converting back
to $x$}. Your answer must be a function of the original variable; an answer
left in $\theta$ is unfinished. Always draw the triangle, label its three
sides from the substitution, and read off whatever trig functions appear in
your antiderivative as ratios of those sides.

A second trap: when the integral is \emph{definite}, you may instead change the
limits to $\theta$-values and never convert back, which is often cleaner. Pick
one approach and follow it through.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{When the root hides inside a shifted quadratic}

Completing the square and trig substitution combine to handle the general
quadratic-under-a-root. For $\int \dfrac{\dd x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 4x + 13}}$, first
complete the square: $x^2 + 4x + 13 = (x+2)^2 + 9$. Substitute $u = x+2$ to
center it, giving $\int \dfrac{\dd u}{\sqrt{u^2 + 9}}$, which is exactly the
tangent-triangle integral above. Reshape (complete the square), then reshape
again (trig substitution): the chapter's whole theme in one problem.

\begin{labbox}
Do this one by hand. Integrate
$\displaystyle \int \frac{\dd x}{\sqrt{5 - 4x - x^2}}.$
\begin{enumerate}
\item Complete the square inside the root:
   \[
   5 - 4x - x^2 = -(x^2 + 4x) + 5 = -\big((x+2)^2 - 4\big) + 5 = 9 - (x+2)^2.
   \]
\item Substitute $u = x+2$: the integral is $\displaystyle\int
   \frac{\dd u}{\sqrt{9 - u^2}}$, a standard arcsine with $a = 3$.
\item Read off $\arcsin(u/3) = \arcsin\!\dfrac{x+2}{3} + C$.
\end{enumerate}
You did not need the triangle here: once centered, the integral was a
standard form. Recognizing a standard form is always the fastest path; the
triangle is the fallback when none fits.
\end{labbox}

\section{One Worldview, Several Tools}
\label{ch24:synthesis}

All of the techniques in this chapter share a single strategy.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Every technique reshapes the integrand into a known form.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Partial fractions}: a ratio of polynomials $\to$ a sum of
   $\dfrac{A}{(x-r)^k}$ and $\dfrac{Bx+C}{(x^2+px+q)^k}$, each a log, a power,
   or an arctangent.
\item \emph{Trig powers (odd)}: peel a factor $\to$ a polynomial in $\sin$ or
   $\cos$, finished by substitution.
\item \emph{Trig powers (even)}: half-angle identities $\to$ lower powers and
   doubled angles, repeated to the end.
\item \emph{Products of waves}: product-to-sum $\to$ a sum of single waves.
\item \emph{Completing the square}: a general quadratic $\to$ a centered
   $u^2 \pm a^2$, matching the arctangent or arcsine form.
\item \emph{Trig substitution}: a square root $\to$ a triangle, where the
   Pythagorean identity makes the root a perfect square.
\end{itemize}
The calculus underneath never changes. What changes is your fluency at
recognizing which rewrite exposes a known form.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
By the middle of the eighteenth century, Leonhard Euler had organized nearly
all of these techniques into one system. His textbook \emph{Institutiones
Calculi Integralis} (``Foundations of Integral Calculus,'' 1768) treats rational
functions by partial fractions, trig integrals by the identities, and
integrands containing roots by substitutions that turn them into rational
functions, including the trigonometric substitutions for roots of quadratics.
The idea Euler organized is the one we have followed: many integrals can be
evaluated not by inventing new operations but by \emph{transforming the
integrand into one of a short list of standard forms}. Much of the skill of
integration is recognizing which algebraic transformation exposes the standard
form.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
A small set of functions suffices here. Integrating rational functions never
requires anything beyond rational functions, logarithms, and arctangents,
while integrating square roots of quadratics adds at most square roots,
arcsines, and logarithms such as $\ln\abs{x+\sqrt{x^2+9}}$. In every case the
antiderivative stays within the \emph{elementary functions} of Chapter~21
(powers, roots, exponentials, logarithms, and trigonometric functions and their
inverses, combined by arithmetic and composition). By contrast, as Chapter~21
mentioned, Liouville proved in 1835 that integrands as simple as $e^{-x^2}$
have no elementary antiderivative at all.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Limitations and next steps}
\label{ch24:road-ahead}

\begin{connectionbox}
Partial fractions gives a step-by-step procedure for integrating any rational function
$P(x)/Q(x)$, provided the denominator $Q(x)$ can be factored into linear and quadratic terms.
Together with trigonometric substitution, it also handles many integrands containing
square roots of quadratics.

The method has two main limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Factoring high-degree denominators:} A \emph{formula in radicals}
  for the roots of a polynomial is one built from its coefficients using only
  $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$ and roots, like the quadratic formula. The
  Abel--Ruffini theorem (Ruffini 1799, with gaps; Abel 1824) says
  that general polynomials of degree $5$ or higher have no such formula. Unless
  we know the linear and quadratic factors of $Q(x)$ explicitly, we cannot
  write down the decomposition in closed form.
  \item \emph{Non-elementary antiderivatives:} Simple-looking integrands such as $e^{-x^2}$
  or $\sqrt{1+x^4}$ have no antiderivative expressible as a finite combination of powers,
  roots, exponentials, logarithms, and trigonometric functions.
\end{enumerate}

When no closed-form antiderivative exists, or when we know the integrand only from
measured data, \emph{numerical integration} (Chapter~27) computes definite integrals
directly from sample values. Before that, Chapter~25 extends integration to unbounded intervals
and vertical asymptotes (\emph{improper integrals}), and Chapter~26 applies integration
to geometric and physical quantities.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch24:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: Partial fraction setup and counting]
Without solving for any constants, write down the partial fraction decomposition template
for $\dfrac{3x^2 + 5}{(x-2)^2(x^2+9)}$. State how many total constants you must determine,
and predict which pieces will yield logarithms, which will yield powers, and which will
yield arctangents.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: Symmetry and parity in trig integrals]
Without computing any antiderivatives, determine the exact value of
$\displaystyle \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \sin^5 x \cos^4 x\,\dd x$, and explain why using the concept of odd functions.
Then predict the exact value of $\displaystyle \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^2 x\,\dd x$ using the average value of $\sin^2 x$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Distinct linear factors]
Decompose and integrate $\displaystyle \int \frac{5x - 4}{(x-1)(x+3)}\,\dd x.$
Use the cover-up method to find both constants, and verify your decomposition
by recombining over a common denominator.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Repeated factor]
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \frac{2x^2 + 3}{(x+1)^3}\,\dd x.$ Set up the full
tower of three terms, find the constants (try the substitution $u = x+1$ as an
alternative to matching coefficients), and identify which pieces become
logarithms and which become powers.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Irreducible quadratic]
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \frac{x^2 + x + 1}{x(x^2+1)}\,\dd x.$ Decompose
with a linear numerator over the quadratic, then split that piece into its
``log half'' and ``arctangent half'' as in
Example~\ref{ch24:ex-quadratic}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Improper fraction]
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \frac{x^2}{x^2 - 4}\,\dd x.$ First explain, in
one sentence, why long division is required before any decomposition, then
carry it out.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Odd trig power]
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \cos^3 x\,\dd x$ and
$\displaystyle \int \sin^3 x\cos^4 x\,\dd x.$ State which factor you peel and
why the substitution then closes.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Even trig powers]
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \cos^4 x\,\dd x$ by applying the half-angle
identity twice. Then, without computing, predict the average value of
$\cos^4 x$ over a full period and check that your antiderivative's linear part
agrees with it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Product of waves]
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \cos 2x\cos 6x\,\dd x$ using a product-to-sum
identity. Then explain, using your answer, why $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos 2x\cos 6x\,
\dd x = 0$, and connect this to the orthogonality of distinct frequencies.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Completing the square]
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \frac{\dd x}{x^2 - 6x + 25}$ and
$\displaystyle \int \frac{2x - 1}{x^2 - 6x + 25}\,\dd x.$ For the second, split
the numerator into a multiple of the denominator's derivative plus a constant.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trig substitution]
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \frac{x^2}{\sqrt{4 - x^2}}\,\dd x$ using the
sine triangle. Draw the triangle, convert every quantity, integrate the
resulting trig expression, and convert your answer back to $x$ explicitly.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Trig substitution, the other two triangles]
Integrate $\displaystyle \int \frac{\dd x}{x^2\sqrt{x^2 - 9}}$ using the secant
triangle, and $\displaystyle \int \sqrt{x^2 + 1}\,\dd x$ using the tangent
triangle. The second will need $\int \sec^3\theta\,\dd\theta$; derive it with
one integration by parts.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual / proof]
Prove that the partial-fraction building blocks $\dfrac{Q(x)}{x - r_k}$, where
$Q(x) = \prod_{i=1}^n (x - r_i)$ has $n$ distinct roots, are linearly
independent as polynomials. (Mimic the cover-up argument of
Section~\ref{ch24:why-pf-exists}.) Explain in a sentence why this independence
is exactly what guarantees the partial-fraction decomposition exists for every
proper numerator.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Synthesis]
The single integral $\displaystyle \int \frac{\dd x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 2x + 2}}$ uses
two techniques from this chapter in sequence. Name them in order, carry the
computation through to a function of $x$, and write one sentence explaining how
this problem embodies the chapter's slogan that every method reshapes the
integrand into a known form.
\end{exercise}
