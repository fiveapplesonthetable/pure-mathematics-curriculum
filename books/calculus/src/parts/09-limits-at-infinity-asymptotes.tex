\chapter{Limits at Infinity and Asymptotes}

In Chapter~8 we used limits to define \emph{continuity}: a graph with no
jumps or breaks, which let the Intermediate Value Theorem trap roots. All
those limits asked a \emph{local} question, one about a single spot: as $x$
moves toward some finite number $a$, where does $f(x)$ head?

This chapter asks a question about the whole far end of the graph instead.
\emph{Where does the function go in the long run?} Does the curve approach a
horizontal line? Does it grow without bound? Does it approach a slanted line?

The behavior of a curve as the input heads out toward positive or negative
infinity is called its \textbf{end behavior}, and the tool for describing it
is the \textbf{limit at infinity}. We will also study \textbf{asymptotes}:
straight lines that a curve gets arbitrarily close to, either as you travel far
along it or as you approach a point where the function grows without bound.
Asymptotes act like the outer frame of a graph: sketch them first, and you see
the curve's long-range shape before plotting a single point. At the end of the
chapter we use the same ideas to rank logarithms, powers and exponentials by
how fast they grow, a ranking that matters wherever large inputs do, from the
running time of computer programs to physics.

\section{What ``Limit at Infinity'' Means}
\label{ch09:meaning}

When we wrote $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ for a finite $a$, the symbol $x\to a$
meant ``$x$ gets arbitrarily close to $a$.'' But $\infty$ is not a number, and
$x$ cannot get \emph{close} to it --- there is nothing to get close to. So we
have to say precisely what the new symbol asserts.

\begin{happeningbox}
The statement $\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = L$ does \textbf{not} say that $x$
arrives anywhere. It is a promise about the long run:

\begin{center}
\emph{No matter how tight a tolerance you demand around $L$, I can name a
starting point far enough to the right so that, from there on, $f(x)$ stays
inside that tolerance.}
\end{center}

There is no last value of $x$, no destination. There is only the guarantee that
\emph{eventually and forever after}, the output is pinned near $L$. What
``$x\to\infty$'' adds is the word \emph{eventually}.
\end{happeningbox}

The precise version copies the $\eps$--$\delta$ definition of Chapter~6, with
``far to the right'' in place of ``close to $a$.'' As before, $\eps$ (epsilon)
is the tolerance, the allowed distance from $L$; the new letter $M$ is the
starting point.

\begin{definition}[Limit at infinity]
\label{ch09:def-limit-inf}
We write $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = L$ if: for every $\eps > 0$
there exists a real number $M$ such that
\[
   x > M \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \abs{f(x) - L} < \eps .
\]
Likewise $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x) = L$ if for every $\eps>0$ there
is an $M$ with $x < M \implies \abs{f(x)-L}<\eps$.
\end{definition}

Compare this with the finite-$a$ definition. There, $\eps$ bought you a $\delta$
and you looked inside the window $0<\abs{x-a}<\delta$. Here, $\eps$ buys you a
threshold $M$ and you look at the entire tail $x>M$. The picture is not a
shrinking window around a point; it is a horizontal corridor of half-width
$\eps$ around the height $L$, and the claim is that the graph enters that
corridor and never leaves.

\begin{labbox}
Show directly from Definition~\ref{ch09:def-limit-inf} that
$\lim_{x\to\infty} \tfrac{1}{x} = 0$. Given $\eps>0$, we need
$x>M \implies \abs{1/x - 0} < \eps$. For $x>0$ this condition unwinds as
\[
  \left\lvert \frac1x - 0 \right\rvert < \eps
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac1x < \eps
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x > \frac1\eps .
\]
So \emph{choose $M = 1/\eps$.} Then any $x>M$ satisfies $1/x < 1/M = \eps$. Done.
$M$ depends on $\eps$: demand a tolerance ten times tighter and the
threshold moves ten times farther out.
\end{labbox}

\subsection{Infinite limits: when the output runs away}

There is a second, different way for a function to head to infinity: the
\emph{output} can blow up while $x$ stays near a finite point. We already met
this with $1/x$ near $x=0$. The phrase ``$=\infty$'' needs a careful reading
too.

\begin{definition}[Infinite limit]
\label{ch09:def-inf-limit}
We write $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = +\infty$ if for every $N>0$ there is
a $\delta>0$ such that
\[
  0 < \abs{x-a} < \delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(x) > N .
\]
That is: name any height $N$, however large, and the graph climbs above it and
stays above it once $x$ is within $\delta$ of $a$. For $-\infty$, replace
``$f(x)>N$'' by ``$f(x)<-N$''; for $x\to\infty$, replace the window around $a$
by a tail $x>M$, as in Definition~\ref{ch09:def-limit-inf}.
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-inf-limit} draws this definition for $f(x)=1/(x-a)^2$: the
height $N$ is the challenge, and the window of half-width $\delta$ around $a$ is
the answer.

\begin{trapbox}
The notation $\lim f(x) = \infty$ is a \textbf{symbolic shorthand}. There is
no real number $\infty$ that the limit equals. Strictly, the limit \emph{does not
exist} as a real number. Writing ``$=\infty$'' records \emph{how} it fails to
exist: by growing without bound, rather than by oscillating or jumping. Treat
``$=\infty$'' as a description meaning ``increases beyond every bound,'' never
as an arithmetic value you can add or divide.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{historybox}
The struggle to make infinite behavior rigorous ran through
nineteenth-century analysis (the branch of mathematics that puts calculus on
exact foundations). Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz computed
with quantities that were ``ultimately'' vanishing or ``infinitely'' large,
and got correct physical results. But
nobody could say what an actual infinity or infinitesimal \emph{was}.

The repair came in stages. In $1817$, Bernard Bolzano in Prague began removing
vague notions of motion and time, defining continuity through static bounds. In
the $1820$s, Augustin-Louis Cauchy advanced this shift, framing limits as values
that variable quantities approach. Finally in Berlin during the $1860$s, Karl
Weierstrass supplied the complete modern grammar --- the $\eps$, the $\delta$,
and (for our chapter) the threshold $M$. Under Weierstrass's formulation, the
phrase ``$x\to\infty$'' contains no actual infinite number. It is an ordinary,
checkable inequality about finite quantities: given any target window $\eps > 0$,
any input $x > M$ keeps $\abs{f(x)-L} < \eps$. Definition~\ref{ch09:def-limit-inf}
is Weierstrass's way to talk about the infinite using only finite numbers.
\end{historybox}

\section{Horizontal Asymptotes}
\label{ch09:horizontal}

A horizontal asymptote is a finite limit at infinity, read off the graph.

\begin{definition}[Horizontal asymptote]
\label{ch09:def-ha}
The line $y=L$ is a \textbf{horizontal asymptote} of $f$ if
$\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = L$ or $\lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x) = L$ (or both).
\end{definition}

Three facts about horizontal asymptotes contradict common beliefs.

\begin{itemize}
\item A graph \textbf{may cross its horizontal asymptote}, even infinitely
often. The asymptote describes the \emph{tail}, not a barrier. The function
$f(x) = \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ crosses $y=0$ at every nonzero multiple of $\pi$ yet still
has $y=0$ as a horizontal asymptote, because its swings above and below the
line get ever smaller (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-sinc}).
\item A function can have \textbf{two different} horizontal asymptotes, one to
the right and one to the left. We will meet $\arctan x$ and the logistic curve,
which level off at different heights in the two directions.
\item Having a horizontal asymptote is a statement about ends only. It says
nothing about what happens in the middle, where the function may do anything.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Two asymptotes, one curve: the inverse tangent and the logistic}

Some curves used throughout science have a floor on one side and a
ceiling on the other. The inverse tangent function $\arctan x$ of Chapter~4,
which takes a
slope and returns the unique angle between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$ having that
slope, levels off at $\pi/2$ as $x\to+\infty$ and at $-\pi/2$ as $x\to-\infty$
(Figure~\ref{ch04:fig-arctan} in Chapter~4).
The \textbf{logistic function} $\sigma(x) = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$ (the Greek
letter $\sigma$ is read ``sigma'') is a smooth S-shaped curve. It
rises from the floor $y=0$ on
the far left to the ceiling $y=1$ on the far right
(Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-logistic}). In both cases the graph is
bounded between two horizontal lines.

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Why classifiers use the ceiling and floor.} A \emph{classifier} is a
program that answers a yes/no question about its input, such as ``is this email
spam?'' Many classifiers, including neural networks (programs built from many
layers of simple adjustable arithmetic steps), first compute a
raw score $z$ that can be any real number, but a probability must lie in
$[0,1]$. Passing $z$ through $\sigma$ squashes it: huge positive $z$ gives a
value near $1$ (``confident yes''), huge negative $z$ a value near $0$ (``confident
no''). The two horizontal asymptotes are what make $\sigma(z)$ usable as a
probability. The same shape models a population approaching its \emph{carrying
capacity} (the largest population its environment can support), a chemical
reaction approaching completion, or a rumor spreading through a
town: anything that grows fast at first and then runs out of room.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: Settling down versus endless oscillation.} Does
$f(x) = \cos x$ have a horizontal asymptote as $x\to\infty$? What about
$g(x) = \dfrac{\cos x}{x^2}$?

\emph{Answer:} As $x\to\infty$, $\cos x$ waves endlessly between $-1$ and $+1$.
It never settles into any narrow horizontal corridor of half-width $\eps=0.1$,
so it has \textbf{no} horizontal asymptote. But $g(x)=\dfrac{\cos x}{x^2}$ is
trapped between $-1/x^2$ and $+1/x^2$. Since both bounding
curves squeeze toward $0$, the Squeeze Theorem (Chapter~7) forces $g(x)\to 0$,
so $g$ has the horizontal asymptote
$y=0$ (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-cos-squeeze}). Oscillation alone does not prevent
an asymptote, provided the size of the swings dies out.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Vertical Asymptotes}
\label{ch09:vertical}

A horizontal asymptote happens because $x$ ran off to infinity. A
\textbf{vertical} asymptote is the opposite: $x$ stays at a finite spot, but the
\emph{output} runs to infinity.

\begin{definition}[Vertical asymptote]
\label{ch09:def-va}
The line $x=a$ is a \textbf{vertical asymptote} of $f$ if at least one of the
one-sided limits $\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x)$ or $\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x)$ is $+\infty$
or $-\infty$.
\end{definition}

The usual culprit is a denominator that hits zero while the numerator does not.
Near such a point the fraction has a fixed, nonzero amount on top divided by a
vanishing amount on the bottom, and ``fixed over tiny'' is ``huge.'' The sign on
each side tells you whether the cliff goes up or down.

\begin{labbox}
Find the vertical asymptote of $f(x)=\dfrac{x+2}{x-3}$ and its two-sided
behavior. The denominator vanishes at $x=3$, where the numerator is $5\ne 0$, so
$x=3$ is a vertical asymptote. Now test the signs. Just to the \emph{right} of
$3$, say $x=3.01$: top $\approx 5>0$, bottom $=0.01>0$, ratio $\approx +500$, so
$f\to+\infty$. Just to the \emph{left}, $x=2.99$: top $\approx 5$, bottom
$=-0.01<0$, ratio $\approx -500$, so $f\to-\infty$. The curve dives down on the
left of the cliff and shoots up on the right (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-rational}).
\end{labbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A zero in the denominator does not guarantee a vertical asymptote.}
If the numerator vanishes there too, the factor may cancel and leave only a
\emph{hole}: a single missing point in an otherwise unbroken graph.
Consider $g(x)=\dfrac{x^2-9}{x-3}=\dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3}$. For
$x\ne 3$ this equals $x+3$, a straight line with a single missing point at
$(3,6)$ (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-hole}). There is no cliff. Always factor and
cancel \emph{before} declaring an asymptote: asymptote when the zero survives
in the bottom; hole when it cancels.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Dominant-Term Reasoning for Rational Functions}
\label{ch09:dominant}

How did we know the horizontal asymptote in Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-rational} was
$y=1$? There is a fast method that works for every \textbf{rational
function} (a fraction formed by dividing one polynomial by another,
$p(x)/q(x)$), and once you understand it you do not need to memorize a table
of cases.

\begin{intuitionbox}
When $x$ is large, a polynomial is \textbf{dominated by its highest-degree term}.
In $3x^2 + 50x + 9000$, plug in $x=10^6$: the $3x^2$ term is $3\times 10^{12}$,
while $50x = 5\times 10^7$ and the constant $9000$ are negligible in
comparison. The highest-degree term, called the \textbf{leading term},
determines the long-run growth (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-leading-term}). So for end
behavior, \emph{keep only the leading term of the top and the leading term of
the bottom, and simplify.}
\end{intuitionbox}

(dashed). Close up, the constant $9000$ makes them look very different. Far

become a tiny fraction of the total.}
\label{ch09:fig-leading-term}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why dominant-term reasoning is exact and rigorous.} Take
$f(x)=\dfrac{3x^2+50x+9000}{x^2-7}$. Divide top and bottom by $x^2$, the highest
power present:
\[
  f(x) = \frac{3 + \dfrac{50}{x} + \dfrac{9000}{x^2}}
              {1 - \dfrac{7}{x^2}} .
\]
For $x\ne 0$, this is an exact algebraic identity, not an approximation. Now every
term with an $x$ in the denominator $\to 0$ as $x\to\infty$, by the
$1/x\to 0$ fact we proved by hand. So the whole thing tends to
$\dfrac{3+0+0}{1-0} = 3$. Keeping only the leading terms is shorthand for
this division, and the division is what justifies it.
\end{whybox}

The general rule falls out of comparing the \textbf{degree} of the numerator $p$
with the degree of the denominator $q$ (the highest power of $x$ appearing in
each polynomial). Let $\deg p = n$ (read ``the degree of $p$ is $n$'') with
\textbf{leading coefficient} $a$, meaning the leading term of $p$ is $ax^n$;
similarly let $\deg q = m$ with leading coefficient $b$, so the leading term of
$q$ is $bx^m$.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{End behavior of $\dfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ as $x\to\pm\infty$.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Bottom wins} ($n<m$): the fraction $\to 0$. Horizontal asymptote
$y=0$. (The numerator grows more slowly than the denominator.)
\item \textbf{Tie} ($n=m$): the fraction $\to a/b$, the ratio of leading
coefficients. Horizontal asymptote $y=a/b$.
\item \textbf{Top wins} ($n>m$): the fraction $\to \pm\infty$. No horizontal
asymptote. If $n=m+1$ exactly, there is instead a \emph{slant} asymptote
(next section).
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}[All three cases at once]
\label{ch09:ex-three-cases}
\begin{align*}
\frac{2x+1}{x^2+5} &\ \to\ 0
&&(n=1<m=2),\\
\frac{4x^2-x}{2x^2+3} &\ \to\ \frac{4}{2}=2
&&(n=m=2),\\
\frac{x^3}{x^2+1} &\ \to\ \pm\infty
&&(n=3>m=2).
\end{align*}
For the middle one, the horizontal asymptote is $y=2$ in \emph{both} directions:
when the degrees are equal, the ratio of leading terms $\frac{4x^2}{2x^2}=2$ no
longer contains $x$, so the sign of $x$ cannot matter.
Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-three-cases} shows the three graphs.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
When degrees \emph{tie}, students sometimes answer ``$1$'' by reflex, as if the
leading $x$'s cancel completely. They do not: you keep the \emph{coefficients}. In
$\frac{4x^2-x}{2x^2+3}$ the limit is $4/2=2$, not $1$. The lower-degree
terms ($-x$ and $+3$) do not affect the limit, but they still bend the curve
before it settles.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{walkbox}
Without computing, decide the end behavior of each by looking only at leading
terms: $\dfrac{x^4-100x}{3x^4+1}$, $\dfrac{7x}{x^3-x}$,
$\dfrac{x^2-1}{x+1}$. (Answers: $\to \tfrac13$; $\to 0$; the last one cancels to
$x-1$ for $x \neq -1$, so it heads to $\pm\infty$ along the line $y=x-1$.)
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{Roots and other non-polynomial tops}

Dominant-term reasoning works beyond polynomials. Whenever
you can identify the fastest-growing piece on top and on bottom, the same divide
trick works, but you have to be careful about signs under square roots.

\begin{example}[A square root that needs care]
\label{ch09:ex-sqrt}
Find $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\dfrac{\sqrt{4x^2+1}}{x+3}$.
The dominant piece on top is $\sqrt{4x^2}=2\abs{x}$, and on the bottom it is $x$.
Divide top and bottom by $x$. The subtlety is the sign: because
$\sqrt{x^2}=\abs{x}$,
\[
  \frac{\sqrt{4x^2+1}}{x} = \frac{\abs{x}\sqrt{4+1/x^2}}{x}
  = \begin{cases}
      +\sqrt{4+1/x^2} & \text{if } x>0,\\[2pt]
      -\sqrt{4+1/x^2} & \text{if } x<0.
    \end{cases}
\]
Hence
\begin{align*}
  x\to+\infty&:\quad \frac{\sqrt{4+1/x^2}}{1+3/x}\ \to\ \frac{\sqrt 4}{1}=2,\\
  x\to-\infty&:\quad \frac{-\sqrt{4+1/x^2}}{1+3/x}\ \to\ -2 .
\end{align*}
Two different horizontal asymptotes, $y=2$ and $y=-2$, both coming from the
$\sqrt{x^2}=\abs{x}$ sign rule (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-sqrt-two-asymptotes}).
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
$\sqrt{x^2}=\abs{x}$, \textbf{not} $x$. For $x\to-\infty$ this single fact flips
a sign and gives you the second asymptote. Forgetting it is the most common
error in limits-at-infinity with radicals. When dividing by $x$ inside a square
root, you are dividing by $\sqrt{x^2}=\abs{x}$, so a minus sign appears on
the negative side.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: Spotting signs under a radical.} What is
$\lim_{x\to-\infty} \dfrac{5x}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}$? Try it mentally before dividing.

\emph{Answer:} As $x\to-\infty$, the numerator $5x$ is negative, while the square
root in the denominator is always non-negative. The ratio of dominant terms is
\[
  \frac{5x}{\sqrt{x^2}} = \frac{5x}{\abs{x}} = \frac{5x}{-x} = -5
  \qquad(\text{since } \abs{x}=-x \text{ for } x<0).
\]
Checking the overall sign up front keeps that minus sign from slipping past you.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{example}[A sneaky difference of roots]
\label{ch09:ex-diff-roots}
Find $\lim_{x\to\infty}\bigl(\sqrt{x^2+x}-x\bigr)$. As written this is
``$\infty-\infty$,'' which decides nothing: it is an \emph{indeterminate form}
(Chapter~7), and the answer depends on how fast each piece grows. Multiply by
the \textbf{conjugate} (the same two terms with the minus sign flipped to a
plus sign, $\sqrt{x^2+x}+x$) divided by itself, and then divide top and bottom
by $x$ (using $\sqrt{x^2+x}/x=\sqrt{1+1/x}$ for $x>0$):
\begin{align*}
\sqrt{x^2+x}-x
&= \frac{\bigl(\sqrt{x^2+x}-x\bigr)\bigl(\sqrt{x^2+x}+x\bigr)}{\sqrt{x^2+x}+x}
&&\text{(multiply by conjugate over itself)}\\
&= \frac{x^2+x-x^2}{\sqrt{x^2+x}+x}
= \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+x}+x}
&&\text{(since $(A-B)(A+B)=A^2-B^2$)}\\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/x}+1}
&&\text{(divide top and bottom by $x$)}\\
&\longrightarrow \frac{1}{\sqrt 1 + 1}=\frac12 .
\end{align*}
The two huge quantities differ by a gap that tends to $\tfrac12$
(Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-diff-roots}). The conjugate turns the undecided
$\infty-\infty$ into a ratio, where dominant terms can be compared.
\end{example}

\section{Slant (Oblique) Asymptotes}
\label{ch09:slant}

When the top of a rational function out-grows the bottom by exactly one degree,
the curve does not flatten and does not merely blow up vertically. Instead it
approaches a \emph{slanted line}.
A \textbf{slant asymptote} (sometimes called an \textbf{oblique
asymptote}) is a non-horizontal straight line $y=mx+b$ ($m\ne 0$) that the
curve draws arbitrarily close to as $x$ heads out to positive or negative
infinity.

\begin{intuitionbox}
A slant asymptote comes from \emph{polynomial long division}. This works like
long division of whole numbers ($17 \div 5 = 3$ remainder $2$, so
$\tfrac{17}{5} = 3 + \tfrac25$): you find a polynomial quotient and a leftover
remainder whose degree is smaller than the degree of the divisor
(Example~\ref{ch09:ex-slant} shows the steps). Dividing
$p(x)$ by $q(x)$ when $\deg p = \deg q + 1$ gives a linear quotient $mx+b$ plus a
remainder fraction $r(x)/q(x)$ with $\deg r < \deg q$:
\[
   \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \underbrace{mx+b}_{\text{the line}}
   + \underbrace{\frac{r(x)}{q(x)}}_{\to\, 0}.
\]
The remainder fraction has a smaller-degree top than bottom, so by the ``bottom
wins'' rule it tends to $0$ at infinity. What is left in the long run is the
line $y=mx+b$. \textbf{The quotient of long division is the asymptote.}
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{example}[Finding a slant asymptote by division]
\label{ch09:ex-slant}
Consider $f(x)=\dfrac{x^2+2x-1}{x-1}$. Degree $2$ over degree $1$: expect a
slant. Long-divide, one leading term at a time, as with whole numbers. First,
$x^2 \div x = x$; subtract $x(x-1)=x^2-x$ from the top to leave $3x-1$. Next,
$3x\div x = 3$; subtract $3(x-1)=3x-3$ to leave $2$, which has lower degree
than $x-1$, so we stop. Written as a chain, the division says
\begin{align*}
x^2+2x-1 &= x(x-1) + (3x-1) && \text{(first step: } x^2\div x = x\text{)}\\
         &= x(x-1) + 3(x-1) + 2 && \text{(second step: } 3x\div x = 3\text{)}\\
         &= (x-1)(x+3) + 2 .
\end{align*}
The quotient is $x+3$ and the remainder is $2$. Dividing by $x-1$,
\[
   f(x) = x+3 + \frac{2}{x-1}.
\]
As $x\to\pm\infty$ the remainder $\dfrac{2}{x-1}\to 0$, so $f(x)$ approaches the
line $y=x+3$. The sign of $\dfrac{2}{x-1}$ also tells you \emph{which side} of
the line the curve rides: positive (above the line) for $x>1$, negative (below)
for $x<1$. There is also a vertical asymptote at $x=1$, where the remainder
itself blows up.
\end{example}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why ``the quotient is the asymptote'' is an exact theorem.}
Two functions $f$ and $g$ are called \textbf{asymptotic} if $f(x)-g(x)\to 0$ as
$x\to\infty$: the vertical gap between their graphs shrinks to nothing. Long division writes
$f = (mx+b) + \text{remainder}$ with remainder $\to 0$, which is the
statement $f(x)-(mx+b)\to 0$. So the line $mx+b$ is asymptotic to $f$ by
definition. The same logic shows the asymptote need not be a line at all: if the
top exceeds the bottom by two degrees, the quotient is a \emph{parabola}, and the
curve hugs that parabola at infinity (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-parabolic}). People
rarely name these ``parabolic
asymptotes,'' but the reasoning is identical: whatever the polynomial quotient
is, the curve approaches it.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
A graph has a slant asymptote \textbf{or} a horizontal asymptote, never both in
the same direction, since a line is either tilted or level. If $\deg p = \deg q$ you
get a horizontal asymptote; if $\deg p = \deg q + 1$ you get a slant one; if the
gap is $2$ or more there is no straight-line asymptote at all. Always compare
degrees \emph{first} to know which question you are even asking.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Quick degree check: Slant, level, or curved?} Before carrying out any
division, decide the nature of the asymptote at infinity for each:
(1) $f(x) = \dfrac{x^3+1}{x^2-4}$, \quad
(2) $g(x) = \dfrac{x^4-2}{x^2+1}$, \quad
(3) $h(x) = \dfrac{3x^2+5}{x^2-1}$.

\emph{Answer:} Compare the degrees $\deg p$ and $\deg q$.
In (1), $\deg p - \deg q = 3-2 = 1$: the degree gap is exactly $1$, so $f$ has a
\textbf{slant asymptote} (dividing yields $y=x$).
In (2), the gap is $4-2 = 2$: the quotient is quadratic, so $g$ has a
\textbf{parabolic asymptote} ($y=x^2-1$), not a line.
In (3), the degrees tie ($2=2$), so $h$ flattens onto a \textbf{horizontal
asymptote} ($y=3$). Checking degrees first prevents wasted algebra.
\end{walkbox}

\section{Growth Rates Revisited: Who Wins the Long Race?}
\label{ch09:growth}

Dominant-term reasoning settled contests \emph{within} the polynomials. The
comparisons used most often are \emph{across} families: logarithms
versus powers versus exponentials. These three grow at very different speeds,
and their order never changes once $x$ is large enough.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The growth hierarchy (for large $x$).}
\[
   \ln x \ \ll\ x^{p}\ (p>0) \ \ll\ a^{x}\ (a>1) \ \ll\ x! \ \ll\ x^{x}.
\]
Here $f \ll g$ (read ``$f$ is much smaller than $g$'') means
$\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\to 0$ as $x\to\infty$: $f$ becomes a vanishing fraction of
$g$. (The factorial $x!=1\cdot2\cdots x$ of Chapter~3 is defined here for whole
numbers $x$.) Logarithmic growth is slower than any positive power, power
growth is slower than exponential growth, and exponentials are surpassed by factorials.
Every exponential, no matter how small its base above $1$ (say $1.000001^x$),
eventually overtakes every power, no matter how large (say $x^{1000}$).
\end{focusbox}

This claim is surprising, so we prove it. The intuition comes first.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Why exponentials beat powers: fixed percentage versus shrinking
percentage.} A power like $x^2$ grows by an ever-smaller \emph{percentage} as $x$
climbs: going from $100$ to $101$ raises $x^2$ by only about $2\%$. An
exponential like $2^x$ grows by a \emph{fixed percentage} ($100\%$ per unit
step) forever (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-percent}). Fixed-percentage growth
compounds; growth whose percentage keeps shrinking cannot keep up.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[Exponential dominates every power]
\label{ch09:thm-exp-beats-power}
For every fixed $p>0$ and every $a>1$,
\[
   \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{x^{p}}{a^{x}} = 0 .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We go through logarithms, which turn the lopsided ratio into a
difference. Take logs of the ratio:
\[
  \ln\!\left(\frac{x^{p}}{a^{x}}\right)
  = p\ln x - x\ln a .
\]
We will show this $\to -\infty$; then the ratio itself, being $e$ to that power,
$\to 0$. Factor out $x$:
\[
  p\ln x - x\ln a = x\left( \frac{p\ln x}{x} - \ln a\right).
\]
Here $\ln a > 0$ is a fixed positive constant. So everything depends on the term
$\dfrac{\ln x}{x}$. If we can show $\dfrac{\ln x}{x}\to 0$, then the bracket
tends to $0-\ln a = -\ln a < 0$, while the factor $x\to+\infty$. A factor
growing without bound times a bracket that settles near a negative number
gives $-\infty$. So it remains only to
prove the central fact: $\ln x / x \to 0$.

\textbf{Base bound: $\ln x < 2\sqrt x$.} We want an explicit upper bound on $\ln x$ for $x \ge 1$.
From Chapter~3 (Figure~\ref{ch03:fig-slope-one}), the upward-bending curve $e^u$ sits on or above its slope-$1$ tangent line $1 + u$ for every $u \ge 0$ (a bound we will prove analytically in Chapter~16). Setting $u = \tfrac12\ln x \ge 0$ gives $e^u = e^{(\ln x)/2} = \sqrt{x}$, so
\[
  \sqrt{x} \;=\; e^{u} \;\ge\; 1 + u \;>\; u \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\ln x,
  \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
  \ln x < 2\sqrt{x}.
\]
The same bound has an area picture. In the area preview of Chapter~3,
$\ln x = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,\dd t$, and $\frac{1}{t} \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}$
on $[1,x]$, so the area under $\frac1t$ is at most the area under
$\frac{1}{\sqrt t}$, which Chapter~21 will compute exactly:
\[
  \ln x = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,\dd t \;\le\; \int_1^x t^{-1/2}\,\dd t
  = 2\sqrt{x} - 2 < 2\sqrt{x}.
\]
Dividing by $x > 0$ gives
\[
  0 \le \frac{\ln x}{x} < \frac{2\sqrt x}{x} = \frac{2}{\sqrt x}
  \quad\text{for } x\ge 1,
\]
and $2/\sqrt x\to 0$, so $\dfrac{\ln x}{x}\to 0$ by the Squeeze Theorem.
That bound, $\ln x < 2\sqrt x$ (Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-log-sqrt}), shows that
the logarithm grows slower than even a
square root, let alone $x$ itself. Feeding this back,
$\dfrac{p\ln x}{x}\to 0$, the bracket $\to -\ln a<0$, the product $\to -\infty$,
and the original ratio $\to 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{happeningbox}
\textbf{What the logarithm trick does.} Exponentials and powers live
on different scales, and dividing them directly is like comparing a sprinter's
position to a walker's: both numbers race off to infinity and the ratio is an
$\infty/\infty$ blur. The logarithm \emph{changes the ruler}: it turns
products into sums, so $a^x$ becomes the straight line
$x\ln a$, and $x^p$ becomes the slow curve $p\ln x$. On this new ruler the contest
is between a \emph{line} and a \emph{logarithm}, and the line grows much faster.
Taking logs is the standard move whenever you must compare two runaway
quantities.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{Big-O and the speed of algorithms.} An \emph{algorithm} is a
step-by-step procedure a computer follows, and its running time is measured by
how the number of steps grows with $n$, the size of the input. An algorithm whose
step count grows like $\ln n$ is extremely fast; \emph{binary search}, which finds a
name in a sorted list of $n$ names by repeatedly checking the middle and
discarding half, is one. About $n$ or $n\ln n$ steps (a good sorting method) is
fine; $n^2$ (a naive sort) is sluggish on
big inputs; and $2^n$ (trying every subset of the items) is hopeless past a few dozen
items. Programmers write ``$f = O(g)$'' (read ``$f$ is big-O of $g$'') to mean
$f$ grows \emph{no faster than} a constant
times $g$, that is, the ratio $f/g$ stays bounded; ``$f = o(g)$'' (``little-o'')
means exactly our $f\ll g$.
Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-bigo} compares these step counts.
The same limits that shape a curve's tail decide whether a program finishes
in seconds or in centuries.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{labbox}
\textbf{Numerical comparison of growth rates.} Compare $x^{10}$ against $2^x$
at a few points. At $x=10$: $x^{10}=10^{10}=10{,}000{,}000{,}000$ while

magnitude,'' where each order of magnitude is a factor of $10$). At $x=60$:
$2^{60}\approx 1.15\times 10^{18}$ while $60^{10}\approx 6.0\times 10^{17}$: the
exponential has just pulled ahead (the crossover is near $x\approx 59$). Beyond
that point, $2^x$ grows much faster than $x^{10}$, as
Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-crossover} shows.
The crossover is late, but Theorem~\ref{ch09:thm-exp-beats-power} guarantees
it comes. Small inputs can suggest the opposite of the long-run behavior.
\end{labbox}

\begin{example}[Putting the hierarchy to work]
\label{ch09:ex-hierarchy}
Evaluate each by spotting the dominant family.
\begin{align*}
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^{100}}{e^x} &= 0
&&\text{(exponential beats power)},\\
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x^{0.001}} &= 0
&&\text{(power beats log)},\\
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3^x + x^5}{3^x - 100}
&= \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1 + x^5/3^x}{1 - 100/3^x}
= \frac{1+0}{1-0}=1
&&\text{(divide by $3^x$)}.
\end{align*}
In the last one we divided by the dominant term $3^x$, then used
$x^5/3^x\to 0$ (exp beats power) and $100/3^x\to 0$. This is the same dominant-term
strategy as for rational functions, except that the ``leading term'' is now the
fastest-growing \emph{family}, not the highest power.
Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-hierarchy} shows the three families side by side.
\end{example}

\section{Reading a Graph From Its Asymptotes}
\label{ch09:reading}

Given a rational function (or any function whose tails
you can analyze), a short checklist lets you sketch its rough shape before
plotting careful points. Two words first: an \textbf{$x$-intercept} is a point
where the graph crosses the $x$-axis ($f(x)=0$), and the
\textbf{$y$-intercept} is where it crosses the $y$-axis (the point $(0,f(0))$).

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Asymptote checklist for $f(x)=p(x)/q(x)$.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Factor} top and bottom. Cancel common factors --- each cancellation
is a \emph{hole}, not an asymptote.
\item \textbf{Vertical asymptotes:} the surviving zeros of the denominator. Check
the sign on each side to see if the cliff goes up or down.
\item \textbf{Horizontal/slant:} compare degrees. $n<m\Rightarrow y=0$;
$n=m\Rightarrow y=a/b$; $n=m+1\Rightarrow$ slant (do the division); $n>m+1
\Rightarrow$ no line asymptote.
\item \textbf{Intercepts:} $x$-intercepts are the surviving zeros of the
\emph{numerator}; the $y$-intercept is $f(0)$ if defined.
\item \textbf{Sketch} the asymptotes as dashed lines first, then thread the curve
through the intercepts and into the correct corners.
\end{enumerate}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}[A full reading]
\label{ch09:ex-full}
Sketch $f(x)=\dfrac{x^2-4}{x^2-1}$.
\emph{Factor:} $f(x)=\dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{(x-1)(x+1)}$ --- no common factors, so no
holes. \emph{Vertical asymptotes:} $x=1$ and $x=-1$. \emph{Horizontal:} degrees
tie ($n=m=2$) with leading coefficients $1$ and $1$, so $y=1$. \emph{Intercepts:}
zeros of the numerator give $x=\pm 2$; and $f(0)=\dfrac{-4}{-1}=4$, the
$y$-intercept. With two vertical asymptotes, a horizontal asymptote $y=1$ at both ends, and
the curve pinned through $(\pm2,0)$ and $(0,4)$, the overall shape is fixed
(Figure~\ref{ch09:fig-full}). The
middle branch has a \emph{local minimum} at $(0,4)$ (a point lower than all
nearby points of the graph), staying strictly above the asymptote $y=1$.
\end{example}

\begin{aestheticbox}
We never solved $f(x)=k$ for a general height $k$ and never
built a table of twenty points. We asked three structural questions (where
does the denominator vanish, how do the degrees compare, where are the zeros)
and they gave the shape of the graph. Much of calculus works this way: the right
\emph{questions} replace pages of arithmetic.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{From limits to the derivative.}
With limits at infinity in hand, our foundation for limits is complete. In
Chapter~10, we flip our perspective: instead of stepping infinitely far back
to inspect the horizon of a graph, we zoom infinitely far into a single point.
The difference quotient $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ produces the indeterminate form
$0/0$, and limits resolve it to give the instantaneous rate of change: the
\emph{derivative}.

(Much later, in Chapter~25, the growth rates and infinite tails of this chapter
return when we evaluate areas under unbounded curves in \emph{improper integrals}.)
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch09:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Warm-up limits]
Evaluate each limit, naming the dominant terms you used.
\[
\text{(a)}\ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{5x^3-2x}{2x^3+7},
\quad
\text{(b)}\ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x+9}{x^2-4},
\quad
\text{(c)}\ \lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{6x^4+1}{1-3x^2},
\]
\[
\text{(d)}\ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\sqrt{9x^2+5}}{2x-1},
\qquad
\text{(e)}\ \lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{\sqrt{9x^2+5}}{2x-1}.
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Two asymptotes from one radical]
Find both horizontal asymptotes of $f(x)=\dfrac{\sqrt{9x^2+2}}{4x-1}$, treating
$x\to+\infty$ and $x\to-\infty$ separately. Explain, using $\sqrt{x^2}=\abs{x}$,
why the two answers differ in sign.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conjugate trick]
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\bigl(\sqrt{x^2+5x}-\sqrt{x^2+x}\bigr)$
by multiplying by the conjugate. (Answer: $2$.) Then explain in one sentence why
the naive answer ``$\infty-\infty=0$'' is wrong.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Hole versus asymptote]
For each function, state every vertical asymptote and every hole, with
coordinates.
\[
\text{(a)}\ \frac{x-2}{x^2-4},
\qquad
\text{(b)}\ \frac{x^2+x-6}{x^2-9},
\qquad
\text{(c)}\ \frac{x^2+1}{x^2-2x}.
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Slant asymptote by division]
Find the slant asymptote of $f(x)=\dfrac{2x^2+3x-1}{x+2}$ and state on which side
of the line the graph lies for large positive $x$ and for large negative $x$.
(Hint: the division leaves $f(x)=2x-1+\frac{1}{x+2}$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Full sketch]
Carry out the asymptote checklist for $g(x)=\dfrac{x^2-x-2}{x-3}$: holes,
vertical asymptotes, slant asymptote, and all intercepts. Then sketch it,
drawing the asymptotes as dashed lines first.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Growth-rate sorting]
Order the following by eventual growth, slowest to fastest, and justify the two
closest calls with a limit:
\[
   x^{50},\qquad (\ln x)^{1000},\qquad 1.01^{x},\qquad \sqrt{x},\qquad x\ln x.
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A crossing curve]
Give an explicit function with horizontal asymptote $y=2$ that \emph{crosses}
the line $y=2$ infinitely many times as $x\to\infty$. Prove your function does
both. (Hint: a decaying oscillation added to $2$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Prove a limit from the threshold definition]
Using Definition~\ref{ch09:def-limit-inf} directly, prove that
$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x-1}{x+4}=2$. That is, given $\eps>0$,
produce an explicit $M$ (in terms of $\eps$) so that $x>M$ forces
$\bigl|\frac{2x-1}{x+4}-2\bigr|<\eps$. (Hint: the difference simplifies to
$\frac{-9}{x+4}$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Prove log loses to every power]
Adapt the bound $\ln x< 2\sqrt x$ from the proof of
Theorem~\ref{ch09:thm-exp-beats-power} to show that for every fixed $p>0$,
$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x^{p}}=0$. (Hint: it suffices to
beat $x^p$ with $\sqrt x$ when $p\ge \tfrac12$; for small $p$, bound $\ln x$ by
$\tfrac{1}{q}x^{q}$ for a suitable $q<p$ using $\int t^{q-1}\,\dd t$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Parabolic asymptote]
The function $f(x)=\dfrac{x^3+1}{x}$ has a top exceeding the bottom by two
degrees, so no straight-line asymptote. By dividing, show that $f(x)$ becomes
indistinguishable from a \emph{parabola} for large $\abs{x}$, and state that
parabola. Confirm $f(x)-(\text{parabola})\to 0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual]
True or false, with a one-line reason each. (a) A function can have at most two
horizontal asymptotes. (b) A function can have infinitely many vertical
asymptotes. (c) If $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L$ then $f$ never equals $L$. (d) A
rational function whose denominator has higher degree than its numerator always
has the horizontal asymptote $y=0$.
\end{exercise}
