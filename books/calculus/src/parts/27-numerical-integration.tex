\chapter{Numerical Integration}

In the previous chapter, we applied integration across geometry and physics:
volumes of solids, arc lengths, work, and probability distributions. In each
case, the application led to a definite integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$.

We usually expect to evaluate such integrals using the Fundamental Theorem of
Calculus by finding an antiderivative $F$ with $F' = f$. But that requires being
able to express $F$ in closed form.

For many functions, no such antiderivative exists. For example,
\[
\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x
\]
has no antiderivative in terms of elementary functions -- no finite formula built
from polynomials, roots, exponentials, logarithms, and trigonometric functions
differentiates to $e^{-x^2}$. Yet the integral represents a well-defined area
(approximately $0.7468$), and the bell-shaped curve $e^{-x^2}$ appears throughout probability
and statistics: rescaled, it is the \emph{normal distribution}, the curve that describes how
repeated measurements scatter around their average.

When an antiderivative cannot be found, we return to the definition of the
integral as an accumulated sum. By evaluating the integrand at selected sample
points, we can estimate the area to any desired accuracy, together with an
\emph{error bound}: a number that is guaranteed to be at least as large as the
gap between our estimate and the true value. Computing such estimates is called
\emph{numerical integration}.

\begin{focusbox}
The integral $\int_a^b f\,\dd x$ is a number: the signed area under $f$. The Fundamental Theorem is one road to that number, available only when an antiderivative exists in usable form. Numerical integration is a different road, always open, that returns to the original meaning of the integral as accumulated area and computes that area directly from samples of $f$. Our goal: get an accurate number \emph{and} a reliable bound on how wrong it might be.
\end{focusbox}

\section{When the Antiderivative Will Not Come}
\label{ch27:noanti}

Numerical methods are necessary for two distinct reasons: functions with no
elementary antiderivative, and functions known only through discrete data points.

Here are integrals that arise constantly and have no elementary antiderivative:
\[
\int e^{-x^2}\,\dd x,\qquad
\int \frac{\sin x}{x}\,\dd x,\qquad
\int \frac{1}{\ln x}\,\dd x,\qquad
\int \sqrt{1 + \tfrac14\cos^2 x}\,\dd x,\qquad
\int \frac{e^{x}}{x}\,\dd x.
\]
The first is the bell curve of probability (often called the \emph{Gaussian}). The second, $\sin(x)/x$, arises in optics
and signal processing. The third, $1/\ln x$, relates to the distribution of prime numbers.
The fourth gives the arc length of an ellipse. The fifth defines a function used in
physics called the \emph{exponential integral}. None of them yields to substitution,
parts, or partial fractions, because no elementary antiderivative exists to be found.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why an impossible antiderivative is not your fault.}
Joseph Liouville (1833--1841) proved which functions can have elementary
antiderivatives, establishing that $e^{-x^2}$, $\sin(x)/x$, and many others
provably cannot. The absence of an elementary antiderivative is an intrinsic
property of these functions, not a lack of technique. In such cases, numerical
integration is the standard path to a quantitative answer.
\end{whybox}

The second reason is that you may not have a formula for $f$ at all. A bridge engineer measuring how far a beam bends, a physiologist tracking how much blood the heart pumps each second, a data scientist with a column of numbers: each has $f$ only as a \emph{table of values}, sampled at points. There is no formula to integrate, only data and the area under it. Numerical integration turns such samples into accumulated totals.

\begin{applicationbox}
A car's speedometer reads its speed $v(t)$ each second. How far did the car travel between $t = 0$ and $t = 60$? Distance is $\int_0^{60} v(t)\,\dd t$. You have no formula for $v(t)$ -- you have sixty-one numbers. The only way to recover distance is to estimate the area under the speed curve from those samples. Fitness trackers and flight data recorders integrate their sensor readings in exactly this way.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{The Crudest Defensible Estimate: Rectangles}
\label{ch27:rectangles}

We already met the idea in the chapter on the integral. Chop $[a,b]$ into $n$ equal slices of width
\[
h = \frac{b-a}{n},
\]
with cut points $x_0 = a,\ x_1 = a+h,\ \dots,\ x_n = b$; in general $x_i = a + ih$. On each slice approximate the area by a rectangle. If we use the left endpoint's height $f(x_{i-1})$ on each slice we get the \emph{left Riemann sum}; the right endpoints give the right sum.

The trouble with rectangles is visible to the eye. A rectangle has a flat top, but $f$ usually slopes. On a rising function, left rectangles sit entirely below the curve and lose area; right rectangles poke above and gain it (Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-rect}). The error on each slice is roughly the little triangular sliver between the flat top and the sloping curve. Halve the slice width and you roughly halve each sliver's base \emph{and} its height, so each sliver's area drops to about a quarter. But there are now twice as many slivers, so the total error only halves. The error of rectangles is proportional to $h$: to get one more correct decimal digit you need ten times as many slices.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The plan for the rest of the chapter is to imitate the curve more closely on each slice. (Numerical integration is also called \emph{numerical quadrature}, after the classical word for finding areas.) A rectangle's top is a constant (degree 0). A line segment (degree 1) follows the local slope, giving the trapezoidal rule. A parabola (degree 2) follows the local bending as well, giving Simpson's rule. Each step up in degree removes the largest remaining part of the error, so the error shrinks faster as the slices get thinner.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{The Trapezoidal Rule: Let the Tops Slope}
\label{ch27:trap}

The missing sliver suggests the remedy. Instead of a flat top, connect the two endpoints of each slice with a straight line. The flat-topped rectangle becomes a \emph{trapezoid} whose slanted top hugs the curve far better (Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-trap}).

\caption{The trapezoidal rule: connect consecutive sample points with straight chords. Each trapezoid hugs the curve much more closely than a flat rectangle. The leftover error per slice now comes only from the curve's \emph{bending}, not its sloping.}
\label{ch27:fig-trap}
\end{figure}

The area of a single trapezoid is its average height times its width. On the slice $[x_{i-1}, x_i]$ the two heights are $f(x_{i-1})$ and $f(x_i)$, and the width is $h$, so
\[
\text{slice area} \approx h\cdot \frac{f(x_{i-1}) + f(x_i)}{2}.
\]
Add up all $n$ slices. Call the sample points $x_0, \dots, x_n$ the \emph{nodes}. Every interior node $x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}$ is the right endpoint of one trapezoid and the left endpoint of the next, so its height gets counted twice. The two outer endpoints $x_0$ and $x_n$ are counted once (Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-weights}, top row). Collecting terms gives a short formula.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Trapezoidal Rule.} With $h = (b-a)/n$ and $x_i = a + i h$,
\[
T_n = \frac{h}{2}\Big[\, f(x_0) + 2f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + \cdots + 2f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n) \,\Big].
\]
The pattern of weights is $1,2,2,\dots,2,1$. Ends count once, interiors count twice, and the whole thing is scaled by $h/2$.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Another way to see the trapezoidal rule.} It replaces the curve with the connect-the-dots polygon through your samples and takes the area under the polygon. That polygon is the only \emph{piecewise-linear} function (one made of straight segments joined end to end) that agrees with $f$ at every node. Integrating it exactly gives $T_n$. So the trapezoidal rule is exact for any function that already \emph{is} a straight line on each slice, and approximately right to the extent that $f$ is nearly straight at the scale of $h$.
\end{whybox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose you use the trapezoidal rule to integrate $f(x) = 5x - 3$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 10$, using a single slice ($n = 1$). What is the error?

\emph{Mental check:} The function is a straight line, so its second derivative is zero everywhere ($f'' = 0$). The top edge of the trapezoid is the line itself, so the error is zero, even with one slice.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{labbox}
Estimate $\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x$ with the trapezoidal rule using $n=4$. Then $h = 0.25$ and the nodes are $0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1$. Compute the heights $f(x) = e^{-x^2}$:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
x & e^{-x^2}\\\hline
0    & 1.000000\\
0.25 & 0.939413\\
0.5  & 0.778801\\
0.75 & 0.569783\\
1.0  & 0.367879
\end{array}
\]
Now apply the weights $1,2,2,2,1$:
\[
T_4 = \frac{0.25}{2}\Big[1.000000 + 2(0.939413) + 2(0.778801) + 2(0.569783) + 0.367879\Big].
\]
The bracket is $1.000000 + 1.878826 + 1.557602 + 1.139566 + 0.367879 = 5.943873$. Multiply by $h/2 = 0.125$: $T_4 = 0.742984$. The true value is $0.746824$. We are off by about $0.0038$, under one percent with only five function evaluations, and without any antiderivative.
\end{labbox}

\subsection{How Wrong Is the Trapezoidal Rule? The Error Comes From Curvature}
\label{ch27:traperror}

Look again at Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-trap}. Where the curve bulges \emph{up} (concave down), the chord cuts underneath and the trapezoid undershoots. Where the curve bulges \emph{down} (concave up), the chord rides above and the trapezoid overshoots. Either way, the error is governed by how much the curve bends -- by the second derivative $f''$. A straight line has $f'' = 0$ and the trapezoidal rule gets it exactly right. The more $f$ curves, the more the chord misses.

We can make this precise by analyzing a single slice. Take a single interval of width $h$ and ask how far the area under the chord differs from the area under $f$. A careful accounting (a short Taylor expansion -- approximating $f$ near the midpoint by its local value, slope, and bending -- done in the box below) gives, for one slice,
\[
\text{(true area)} - \text{(trapezoid area)} = -\frac{h^3}{12}\, f''(\xi)
\]
for some point $\xi$ (Greek ``xi'') inside the slice; we do not know exactly where, only that it lies in the slice. The error on one slice is proportional to $h^3$ and to the curvature there.

\begin{happeningbox}
\textbf{Why $h^3$ per slice but $h^2$ overall.} Write $\text{error} \sim h^3$ to mean ``the error is roughly a fixed number times $h^3$''. Each slice carries error of size $\sim h^3$. But to cover a fixed interval $[a,b]$ you need $n = (b-a)/h$ slices, so the errors stack up:
\[
\text{total error} \sim n \cdot h^3 = \frac{b-a}{h}\cdot h^3 = (b-a)\,h^2.
\]
One power of $h$ is eaten by needing more slices as $h$ shrinks. What survives is $h^2$. The same pattern holds for every rule in this chapter: a method with per-slice error $\sim h^{p+1}$ has \emph{global} error (the total error over all of $[a,b]$) $\sim h^{p}$. The trapezoidal rule has $p=2$. Halve $h$ (double the work) and the error drops by a factor of $4$. With rectangles, halving $h$ only halves the error.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Where the $-h^3 f''/12$ comes from.} Put the slice symmetrically as $[-h/2, h/2]$ to keep the algebra clean. Taylor-expand $f$ about the midpoint:
\[
f(x) = f(0) + f'(0)\,x + \tfrac12 f''(0)\,x^2 + \cdots
\]
Integrate across the slice. The odd term $f'(0)x$ integrates to zero by symmetry, leaving
\begin{align*}
\int_{-h/2}^{h/2} f\,\dd x
  &= f(0)\,h + \tfrac12 f''(0)\cdot \frac{2}{3}\Big(\frac{h}{2}\Big)^3 + \cdots\\
  &= f(0)\,h + \frac{f''(0)\,h^3}{24} + \cdots
\end{align*}
The trapezoid uses the two endpoint heights $f(\pm h/2)$. Their average is
\begin{align*}
\frac{f(h/2)+f(-h/2)}{2}
  &= f(0) + \tfrac12 f''(0)\Big(\frac{h}{2}\Big)^2 + \cdots\\
  &= f(0) + \frac{f''(0)\,h^2}{8} + \cdots,
\end{align*}
so the trapezoid area is $h\big(f(0) + \tfrac{f''(0)h^2}{8}\big) = f(0)h + \tfrac{f''(0)h^3}{8}$, ignoring higher powers of $h$. Subtract:
\begin{align*}
\text{true} - \text{trap}
  &= \frac{f''(0)\,h^3}{24} - \frac{f''(0)\,h^3}{8}\\
  &= f''(0)\,h^3\Big(\frac{1}{24} - \frac{3}{24}\Big)
   = -\frac{f''(0)\,h^3}{12}.
\end{align*}
The \emph{midpoint rule} (a rectangle of height $f(0)$, the value at the center, so area $f(0)\,h$) misses the truth by $+\tfrac{1}{24}f''(0)h^3$, while the trapezoid misses by $-\tfrac{1}{12}f''(0)h^3$. Both errors are governed by $f'' h^3$, but the trapezoid's error is exactly
\emph{twice as large, in the opposite direction}. This relationship is the foundation
for Simpson's rule.
\end{whybox}

Now add up the slices. Let $M_2$ be any number with $\abs{f''(x)}\le M_2$ for every $x$ in $[a,b]$ (an upper bound on the curvature). Each slice error $-\frac{h^3}{12}f''(\xi_i)$ is then at most $\frac{h^3}{12}M_2$ in size, and by the triangle inequality the total is at most the sum of the sizes:
\[
n\cdot\frac{h^3}{12}\,M_2 = \frac{(b-a)\,h^2}{12}\,M_2, \qquad\text{since } nh = b-a.
\]
Adding up $n$ slices costs one power of $h$. (When $f''$ is continuous, the average $\frac1n\sum f''(\xi_i)$ lies between the minimum and maximum of $f''$, so the Intermediate Value Theorem even pins the exact global error to $-\frac{(b-a)h^2}{12}f''(\xi)$ for some $\xi\in[a,b]$.)

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Trapezoidal error bound.} If $\abs{f''(x)} \le M_2$ for all $x$ in $[a,b]$, then
\[
\abs{\,\int_a^b f\,\dd x - T_n\,} \le \frac{(b-a)\,h^2}{12}\,M_2 = \frac{(b-a)^3}{12\,n^2}\,M_2.
\]
The error is controlled by the largest curvature on the interval and falls like $1/n^2$.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{labbox}
Bound the error of our $T_4$ estimate of $\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x$. We need a bound $M_2$ on $\abs{f''}$ over $[0,1]$. With $f = e^{-x^2}$, $f' = -2x e^{-x^2}$ and $f'' = (4x^2 - 2)e^{-x^2}$. On $[0,1]$ this ranges between $-2$ (at $x=0$) and about $0.74$ (at $x=1$), so $\abs{f''} \le 2$; take $M_2 = 2$. With $b-a = 1$, $n=4$, $h = 0.25$:
\[
\abs{\text{error}} \le \frac{1 \cdot (0.25)^2}{12}\cdot 2 = \frac{0.0625}{12}\cdot 2 = 0.0104.
\]
Our actual error was $0.0038$, comfortably inside the guaranteed bound of $0.0104$. The bound is valid but conservative: it assumes the worst-case curvature everywhere, while the real curvature is smaller over most of the interval. This is typical: the bound guarantees, and the true error is usually well below it.
\end{labbox}

\section{Simpson's Rule: Let the Tops Be Parabolas}
\label{ch27:simpson}

The trapezoidal rule replaced the curve by straight chords and was left with an error driven by curvature, $f''$. The next step is to replace the curve by \emph{parabolas}, which can themselves curve. A parabola is determined by three points, so we take the samples two slices at a time -- $x_0, x_1, x_2$ -- fit the unique parabola through those three heights, and use the exact area under that parabola as our estimate for the area over $[x_0, x_2]$ (Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-simp}).

Integrating the parabola through three points gives a simple weight pattern. Over a double-slice $[x_0, x_2]$ of total width $2h$, the exact area under the \emph{interpolating parabola} (the unique quadratic passing through the three sample points) works out to
\[
\int_{x_0}^{x_2} (\text{parabola})\,\dd x = \frac{h}{3}\Big[\,f(x_0) + 4 f(x_1) + f(x_2)\,\Big].
\]
The middle point gets weight $4$, the two ends weight $1$, scaled by $h/3$. (The box below derives this by integrating a quadratic.) Now stitch these double-slices together along $[a,b]$. This requires $n$ to be \emph{even} so the slices pair up cleanly. Each interior odd-indexed node $x_1, x_3, x_5, \dots$ is the apex of its own parabola and gets weight $4$. Each interior even-indexed node $x_2, x_4, \dots$ is shared as the right end of one parabola and the left end of the next, so its two weight-$1$ contributions add to $2$. The outer ends keep weight $1$.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Simpson's Rule} (requires $n$ even). With $h = (b-a)/n$,
\[
S_n = \frac{h}{3}\Big[\, f(x_0) + 4f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + 4f(x_3) + 2f(x_4) + \cdots + 4f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)\,\Big].
\]
The weight pattern is $1,4,2,4,2,\dots,4,1$: ends are $1$, odd interior nodes are $4$, even interior nodes are $2$, all scaled by $h/3$.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Deriving the $\tfrac{h}{3}[1,4,1]$ formula.} Center the double-slice at the origin: nodes at $-h, 0, +h$ with heights $y_{-1}, y_0, y_1$. Any parabola is $p(x) = A + Bx + Cx^2$. Integrate it across $[-h,h]$:
\[
\int_{-h}^{h} (A + Bx + Cx^2)\,\dd x = 2Ah + \frac{2Ch^3}{3},
\]
since the odd term $Bx$ integrates to zero. Now express $A$ and $C$ through the sampled heights. Plugging in the nodes: $y_0 = A$, and $y_1 + y_{-1} = 2A + 2Ch^2$, so $C = (y_1 - 2y_0 + y_{-1})/(2h^2)$. Substitute:
\[
2Ah + \frac{2Ch^3}{3} = 2y_0 h + \frac{h}{3}(y_1 - 2y_0 + y_{-1}) = \frac{h}{3}\big(y_{-1} + 4y_0 + y_1\big).
\]
This yields the $1,4,1$ coefficient pattern. The linear term $Bx$
dropped out by odd symmetry across $[-h,h]$, and, as the next section shows, the
same symmetry removes any cubic term $Dx^3$ too.
\end{whybox}

\begin{labbox}
Estimate $\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x$ with Simpson's rule, $n = 4$, reusing the exact same five heights from the trapezoidal lab. Apply the weights $1,4,2,4,1$:
\[
S_4 = \frac{0.25}{3}\Big[1.000000 + 4(0.939413) + 2(0.778801) + 4(0.569783) + 0.367879\Big].
\]
The bracket: $1.000000 + 3.757652 + 1.557602 + 2.279132 + 0.367879 = 8.962265$. Multiply by $h/3 = 0.083333$: $S_4 = 0.746855$. The true value is $0.746824$. The error is $0.000031$, about $31$ \emph{millionths}. The trapezoidal rule with the very same data missed by $0.0038$. Simpson, using not a single extra function evaluation, is more than a hundred times more accurate. Why does a change of weights buy so much?
\end{labbox}

\section{Cubic Exactness and Fourth-Order Convergence}
\label{ch27:whysimpson}

Simpson's rule is derived by fitting parabolas, which guarantees exact integration
for quadratic polynomials. It is also exact for all \emph{cubic}
polynomials. That extra degree explains why its error falls like $h^4$. (A rule whose
global error is $\sim h^p$ is said to have \emph{order} $p$, and the way its estimates
approach the true value as $h$ shrinks is called its \emph{convergence}; so Simpson's rule
has fourth-order convergence, and the trapezoidal rule second-order.)

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Cubic exactness via symmetry.} Consider $f(x) = x^3$ on the symmetric double-slice $[-h, h]$ with midpoint at $0$. Its true integral is zero, because $x^3$ is an odd function: the positive area on $[0,h]$ exactly cancels the negative area on $[-h,0]$. Now evaluate Simpson's rule on this interval: the weights $1,4,1$ act on the sample values $(-h)^3 = -h^3$, $0^3 = 0$, and $h^3$, giving $1\cdot(-h^3) + 4\cdot 0 + 1\cdot h^3 = 0$. Simpson's rule yields zero with zero error. The symmetric evaluation cancels the cubic contribution just as the true integral does. Consequently, a method constructed to interpolate quadratics integrates all cubics exactly as well.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose you apply Simpson's rule to $f(x) = 2x^3 - 5x^2 + 4x - 1$ on $[-2, 2]$ using $n = 2$. How far will your numerical estimate be from the exact integral?

\emph{Mental check:} The error is exactly zero. Simpson's rule fits a degree-2 parabola, but because the weighting is symmetric, the cubic and linear terms cancel out completely. Any polynomial up to degree 3 is integrated with zero error by Simpson's rule.
\end{recoverybox}

To see where the cancellation comes from, look again at the \emph{midpoint rule}. Instead of sampling at the ends of a slice (trapezoid) it samples at the \emph{center} and uses a rectangle of that single height. Because the rectangle's flat top crosses the curve in the middle of the slice, it overshoots on one side and undershoots on the other, and those two errors largely balance each other (Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-mid}). That cancellation is why the midpoint rule, despite using only \emph{one} sample per slice, has error $\sim h^2$, and its leading error term has the \emph{opposite sign} of the trapezoidal error, which Simpson's rule exploits directly.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Simpson is the trapezoid's error, cancelled.} Recall the two facts we proved earlier on a single slice: the \emph{midpoint rule} has error $+\tfrac{1}{24} f'' h^3$ per slice, while the \emph{trapezoid} has error $-\tfrac{1}{12}f'' h^3$. The trapezoid's leading error is exactly $-2$ times the midpoint's. So take the weighted blend
\[
\frac{2\,(\text{Midpoint}) + 1\,(\text{Trapezoid})}{3}.
\]
The leading curvature errors cancel: $2\cdot(+\tfrac{1}{24}) + 1\cdot(-\tfrac{1}{12}) = \tfrac{1}{12} - \tfrac{1}{12} = 0$. This blend \emph{is} Simpson's rule. On a double-slice $[-h,h]$ of width $2h$, the one-rectangle midpoint area is $2h\,f(0)$ and the one-trapezoid area is $h\big(f(-h)+f(h)\big)$. Blending them $2{:}1$ gives
\[
\frac13\Big[4h\,f(0) + h\big(f(-h)+f(h)\big)\Big] = \frac{h}{3}\Big[f(-h)+4f(0)+f(h)\Big],
\]
exactly the $1,4,1$ pattern. So Simpson's rule is the combination of the two simple rules chosen to cancel their common $f''$ error term.

With the curvature error gone, what is the leading remainder? Expand $f$ to fourth order about $0$. Odd powers cancel, both in the integral and in the sum
\[
f(-h)+f(h) = 2f(0) + f''(0)\,h^2 + \tfrac{1}{12}f^{(4)}(0)\,h^4 + \cdots.
\]
The true integral and Simpson's estimate are
\begin{align*}
\int_{-h}^{h} f\,\dd x &= 2f(0)\,h + \tfrac{1}{3}f''(0)\,h^3 + \tfrac{1}{60}f^{(4)}(0)\,h^5 + \cdots,\\
\frac{h}{3}\big[f(-h)+4f(0)+f(h)\big] &= 2f(0)\,h + \tfrac{1}{3}f''(0)\,h^3 + \tfrac{1}{36}f^{(4)}(0)\,h^5 + \cdots.
\end{align*}
The difference is $(\tfrac{1}{60} - \tfrac{1}{36})f^{(4)}(0)h^5 = -\tfrac{1}{90}f^{(4)}(0)h^5$ per double-slice of width $2h$. Summing over all $n/2$ double-slices (with $(n/2)\cdot 2h = b-a$) gives a global error of $-\tfrac{(b-a)h^4}{180}f^{(4)}(\xi)$ for some $\xi$ in $[a,b]$: every lower power of $h$ has dropped out of the error.
\end{whybox}

Because the $f''$ error is gone, Simpson's per-slice error is $\sim h^5 f^{(4)}$, and summed over the $n/2$ double-slices the global error is $\sim h^4 f^{(4)}$. Compare:

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Simpson error bound.} If $\abs{f^{(4)}(x)} \le M_4$ on $[a,b]$, then
\[
\abs{\,\int_a^b f\,\dd x - S_n\,} \le \frac{(b-a)\,h^4}{180}\,M_4 = \frac{(b-a)^5}{180\,n^4}\,M_4.
\]
The error falls like $1/n^4$. Doubling the number of slices divides the error by $16$.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{labbox}
Bound the error of $S_4$ for $\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x$. We need $M_4 = \max\abs{f^{(4)}}$ on $[0,1]$. For $f = e^{-x^2}$, $f^{(4)} = (16x^4 - 48x^2 + 12)e^{-x^2}$. At $x = 0$ this is $12$; on the rest of $[0,1]$ it stays between about $-7.4$ and $12$, so $\abs{f^{(4)}} \le 12$; take $M_4 = 12$. With $b-a=1$, $h=0.25$:
\[
\abs{\text{error}} \le \frac{1\cdot (0.25)^4}{180}\cdot 12 = \frac{0.00390625}{180}\cdot 12 = 0.00026.
\]
Our actual error was $0.000031$, well inside the guaranteed $0.00026$ and about eight times smaller.
\end{labbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Moving from straight chords to parabolas, with symmetry cancelling the cubic term, improves the global error from $h^2$ to $h^4$. In practice the difference is large: for $\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x$ to an accuracy of $10^{-8}$, the trapezoidal rule needs about $2{,}500$ slices, whereas Simpson's rule needs about thirty (Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-conv}). The function values are the same; only the weights change, from $1,2,2,\dots,2,1$ to $1,4,2,4,\dots,4,1$. Choosing a higher-order rule usually gains far more than adding grid points to a low-order one.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{A Fuller Worked Example with Both Rules}
\label{ch27:worked}

Let us integrate something with a known answer so we can check ourselves against the truth. Take
\[
\int_1^2 \frac{1}{x}\,\dd x = \ln 2 = 0.693147\ldots
\]
This one \emph{does} have an antiderivative, $\ln x$, but that makes it a perfect test bench: we can compare our estimates against the exact value to the last digit.

Use $n = 4$, so $h = 0.25$ and the nodes are $1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2$. The heights $f(x) = 1/x$:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
x & 1/x\\\hline
1.00 & 1.000000\\
1.25 & 0.800000\\
1.50 & 0.666667\\
1.75 & 0.571429\\
2.00 & 0.500000
\end{array}
\]

\textbf{Trapezoidal.} Weights $1,2,2,2,1$:
\[
T_4 = \frac{0.25}{2}\big[1.000000 + 2(0.800000) + 2(0.666667) + 2(0.571429) + 0.500000\big].
\]
Bracket: $1.000000 + 1.600000 + 1.333334 + 1.142858 + 0.500000 = 5.576192$. Times $0.125$: $T_4 = 0.697024$. Error: $0.003877$.

\textbf{Simpson.} Weights $1,4,2,4,1$:
\[
S_4 = \frac{0.25}{3}\big[1.000000 + 4(0.800000) + 2(0.666667) + 4(0.571429) + 0.500000\big].
\]
Bracket: $1.000000 + 3.200000 + 1.333334 + 2.285716 + 0.500000 = 8.319050$. Times $0.083333$: $S_4 = 0.693254$. Error: $0.000107$.

Simpson beats the trapezoidal rule by a factor of about $36$ here, on identical data. Now check the error bounds. For $f = 1/x$ on $[1,2]$: $f'' = 2/x^3$, maximized at $x=1$ giving $M_2 = 2$; and $f^{(4)} = 24/x^5$, maximized at $x=1$ giving $M_4 = 24$.
\[
\text{Trap bound} = \frac{1\cdot(0.25)^2}{12}\cdot 2 = 0.01042,\qquad
\text{Simpson bound} = \frac{1\cdot(0.25)^4}{180}\cdot 24 = 0.00052.
\]
Both actual errors ($0.0039$ and $0.0001$) sit safely below their bounds, just as they did for $e^{-x^2}$.

\begin{labbox}
\textbf{Use the bound to choose $n$ in advance.} Suppose you want $\int_1^2 \tfrac1x\,\dd x$ to within $10^{-6}$ using Simpson. Solve the bound for $n$:
\[
\frac{(b-a)^5}{180\,n^4}M_4 \le 10^{-6}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\frac{1\cdot 24}{180\,n^4} \le 10^{-6}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
n^4 \ge \frac{24}{180\cdot 10^{-6}} = 133{,}333
\;\Longrightarrow\;
n \ge 19.1.
\]
Round up to the next even integer: $n = 20$. With only twenty-one function values, Simpson guarantees six correct digits of $\ln 2$. The same calculation for the trapezoidal rule demands $n \ge 409$, twenty times the work for the same accuracy. Inverting the error bound like this lets you choose the step size before computing anything, instead of by trial and error.
\end{labbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Suppose your simulation needs to improve an integral's accuracy by a factor of $10{,}000$. How many times more slices must the trapezoidal rule use? How many times more for Simpson?

\emph{Mental check:} The trapezoid error falls like $1/n^2$, so cutting error by $10{,}000$ requires $\sqrt{10{,}000} = 100$ times as many slices. Simpson's error falls like $1/n^4$, so it requires only $(10{,}000)^{1/4} = 10$ times as many slices. A ten-fold increase in evaluation points buys a ten-thousand-fold gain in precision.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Three common numerical integration pitfalls:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Odd $n$ in Simpson.} Simpson pairs slices into parabolas, so $n$ must be even. With odd $n$ the weight pattern $1,4,2,\dots$ does not close properly and your answer is silently wrong. Always check $n$ is even before applying $h/3\,[1,4,2,\dots,4,1]$.
\item \textbf{Forgetting the rules assume smoothness.} The error bounds rely on $f''$ (trapezoid) or $f^{(4)}$ (Simpson) being bounded. If $f$ has a kink, a spike, or a vertical tangent in the interval -- think $\sqrt{x}$ near $0$, whose derivatives blow up -- the bound is infinite and the promised convergence rate evaporates. Subdivide at the bad point or change variables first.
\item \textbf{Mixing up $h/2$ and $h/3$.} Trapezoid is $h/2$, Simpson is $h/3$. Swap them and every digit is off. The mnemonic: trapezoid \emph{averages} two heights ($\div 2$); Simpson's weights $1{+}4{+}1 = 6 = 3\times 2$ live naturally over $\div 3$.
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\section{How Computers Actually Integrate}
\label{ch27:computers}

When you call an integration routine in a scientific library (for example \texttt{quad(f, a, b)} in Python's SciPy), what runs is a refined descendant of the rules we have built.

\textbf{Adaptive subdivision.} A fixed, uniform $h$ is wasteful. Where $f$ is nearly straight, big slices suffice; where $f$ swings violently, you want tiny slices. So real integrators are \emph{adaptive}: they apply Simpson's rule on a region, then split the region in half and apply Simpson to each half. If the two-half estimate agrees with the whole-region estimate to within the \emph{tolerance} (the error you have said you are willing to accept), the region is declared done. Otherwise each half goes through the same test, and is split again if needed, and so on. Effort flows automatically to wherever the function is hardest (Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-adaptive}).

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{How the computer estimates its own error without knowing the answer.} Write $I = \int_a^b f\,\dd x$ for the true value. Run Simpson once on $[a,b]$ with step $h$ to get $S(h)$, then again with step $h/2$ to get $S(h/2)$. We know the error scales like $h^4$, so $S(h)$ is off by about $C h^4$, where $C$ is some constant depending on $f$, and $S(h/2)$ is off by about $C(h/2)^4 = Ch^4/16$. Subtract the two estimates:
\[
S(h/2) - S(h) \approx \Big(I - \frac{Ch^4}{16}\Big) - \big(I - Ch^4\big) = Ch^4 - \frac{Ch^4}{16} = \frac{15}{16}\,Ch^4.
\]
The error of the better estimate, $Ch^4/16$, cannot be seen directly, but it is about $\tfrac{1}{15}$ of the \emph{difference between the two estimates}, which we \emph{can} compute. So the computer gauges its own error by comparing two grids, without knowing the true value. When that estimated error drops below your tolerance, it stops. Adaptive routines use exactly this test to decide whether a region needs splitting.
\end{whybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
That same comparison of two grids can be pushed one step further, an idea called \emph{Richardson extrapolation}: form the combination $\big(16\,S(h/2) - S(h)\big)/15$, which cancels the leading $h^4$ error entirely and leaves an error of order $h^6$. Applied over and over to the trapezoidal rule, this idea generates the \emph{Romberg integration} table, a triangular scheme that raises the order by two at each column. Romberg's first column is trapezoidal estimates on ever-finer grids; its second column is exactly Simpson's rule; later columns are higher-order rules nobody had to derive by hand. It is the same Taylor-cancellation logic used to construct Simpson's rule, carried to higher orders. We meet this extrapolation principle again when studying Taylor series.
\end{connectionbox}

\textbf{Gaussian quadrature.} We assumed the sample points were \emph{equally spaced}, because that is natural and because it is forced on you when $f$ is a table of measurements. But if you are free to choose \emph{where} to sample $f$, you can do much better. Gauss asked: with a fixed budget of $n$ sample points, placed wherever we like and weighted however we like, what is the most accurate rule possible? His answer places the points at the roots of certain polynomials called \emph{Legendre polynomials} (you do not need their formulas here; the points and weights are listed in tables and built into software). The points avoid the ends of the interval and crowd closer together toward them than equal spacing would (Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-gauss}). The resulting $n$-point rule is exact for every polynomial of degree up to $2n-1$. Simpson's rule stays exact only up to degree $3$ however many points it uses; a Gauss rule with five points is already exact up to degree $9$. For smooth functions this is the method of choice, and it is what high-precision libraries reach for first.

\begin{historybox}
Around 250~BCE, Archimedes approximated the area of a circle using inscribed and
circumscribed polygons, establishing the principle of bounding areas by simpler shapes.
Surveyors in Egypt and Rome similarly estimated irregular land areas using trapezoidal slices.

The systematic theory began around 1676--1711, when Isaac Newton and Roger Cotes
developed what is now called \textbf{Newton--Cotes quadrature}. Working on planetary
orbits where no algebraic antiderivatives were available, they interpolated functions
with polynomials over equally spaced points.

Thomas Simpson published the three-point parabolic rule bearing his name in his 1743
treatise \emph{Mathematical Dissertations}, though the formula had been derived earlier
by James Gregory in 1668 and used by Newton and Cavalieri.

Newton and Cotes assumed that increasing polynomial degree on equally spaced nodes would
yield arbitrary accuracy. However, for degree 8 and higher, the interpolation weights
oscillate in sign and grow large, making the formulas sensitive to rounding errors.
This is related to \emph{Runge's phenomenon}: a high-degree polynomial forced through many
equally spaced points can swing wildly between them near the ends of the interval. In 1814, Carl Friedrich Gauss showed that by
choosing both the sample locations and their weights optimally, an $n$-point rule could
integrate polynomials up to degree $2n-1$ exactly, with all weights positive. Modern
scientific software commonly uses adaptive extensions such as \emph{Gauss--Kronrod quadrature},
a Gauss rule with extra points added so that it can estimate its own error.
\end{historybox}

\begin{happeningbox}
\textbf{What a computer evaluates} during numerical integration is a weighted sum. It samples $f$ at chosen points $x_i$ (the nodes), multiplies each value by a precomputed number $w_i$ (its weight), and adds the results:
\[
\int_a^b f\,\dd x \approx \sum_i w_i\, f(x_i).
\]
Every definite integral evaluated by computer, whether a probability, the energy of an orbit, or the distance covered by a car, comes down to such a sum. For the trapezoidal rule the weights are $\tfrac{h}{2}$ times $1,2,2,\dots,2,1$; for Simpson's rule they are $\tfrac{h}{3}$ times $1,4,2,4,\dots,1$; Gaussian quadrature computes its nodes and weights from the Legendre polynomials. Each method is a choice of nodes and weights that makes the finite sum as close to the integral as the smoothness of $f$ allows.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{walkbox}
On your walk, picture the speedometer-and-distance problem. You glance at the speedometer once a minute on a winding drive. The trapezoidal rule says: average each consecutive pair of readings and multiply by a minute. Simpson says: take readings three at a time and trust that the speed varied parabolically between them. Now ask yourself: on a stretch where your acceleration was steadily increasing (constant $f''$, so $f''' = 0$), which rule is exactly right, and why does Simpson nail it while the trapezoid does not? Then ask the harder one: if you got to choose \emph{when} to glance, would you space your glances evenly -- or look more often through the twists and less often on the straightaways?
\end{walkbox}

\section{Summary: The Logic of the Whole Subject}
\label{ch27:summary}

We began stranded -- handed integrals like $\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x$ that provably have no elementary antiderivative, so the Fundamental Theorem cannot help. We returned to the integral's true meaning as area and learned to estimate that area from samples.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Rectangles} approximate $f$ by constants on each slice. Global error $\sim h$. Crude.
\item \textbf{Trapezoidal rule}, weights $\tfrac{h}{2}[1,2,\dots,2,1]$, approximates $f$ by straight chords. Exact for lines; error driven by curvature $f''$; global error $\sim h^2$. Built from connect-the-dots.
\item \textbf{Simpson's rule}, weights $\tfrac{h}{3}[1,4,2,4,\dots,4,1]$ with $n$ even, approximates $f$ by parabolas. Exact for cubics (one bonus degree, by symmetry); error driven by $f^{(4)}$; global error $\sim h^4$. Built as the curvature-cancelling blend $\tfrac{2\,\text{Mid} + \text{Trap}}{3}$.
\item \textbf{Error bounds} let you certify accuracy and choose $n$ in advance: trapezoid $\le \tfrac{(b-a)^3}{12n^2}M_2$, Simpson $\le \tfrac{(b-a)^5}{180n^4}M_4$.
\item \textbf{Real software} uses these as building blocks inside adaptive subdivision (spend effort where $f$ is wild), self-error-estimation by comparing grids, Richardson extrapolation and Romberg tables, and Gaussian quadrature (optimal sample placement). It is always, in the end, a weighted sum $\sum w_i f(x_i)$.
\end{itemize}

The throughline is a single idea, applied with increasing refinement: \emph{replace the curve, locally, by something you can integrate exactly, and the better the local fit, the faster the error dies}. Constant, line, parabola -- and the error exponent climbs $h, h^2, h^4$. That is calculus turning its own definition into an algorithm.

\section{Limitations and next steps}
\label{ch27:reach}

\begin{connectionbox}
Numerical quadrature evaluates definite integrals directly from samples, bypassing
the need for elementary antiderivatives. When formulas like Simpson's rule or
Gauss--Kronrod quadrature are applied to smooth functions in one dimension, accurate
approximations require only a modest number of function evaluations.

However, standard grid-based quadrature faces a major limitation in higher dimensions.
When integrating over a $d$-dimensional domain (a line for $d=1$, a square or other flat region for $d=2$, a box for $d=3$, and so on), a grid of $N$ points per axis requires
$N^d$ total function evaluations (Figure~\ref{ch27:fig-grid}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item In $1$ dimension: $100^1 = 100$ evaluations;
  \item In $3$ dimensions: $100^3 = 10^6$ evaluations;
  \item In $10$ dimensions: $100^{10} = 10^{20}$ evaluations.
\end{itemize}
This exponential growth in computation is known as the \emph{curse of dimensionality}.
Fixed grids quickly become impractical as dimension grows.

In high-dimensional problems, two alternative approaches are standard:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Analytic reductions:} When symmetry exists, coordinate transformations
  and Fubini's theorem (which lets you compute a multidimensional integral as ordinary
  one-variable integrals done one after another) can reduce multidimensional integrals to simpler nested forms
  (Chapter~34).
  \item \emph{Monte Carlo integration:} Evaluate $f$ at $N$ randomly chosen points and
  average the results (named after the casino, because it relies on chance). Its error
  shrinks roughly like $1/\sqrt{N}$, written $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ in the big-O notation of Chapter~9, whatever the dimension $d$.
\end{itemize}
\end{connectionbox}

This marks a change of viewpoint. The first twenty-seven chapters focused on the
continuum, where variables vary continuously. Numerical approximations generate an ordered
list of discrete numbers:
\[
  a_1,\; a_2,\; a_3,\; a_4,\; \dots,\; a_k,\; \dots
\]
where each entry $a_k = T_{2^k}$ is indexed by a positive integer $k \in \N$. As the grid
is refined, this list approaches a definite limit:
\[
  \lim_{k \to \infty} a_k = \int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x.
\]
Such an infinite ordered list is a \emph{sequence}.

Having studied continuous rates of change and continuous accumulation, we now turn to
discrete lists and infinite sums. In Chapter~28, we begin with \emph{Sequences and Their Limits}.

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch27:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental checks]
Answer each of the following without pencil or paper:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $f(x)$ is strictly concave down ($f'' < 0$) on $[a,b]$, will the trapezoidal estimate $T_n$ be strictly greater than or strictly less than the true integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$?
\item You evaluate an integral using the trapezoidal rule with $n = 10$ and obtain an error of $0.04$. If you increase to $n = 20$, approximately what error do you expect?
\item You evaluate the same integral with Simpson's rule and find an error of $0.0016$ with $n = 10$. If you increase to $n = 20$, approximately what error do you expect?
\item For which of the following functions is Simpson's rule with $n = 2$ exact: $f(x) = 4x^3 - 2x + 7$, $g(x) = x^4$, $h(x) = \sin x$?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Estimate $\int_0^2 \sqrt{1 + x^3}\,\dd x$ using the trapezoidal rule with $n = 4$. (Nodes $0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2$; compute each $\sqrt{1+x^3}$ to four decimals, then apply weights $1,2,2,2,1$ and multiply by $h/2$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Estimate the same integral $\int_0^2 \sqrt{1+x^3}\,\dd x$ with Simpson's rule, $n = 4$, reusing your five heights from the preceding exercise but with weights $1,4,2,4,1$ and factor $h/3$. By how much do the two estimates differ?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Compute $\int_0^{\pi} \sin x\,\dd x$ exactly (you know the antiderivative), then estimate it with Simpson's rule using $n = 2$ (nodes $0, \pi/2, \pi$). Find the actual error. Why is it not zero even though $\sin$ is smooth?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
For $\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x$, how large must $n$ be to guarantee the trapezoidal rule is within $10^{-4}$ of the truth? Use $M_2 = 2$. Then find the $n$ needed for Simpson to reach the same tolerance using $M_4 = 12$. Comment on the ratio.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
A car's speed (in m/s) is recorded every $2$ seconds: at $t = 0,2,4,6,8,10,12$ the readings are $0, 8, 17, 22, 24, 25, 25$. This gives $n = 6$ intervals -- an even number, so Simpson applies. Estimate the distance travelled, $\int_0^{12} v\,\dd t$, with (a) the trapezoidal rule and (b) Simpson's rule. Which would you trust more, and why?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Conceptual.) Explain in your own words why the trapezoidal rule \emph{overestimates} the integral of a function that is concave up (like $e^x$) on the whole interval, and underestimates a concave-down function. Tie your answer to the sign of $f''$ in the error term $-\tfrac{h^3}{12}f''(\xi)$ per slice.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Proof.) Show directly that Simpson's rule is exact for every cubic $f(x) = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d$ on a single double-slice $[-h, h]$. That is, compute $\int_{-h}^{h} f\,\dd x$ exactly and compare it to $\tfrac{h}{3}[f(-h) + 4f(0) + f(h)]$, and verify they are equal. Identify which term of $f$ would have to be present (degree $4$) for Simpson to start being wrong.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Proof / derivation.) Show that for the trapezoidal rule applied to a single slice $[x_0, x_1]$ of width $h$, the leading error term is $-\tfrac{h^3}{12}f''(\xi)$ by Taylor-expanding $f$ about the midpoint and integrating, mirroring the symmetric derivation in the chapter.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Self-error estimation.) Using $\int_1^2 \tfrac1x\,\dd x$ with Simpson, compute $S_2$ (i.e. $h = 0.5$) and $S_4$ ($h = 0.25$). Form the estimated error of $S_4$ as $\tfrac{1}{15}(S_4 - S_2)$ and compare it to the actual error $\ln 2 - S_4$. How close is the estimate? This is exactly what adaptive software does.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
(Richardson extrapolation.) Continue the previous exercise: form the combination $S^\star = \tfrac{16 S_4 - S_2}{15}$, which cancels the leading $h^4$ error. Compute $S^\star$ and its error against $\ln 2 = 0.693147$. You should find it dramatically more accurate than either $S_2$ or $S_4$ alone -- this is the first step of Romberg integration.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The sequence of approximations]
Let $f(x) = e^{-x^2}$ on $[0,1]$. For each positive integer $k \in \N$, let $s_k = S_{2^k}$ denote the Simpson's rule approximation with $n = 2^k$ slices.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Write down the first three terms of this list: $s_1 = S_2$, $s_2 = S_4$, $s_3 = S_8$.
\item Explain why the collection $(s_k)_{k=1}^\infty$ is a \emph{sequence} of real numbers, and what it means to write $\lim_{k \to \infty} s_k = \int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x$.
\item Using the error bound $\abs{\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x - S_n} \le \frac{M_4}{180 n^4}$ with $M_4 = 12$, find a power $n = 2^k$ that mathematically guarantees $\abs{\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,\dd x - s_k} < 10^{-10}$. (This challenge-and-response behavior on discrete indices is the foundation of Chapter~28.)
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
