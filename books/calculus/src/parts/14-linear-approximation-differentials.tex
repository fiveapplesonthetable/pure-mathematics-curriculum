\chapter{Linear Approximation and Differentials}

In the previous chapter, we calculated tangent slopes for implicit curves
and related rates. Why spend so much effort finding tangent slopes? Because of
a single geometric fact that sits underneath all of differential calculus:

\begin{center}
\emph{A smooth curve, looked at closely enough, is a straight line.}
\end{center}

Here \emph{smooth} means differentiable: the curve has a definite slope at the
point, with no corner or break. (From Chapter~30 on, where Taylor series need
derivatives of every order, ``smooth'' will mean the stronger ``infinitely differentiable''.) Geometrically, the derivative $f'(a)$ is the slope
of the \emph{tangent line that the curve approaches} when you magnify the graph
near $x=a$.

This chapter replaces a curve by its tangent line near a point and asks how large
an error that causes. The same replacement gives the symbols $\dd x$ and $\dd y$ a
precise meaning as ordinary numbers, tells us how a measurement error in an input
carries through to a computed result, and gives a method for solving equations.

\chapterline

\section{Zooming In: Why Smooth Means Straight}
\label{ch14:zooming}

Take any function you like that is differentiable at a point -- say
$f(x)=x^2$ near $x=1$ -- and imagine doing the following experiment. Plot it.
Then zoom in on the point $(1,1)$: cut the viewing window in half, then in half
again, then again. What happens to the parabola?

It flattens. The more you zoom, the straighter the visible piece of curve looks,
until on a small enough window you cannot tell it from a straight line
drawn with a ruler. Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-zoom} shows three frames of this
experiment.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Differentiability \emph{is} this zoom property. To say $f$ has a derivative at
$a$ is to say: there is a single straight line through $(a,f(a))$ that the curve
approaches under magnification. A function with a corner (like $\abs{x}$ at $0$) fails
the test: no matter how far you zoom into the corner, you still see two
different slopes meeting, never one line (Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-corner}).
\end{intuitionbox}

Why should you care that the curve becomes straight? Because straight lines are
the easiest functions to compute with. A line says ``start
here, go up by the slope times how far you walk.'' No squaring, no exponentials,
no trig tables. If we are willing to accept a small error, we can replace any
hard smooth function, \emph{near a point}, by a line and do arithmetic instead of
harder calculations. This trade, a small error in exchange for much simpler
arithmetic, is made all the time in science and engineering.

\begin{walkbox}
Picture the surface of the Earth. Locally -- in your backyard, on a soccer field
-- it looks flat, and flat geometry (Pythagoras, straight survey lines)
works well. The error only shows up over hundreds of miles, where the
\emph{curvature} (how sharply the surface bends) adds up. The Earth plays the role
of the function. The flat backyard plays the role of its \emph{tangent plane}: the
flat plane that touches the globe at your location, the two-dimensional version of
a tangent line. The error comes from the curvature. Where else in life do you
replace a curved thing by a flat one because the flat version is good enough up
close?
\end{walkbox}

\section{The Tangent Line as the Best Linear Approximation}
\label{ch14:tangent-best}

Now turn the picture into algebra. We have a function $f$ differentiable at a
point $a$. We want the straight line that best matches $f$ near $a$. A straight
line through the point $(a,f(a))$ has the form
\[
L(x) = f(a) + m\,(x-a),
\]
where $m$ is its slope, and the only freedom we have is the choice of $m$. Which
slope makes $L$ hug the curve best?

The zoom picture suggests the answer: pick the slope of the
curve itself at $a$, namely $m=f'(a)$. That gives the \emph{tangent line}. But
``looks best'' is not a definition. Below we say precisely what ``best'' means and
then prove the tangent line wins.

\begin{definition}[Linearization]
\label{ch14:def-linearization}
Let $f$ be differentiable at $a$. The \textbf{linearization} of $f$ at $a$ is the
linear function
\[
L(x) \;=\; f(a) + f'(a)\,(x-a).
\]
The approximation $f(x)\approx L(x)$ for $x$ near $a$ is called the
\textbf{linear} (or \textbf{tangent-line}) \textbf{approximation}.
\end{definition}

The formula for $L$ uses only the function's \emph{height} $f(a)$ and its
\emph{slope} $f'(a)$ at the single point $a$, and nothing else. It is the most a
straight line can know about $f$. Read it aloud: ``Start at the known value
$f(a)$. Walk a distance $(x-a)$. For each unit you walk, climb by the rate
$f'(a)$.''

\subsection{In what sense is it ``best''?}

Compare the tangent line to any other line through $(a,f(a))$ with a different
slope $m\neq f'(a)$. The error of a line $L_m(x)=f(a)+m(x-a)$ is the gap between
the curve and the line,
\[
E_m(x) = f(x) - L_m(x).
\]
At $x=a$ every such line is exact: $E_m(a)=0$, because they all pass through the
point. So the contest is about how fast the error grows as you step away. Write
$x = a+h$, so $h$ is the size of the step, and divide the error by $h$:
\[
\frac{E_m(a+h)}{h} = \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} - m.
\]
As $h\to 0$ the difference quotient $\bigl(f(a+h)-f(a)\bigr)/h$ tends to $f'(a)$.
Therefore
\[
\frac{E_m(a+h)}{h} \;\longrightarrow\; f'(a)-m.
\]
If $m\neq f'(a)$, this limit is a nonzero number; the
error is roughly $(f'(a)-m)\cdot h$, growing \emph{in proportion to} $h$. But if
$m=f'(a)$, the limit is $0$: the error, divided by $h$, approaches zero. The error of the
tangent line shrinks \emph{faster than $h$ itself}. That is the precise sense in
which the tangent line is the uniquely best linear approximation.
Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-best-line} compares the two kinds of error for $f(x)=x^2$
at $a=1$.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The defining property of the tangent line.} Among all lines through
$(a,f(a))$, the tangent line is the only one whose error is \emph{small compared
to the step}: writing $x=a+h$,
\[
\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h) - \bigl[f(a)+f'(a)\,h\bigr]}{h} \;=\; 0.
\]
Every other line leaves an error proportional to $h$; the tangent line leaves an
error that is negligible next to $h$. We say the tangent line agrees with $f$ ``to
first order.''
\end{focusbox}

\begin{whybox}
Why does matching the slope kill the first-order error? Because the derivative
\emph{is} the rate of change at $a$. Near $a$,
\[
f(a+h) = f(a) + f'(a)\,h + (\text{error}),
\]
and the very definition of $f'(a)$ as a limit forces the error to be small
relative to $h$. Choosing the slope to be anything other than $f'(a)$ leaves a
leftover term $(f'(a)-m)h$ that is itself proportional to $h$ -- a first-order
mistake, much larger than the bending of the curve for small $h$. Matching the
slope cancels that term, leaving an error
that scales with $h^2$ as the curvature pulls the curve away from the line.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{A first worked example}

\begin{example}[Approximating a square root]
\label{ch14:ex-sqrt}
Estimate $\sqrt{4.1}$ without a calculator.

Let $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$, and choose the \emph{anchor} (the point where we build the
line) $a=4$, because we know
$\sqrt{4}=2$ exactly and $4$ is close to $4.1$. We need the slope:
\[
f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}, \qquad f'(4) = \frac{1}{2\cdot 2} = \frac14.
\]
The linearization is
\[
L(x) = f(4) + f'(4)\,(x-4) = 2 + \tfrac14 (x-4).
\]
Plug in $x=4.1$:
\[
\sqrt{4.1} \approx L(4.1) = 2 + \tfrac14 (0.1) = 2 + 0.025 = 2.025.
\]
The true value is $\sqrt{4.1} = 2.024845\ldots$, so our estimate is right to four
decimal places. We did it with one division.
\end{example}

Picking a good anchor $a$ means balancing two things: you want a point where $f$ and $f'$
can be computed exactly, and which sits as close to your target as possible.
Here $a=4$ serves both purposes: $4$ is a perfect square and is close to $4.1$.

\begin{labbox}
Estimate $\sqrt{4.4}$ the same way: $L(4.4) = 2 + \tfrac14(0.4) = 2.1$. The true
value is $2.0976\ldots$. The error has grown from about $0.00015$ (at the
step $h=0.1$) to about $0.0024$ (at the step $h=0.4$): the step grew by a factor
of $4$, and the error grew by a factor of about $16=4^2$. That quadratic growth
reflects the curvature, and Section~\ref{ch14:error} explains
why the error scales with $h^2$.
\end{labbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} Look at our estimate $\sqrt{4.1} \approx 2.025$. Before
consulting a calculator: is $2.025$ slightly too large or slightly too small?
Look at the graph of $y=\sqrt{x}$ in Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-sqrt}. As $x$ grows,
the curve gets flatter -- it bends downward (concave down). Any tangent line
drawn to a downward-bending curve rides \emph{above} the curve, so without
computing a single extra digit we know $2.025$ is an overestimate. Check:
$2.025^2 = 4.100625 > 4.1$.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Linearization is a \emph{local} tool. It is only trustworthy near the anchor. If
you used the line $L(x)=2+\tfrac14(x-4)$ to estimate $\sqrt{100}$ you would get
$2+\tfrac14(96)=26$, which is absurd (the truth is $10$). The approximation does
not ``know'' anything about the curve far from $a$ -- it only ever knew the height
and slope at one point. Always ask: is my target close to my anchor?
\end{trapbox}

\section{What the Differentials \texorpdfstring{$\dd y$}{dy} and \texorpdfstring{$\dd x$}{dx} Mean}
\label{ch14:differentials}

You have been writing $\dv{y}{x}$ for chapters now, as a single symbol defined as the
limit of a difference quotient. Now we can give $\dd x$ and $\dd y$ separate
meanings, as two numbers whose ratio is the derivative. Leibniz thought of $\dd x$
as an infinitesimal change, an amount smaller than every positive number yet not
zero; that idea took two centuries to put on solid ground (see the History box
below). Here is the modern definition.

\begin{definition}[The differentials $\dd x$ and $\dd y$]
\label{ch14:def-differentials}
Let $y=f(x)$ be differentiable at $x$. We declare $\dd x$ to be an
\textbf{independent real variable} -- an ordinary number, which you are free to
choose, representing a step in the $x$-direction. Then we \emph{define} the
\textbf{differential of $y$} by
\[
\dd y \;=\; f'(x)\,\dd x.
\]
$\dd y$ is the rise of the \emph{tangent line} over a run of $\dd x$.
\end{definition}

\begin{happeningbox}
\textbf{What $\dd x$ and $\dd y$ are.} They are not zero. They are not
``infinitely small.'' $\dd x$ is a number you pick -- the size of a step.
And $\dd y$ is the corresponding \emph{rise along the tangent line}, not along the
curve. Look at Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-dydx}. Take a real step $\dd x = \Delta x$
from the point $(a,f(a))$. Two things rise:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{curve} rises by $\Delta y = f(a+\Delta x) - f(a)$ -- the true
change;
\item the \emph{tangent line} rises by $\dd y = f'(a)\,\dd x$ -- the linear
estimate of the change.
\end{itemize}
The differential $\dd y$ is the \textbf{approximation}; the increment (actual
change) $\Delta y$ is the \textbf{truth}. The gap between them, $\Delta y - \dd y$, is the error of
linear approximation -- exactly the quantity we studied in the last section. The
notation $\dv{y}{x}=f'(x)$ is now a true quotient of two numbers $\dd y$
and $\dd x$, because that is how we defined $\dd y$. Leibniz's fraction is a real
fraction, provided the rise is measured on the tangent line.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{historybox}
Calculus began not with limits, but with the practical struggle to approximate curved motion using straight lines. In the 1660s and 1670s, Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently discovered that the slope of a curve could be found by imagining a change so small that the curve was effectively straight. Newton thought in terms of motion, speaking of ``fluxions'' (instantaneous velocities) and ``evanescent increments'' (quantities caught at the instant they vanish). Leibniz developed the notation of differentials that we still use: he pictured $\dd x$ as an \emph{infinitesimal}, a quantity smaller than any positive real number you can name, yet greater than zero. With $\dd y = f'(x)\,\dd x$, Leibniz treated derivatives as ratios of geometric lengths, which made the chain rule $\dv{y}{x}=\dv{y}{u}\dv{u}{x}$ look like cancelling fractions.

Their practical results were accurate, but their foundations provoked immediate debate. In 1734, Bishop George Berkeley published his critique \emph{The Analyst}, pointing out a logical inconsistency: in calculating a derivative, mathematicians treated $\dd x$ as nonzero so they could divide by it, and then set $\dd x = 0$ to discard the remaining terms. Within the ordinary real numbers, no single quantity can satisfy both conditions.

The answer took over a century. Meanwhile the methods kept working. In 1768, Leonhard Euler published the first volume of his \emph{Institutiones calculi integralis}, showing that stepping along tangent lines ($y_{n+1} = y_n + f'(x_n)\Delta x$) gives a general numerical method, now called \emph{Euler's method}, for approximately solving \emph{differential equations} (equations that relate an unknown function to its derivatives) that cannot be solved by a formula.

The full theoretical resolution arrived in 1823 with Augustin-Louis Cauchy. Cauchy rebuilt the derivative as the limit of a difference quotient of ordinary real numbers, and proved that linear approximation comes with a guaranteed error bound (we prove it in Section~\ref{ch14:error}): the error $f(x) - L(x)$ is at most $\frac{1}{2} M (x-a)^2$ in size, where $M$ is the largest size of $f''$ between $a$ and $x$. Leibniz's $\dd x$ and $\dd y$ were restored as ordinary, finite numbers: $\dd x$ is a chosen horizontal step, and $\dd y = f'(a)\,\dd x$ is the exact vertical rise of the tangent line.

In the 1960s, the logician Abraham Robinson used mathematical logic to show that Leibniz's infinitesimals can be made logically consistent after all, founding \emph{nonstandard analysis}. The theory of linear approximation for one-variable functions is complete. An open question in applied mathematics is when the linearization of large, complex systems, such as the climate, turbulent fluids, or deep neural networks, stops being a reliable guide.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Differentials as a notation for approximation}

The most used fact about differentials is this approximation:
\[
\boxed{\;\Delta y \;\approx\; \dd y \;=\; f'(x)\,\dd x\;}
\]
``The true change is approximately the linear change.'' The rest of
this chapter applies that boxed line. Here is
Example~\ref{ch14:ex-sqrt} redone in this language.

\begin{example}[$\sqrt{4.1}$ via differentials]
\label{ch14:ex-sqrt-diff}
We want $\sqrt{4.1}$. Anchor at $x=4$, take the step $\dd x = 0.1$. With
$y=\sqrt{x}$ we have $\dd y = \dd x/(2\sqrt{x})$, so
\[
\dd y = \frac{1}{2\sqrt 4}\,(0.1) = \frac{0.1}{4} = 0.025.
\]
Then
\[
\sqrt{4.1} = \sqrt 4 + \Delta y \approx \sqrt 4 + \dd y = 2 + 0.025 = 2.025,
\]
matching what we found before. The differential $\dd y=0.025$ \emph{is} the
estimated change in $\sqrt{x}$ caused by nudging $x$ from $4$ to $4.1$.
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
The boxed approximation $\Delta y \approx f'(x)\,\dd x$ is the one-variable seed
of the \emph{total differential} you will meet in multivariable calculus
(Chapter~32). There $z=f(x,y)$ depends on two inputs, and
\[
\dd z = \pdv{f}{x}\,\dd x + \pdv{f}{y}\,\dd y,
\]
where the \emph{partial derivatives} $\pdv{f}{x}$ and $\pdv{f}{y}$ are the slopes of
$f$ in the $x$- and $y$-directions, and $\dd x$, $\dd y$ are now the steps in the
two inputs. A small move in the plane changes $z$ by
(rate in $x$)$\times$(step in $x$) plus (rate in $y$)$\times$(step in $y$): the
tangent \emph{plane} replaces the tangent line, while the principle -- locally,
smooth equals linear -- stays the same.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Linearization and Error: How Wrong Are You?}
\label{ch14:error}

An approximation is only useful if we know how far off it can be. For linear
approximation we can say precisely how large the error is. Define the error of the linearization at $a$ as
\[
E(x) = f(x) - L(x) = f(x) - \bigl[f(a)+f'(a)\,(x-a)\bigr].
\]
We already know (Section~\ref{ch14:tangent-best}) that $E$ vanishes faster than
the step: $E(a)=0$ and $E(x)/(x-a)\to 0$. That tells us the error is small, but
not \emph{how} small. The next theorem gives the size:
\emph{the error is controlled by the second derivative} $f''$, the derivative of
$f'$. It measures how fast the slope changes, that is, how sharply the graph bends.
In this chapter we use the word \emph{curvature} informally for this bending; a
straight line has none.

\begin{theorem}[Error of linear approximation]
\label{ch14:thm-error}
Suppose $f$ has a continuous second derivative on an interval containing $a$ and
$x$. Then there is a point $c$ between $a$ and $x$ such that
\[
f(x) - \bigl[f(a) + f'(a)\,(x-a)\bigr] \;=\; \frac{f''(c)}{2}\,(x-a)^2.
\]
\end{theorem}

Writing $h = x-a$ for the step, the theorem says
\[
E = \tfrac12\, f''(c)\, h^2.
\]
This tells us three things. First, the error is proportional to $h^2$, not $h$ -- so
halving your distance from the anchor quarters the error, exactly the square law
we saw numerically in the ``Work it yourself'' box. Second, the multiplier is $f''$, the
curvature: a flatter curve (small $f''$) is approximated better, and a perfectly
straight $f$ (with $f''=0$) has \emph{zero} error, as it must. Third, the sign of
$f''$ tells you which way you missed: if the curve is concave up ($f''>0$) the
tangent line lies below it and you underestimate; concave down ($f''<0$) and you
overestimate (Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-concavity}). That is why in Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-sqrt} the line for the
downward-bending $\sqrt{x}$ sat \emph{above} the curve, and our estimate $2.025$
came out slightly high. Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-errgrow} shows the $h^2$ growth
directly: the true error follows a parabola and stays below the guaranteed bound
given in the box after the proof.

\begin{whybox}
Where does the $\tfrac12$ come from, and why $f''$? Think of building $f$ back up
from its derivatives, like reconstructing a journey from your speedometer. The
value $f(a)$ is where you start. The term $f'(a)h$ is how far you would travel if
your speed never changed -- the linear estimate. But your speed \emph{does} change,
at the rate $f''$ (that is what the second derivative is: the rate the slope
changes). Over a step $h$, a steadily changing speed contributes an
\emph{average} extra displacement. Just as distance under constant acceleration is
$\tfrac12 a t^2$ in physics, the curvature contributes $\tfrac12 f'' h^2$. The
$\tfrac12$ is the same $\tfrac12$ that appears in $\tfrac12 at^2$ -- it is the
average of a quantity ramping linearly from $0$ to its full value.
Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-ramp} draws this as areas.
\end{whybox}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{ch14:thm-error}]
This proof uses a result proved in the next chapter, the \emph{Mean Value
Theorem}: if a function is continuous on the closed interval from $a$ to $x$ and
differentiable between them, then at some point strictly between $a$ and $x$ its
slope equals its average slope over the interval. We also use its two-function
version, the \emph{Cauchy Mean Value Theorem}: for two such functions $g$ and $p$,
with $p'$ never zero strictly between $a$ and $x$, there is a point $c_1$ strictly
between $a$ and $x$ where the ratio of total changes equals the ratio of slopes:
\[
\frac{g(x)-g(a)}{p(x)-p(a)} = \frac{g'(c_1)}{p'(c_1)}.
\]
The idea of the proof is to compare the error with $(x-a)^2$.

Fix $a$ and let $x$ vary. If $x=a$, both sides of the claimed formula are $0$, so
assume $x\neq a$. Consider the two functions
\[
g(t) = f(t) - \bigl[f(a)+f'(a)\,(t-a)\bigr],
\qquad
p(t) = (t-a)^2,
\]
both on the closed interval between $a$ and $x$. We want $g(x)/p(x)$. Note that
$g(a)=0$ and $p(a)=0$, and also
\[
g'(t) = f'(t) - f'(a), \qquad p'(t) = 2(t-a).
\]
At every interior point $t$ strictly between $a$ and $x$, we have $p'(t)=2(t-a)\neq 0$
(even though $p'(a)=0$ at the endpoint). So the ratio form of the Cauchy mean value
theorem applies on the interval between $a$ and $x$: there is a point $c_1$ strictly
between $a$ and $x$ with
\begin{align*}
\frac{g(x)}{p(x)} &= \frac{g(x)-g(a)}{p(x)-p(a)} \qquad \text{(since $g(a)=p(a)=0$)}\\
&= \frac{g'(c_1)}{p'(c_1)} \qquad \text{(Cauchy mean value theorem)}\\
&= \frac{f'(c_1)-f'(a)}{2(c_1-a)}.
\end{align*}
Now apply the ordinary mean value theorem to $f'$ on the interval from $a$ to
$c_1$: there is a point $c$ between $a$ and $c_1$ (hence between $a$ and $x$) with
\[
\frac{f'(c_1)-f'(a)}{c_1-a} = f''(c).
\]
Substituting,
\[
\frac{g(x)}{p(x)} = \frac{f''(c)}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
g(x) = \frac{f''(c)}{2}\,(x-a)^2,
\]
which is exactly the claim, since $g(x)=E(x)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Usable error bound.} You rarely know the point $c$, but you do not
need it. If $\abs{f''(t)}\le M$ for all $t$ between $a$ and $x$, then
\[
\bigl|f(x)-L(x)\bigr| \;\le\; \frac{M}{2}\,(x-a)^2.
\]
Replace the unknown $f''(c)$ by an \emph{upper bound} $M$ on the curvature and you get
an \emph{error bar}: a limit the error is guaranteed not to exceed, though the true
error may be smaller.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}[Putting a number on the $\sqrt{4.1}$ error]
\label{ch14:ex-sqrt-bound}
For $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ we have $f''(x) = -\tfrac14 x^{-3/2}$. On the interval
$[4,4.1]$ the largest size of $f''$ occurs at the left end $x=4$ (where
$x^{-3/2}$ is biggest):
\[
\abs{f''(x)} \le \tfrac14 \cdot 4^{-3/2} = \tfrac14\cdot \tfrac18 = \tfrac{1}{32}
= M.
\]
With $h = 0.1$,
\begin{align*}
\bigl|\sqrt{4.1}-2.025\bigr| &\le \frac{M}{2}\,h^2\\
&= \frac{1}{64}\,(0.1)^2 = \frac{0.01}{64} \approx 0.000156.
\end{align*}
The actual error was $0.000154\ldots$, so the bound is almost exactly the true
error, and we obtained it knowing only that $\sqrt{x}$ does not bend too sharply on $[4,4.1]$.
Because $f''<0$ there, we even know the \emph{sign}: the tangent line rides above the
curve, confirming that $2.025$ is a slight overestimate.
\end{example}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Theorem~\ref{ch14:thm-error} is the first case of \emph{Taylor's theorem}
(Chapter~31), which approximates a function near $a$ by polynomials of higher
degree. The pattern continues:
the linear approximation has error of size about $f'' h^2/2$; if you also match
the second derivative you get a quadratic (degree-two) approximation, with error of
size about $f''' h^3/6$, where $f'''$ is the third derivative;
and matching higher derivatives gives errors with higher powers of $h$. Linear
approximation is Taylor's theorem stopped at degree one.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Propagation of Error: Differentials in Practice}
\label{ch14:propagation}

You measure something -- a radius, a voltage, a
time -- and your measurement has an uncertainty. You then \emph{compute} something
from it -- an area, a power, a distance. How does the uncertainty in what you
measured affect what you computed? The answer is one application of
$\dd y = f'(x)\,\dd x$, read in a new way: $\dd x$ is now the \emph{measurement
error}, and $\dd y$ is the resulting error in the computed quantity.
Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-propagation} shows the picture: the tangent line carries an
uncertainty band of width $2\abs{\dd x}$ on the input axis to a band of width
$2\abs{f'(x)}\abs{\dd x}$ on the output axis. A steep graph widens the band; a flat
one narrows it.

\begin{example}[Error in the area of a circle]
\label{ch14:ex-circle}
You measure the radius of a circular plate as $r = 10\,\text{cm}$, with your ruler good
to about $\dd r = \pm 0.05\,\text{cm}$. You report the area $A=\pi r^2$. What is the
uncertainty in $A$?

The area's differential is
\[
\dd A = \dv{A}{r}\,\dd r = 2\pi r\,\dd r.
\]
At $r=10$, $\dd r = 0.05$:
\[
\dd A = 2\pi (10)(0.05) = \pi \approx 3.14\,\text{cm}^2.
\]
So $A = \pi(10)^2 \pm \pi \approx 314.16 \pm 3.14\,\text{cm}^2$. A half-millimeter
slip in the radius costs you about $3\,\text{cm}^2$ of area.
\end{example}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} Suppose you measure the electrical resistance $R$ of a resistor with a $2\%$ uncertainty, and calculate the power $P = I^2 R$ it turns into heat at a known, fixed current $I$. What is the relative uncertainty in $P$, that is, the uncertainty as a percentage of $P$ itself?
Now suppose instead that $R$ is known, while the current $I$ has a $2\%$ uncertainty. What is the relative uncertainty in $P = I^2 R$?
\emph{Answer:} With fixed $I$, $\dd P = I^2\,\dd R$, so $\dd P/P = \dd R/R = 2\%$ (linear scaling). With fixed $R$, $\dd P = 2IR\,\dd I$, so $\dd P/P = (2IR\,\dd I)/(I^2 R) = 2\,\dd I/I = 4\%$: squaring the current doubles its relative error. The same doubling happens for the area of a circle, as we see next.
\end{recoverybox}

The \emph{relative} error $\dd A/A$ is the error as a fraction (or percentage) of
the value itself. It is often more informative than the absolute error $\dd A$,
because it has no units and tells you how many \emph{significant figures} (leading
digits that carry real information) of the answer you can trust. Divide:
\[
\frac{\dd A}{A} = \frac{2\pi r\,\dd r}{\pi r^2} = 2\,\frac{\dd r}{r}.
\]

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Relative error doubles for area, triples for volume.} For $A = \pi r^2$,
\[
\frac{\dd A}{A} = 2\,\frac{\dd r}{r}.
\]
A $1\%$ error in radius becomes a $2\%$ error in area. For a volume $V = \tfrac43
\pi r^3$ the same calculation gives
\[
\frac{\dd V}{V} = 3\,\frac{\dd r}{r},
\]
so a $1\%$ radius error becomes a $3\%$ volume error. For a \emph{power law}, a formula
$y=c\,x^n$ with constants $c$ and $n$, the exponent $n$ is the factor by which the
relative error is multiplied (Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-powerlaw}).
\end{focusbox}

The output error is $n$ times the input error: squaring doubles it, cubing triples
it, and taking a root shrinks it.}
\label{ch14:fig-powerlaw}
\end{figure}

\begin{whybox}
Why does the exponent become the multiplier? Because this is the
power rule written in the language of errors. If $y = x^n$ then $\dd y = n x^{n-1}\,\dd x$,
and dividing by $y=x^n$ gives
\[
\frac{\dd y}{y} = n\,\frac{\dd x}{x}.
\]
The logarithm gives the same result in one line: $\ln y = n\ln x$, so
$\dd y/y = n\,\dd x/x$ is exactly the differential of that identity (since
$\dd(\ln u) = \dd u/u$). Relative error is the \emph{differential of the
logarithm}. That is why, for any product of powers, the relative errors
add up, each multiplied by its exponent -- taking logs turns products into sums,
and differentiation turns the sums into added relative errors.
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[Combining several measurements]
\label{ch14:ex-combine}
A pendulum's period (the time for one full swing) is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, where
$L$ is its length (not a linearization here) and $g$ is the acceleration due to
gravity. Suppose you know $L$ to within
$0.5\%$ and $g$ to within $0.2\%$. How accurately do you know $T$?

Take logarithms first: $\ln T = \ln(2\pi) + \tfrac12\ln L - \tfrac12 \ln g$.
Now take the differential of each side, using $\dd(\ln u)=\dd u/u$:
\[
\frac{\dd T}{T} = \tfrac12\,\frac{\dd L}{L} - \tfrac12\,\frac{\dd g}{g}.
\]
The two errors could have either sign. In the \emph{worst case} they push $T$ the
same way, so for a guaranteed bound we add their sizes:
\begin{align*}
\left|\frac{\dd T}{T}\right| &\le \tfrac12\,(0.5\%) + \tfrac12\,(0.2\%)\\
&= 0.25\% + 0.1\% = 0.35\%.
\end{align*}
Taking logarithms turned a square root and a quotient into a sum of weighted
relative errors. Physics labs use this arithmetic all the time.
\end{example}

\begin{applicationbox}
Error propagation via differentials runs through all of quantitative science.
Surveyors turn measured angles into distances, engineers turn
measured currents into dissipated power $P=I^2R$ (so $\dd P/P = 2\,\dd I/I$, doubling any relative current error),
and economists turn growth rates into doubling times. A machine
learning engineer asks how a small change $\dd x$ in an input shifts a neural
network's output; the answer, $\dd y = f'(x)\,\dd x$, needs the derivative, which
backpropagation (Chapter~11) computes. In every case the question ``how
much does the output wobble when the input wobbles?'' is answered by the
derivative times the input variation.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{The Newton Step: Linear Approximation in Reverse}
\label{ch14:newton}

So far we used the tangent line to estimate a function's \emph{value}. Now turn
the question inside out. Suppose we want to \emph{solve} an equation $f(x)=0$ --
to find a root -- but the equation is too hard to solve exactly. Examples are
$\cos x = x$ and $x^5 - x - 1 = 0$. Neither has a \emph{closed-form} solution: no
formula built from arithmetic, roots and the standard functions gives the answer
exactly. The tangent line gives a method:

\begin{center}
\emph{We cannot solve $f(x)=0$, but we can always solve $L(x)=0$,\\ because $L$
is a straight line.}
\end{center}

Start with a guess $x_0$ near the root. Replace the curve by its tangent line at
$x_0$ -- the best line we know how to build. Then find where \emph{that line}
crosses zero, and call it $x_1$. Because the line approximates the curve, its crossing
is usually much closer to the true root than $x_0$ was. Repeating this process
gives a sequence of guesses $x_0, x_1, x_2, \ldots$ (the \emph{iterates}) that,
when $x_0$ starts close enough, approach the root quickly.
Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-newton} shows the first two steps of this iteration.

To find the formula, write the tangent line at $x_0$:
\[
L(x) = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)\,(x - x_0).
\]
Set $L(x_1)=0$ and solve for $x_1$:
\[
0 = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)\,(x_1 - x_0)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x_1 = x_0 - \frac{f(x_0)}{f'(x_0)}.
\]
Repeating the same step from each new guess is called \emph{iteration}.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Newton's iteration.}
\[
x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
\]
In words: from your current guess, step by ``minus the value over the slope.''
The value $f(x_n)$ tells you how far off you are; dividing by the slope
$f'(x_n)$ converts that vertical miss into the horizontal correction that a
straight line of that slope would need (Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-newton-step}).
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}[Computing $\sqrt 2$ by hand]
\label{ch14:ex-newton-sqrt2}
To find $\sqrt 2$, solve $f(x)=x^2-2=0$, with $f'(x)=2x$. The iteration becomes
\[
x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{x_n^2 - 2}{2x_n} = \frac{x_n}{2} + \frac{1}{x_n},
\]
the ``average your guess with $2/\text{guess}$'' rule (known to the
Babylonians millennia before Newton). Start at $x_0 = 1.5$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
x_1 &= \tfrac{1.5}{2} + \tfrac{1}{1.5} \approx 0.75 + 0.66667 = 1.41667,\\
x_2 &= \tfrac{1.41667}{2} + \tfrac{1}{1.41667} \approx 0.70834 + 0.70588 = 1.41422,\\
x_3 &\approx 1.41421356237.
\end{aligned}
\]
The true value is $\sqrt 2 = 1.41421356237\ldots$. After three steps we are
correct to twelve digits. The number of correct digits roughly
\emph{doubled} each step: $1$, then $3$, then $6$, then $12$
(Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-newton-digits}). This doubling of correct digits is called
\emph{quadratic convergence}, and it comes from using the best linear
approximation.
\end{example}

\begin{whybox}
Why does Newton's method converge so fast? Because the error it leaves
behind comes from the linear-approximation error of Theorem~\ref{ch14:thm-error},
which is proportional to the square of the step. Each step uses the tangent line, whose miss
is $\tfrac12 f''(c) h^2$; so if your current distance to the root is $h$,
your next distance is roughly a fixed multiple of $h^2$. Square a small number and it becomes tiny:
$0.01 \to 0.0001 \to 0.00000001$. The doubling of correct digits per step reflects this
$h \mapsto h^2$ scaling.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Newton's method can fail. If $f'(x_n)=0$ the step divides by
zero -- the tangent is horizontal and never reaches the axis. A bad starting guess
can send the iterates flying away from the root, or trap them in a loop.
Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-newton-fail} shows the first two failures for $f(x)=x^2-2$. And if
the root is one where $f'$ also vanishes (a double root), the fast doubling slows
to a crawl. We treat all of this carefully in Chapter~19; here the point is only
the \emph{idea} -- root-finding is linear approximation run backwards, solving the
line instead of the curve.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{From algebra to geometry: Newton and Raphson.}
Newton described an early version of this method around 1669 for polynomial equations,
manipulating terms algebraically without explicitly picturing tangent slopes. In 1690,
Joseph Raphson streamlined the algebra into the iterative algorithm we use today, which
is why it is often called the \emph{Newton--Raphson method}. The modern formulation
$x_{n+1}=x_n-f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$, framed directly in terms of the derivative and sliding
down the tangent line, emerged once differential calculus matured: the same tangent
line that estimates a curve's height also drives iterative root-finding across
scientific computing.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{The Seed of All of Calculus}
\label{ch14:seed}

One idea, \emph{locally, smooth is linear}, has done all of the following in this
chapter.

\begin{itemize}
\item It gave the derivative a geometric meaning: $f'(a)$ is the slope of the
line the curve becomes under magnification.
\item It gave us \emph{linearization}: replace $f$ by $f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$ to compute
hard values with easy arithmetic.
\item It pinned down \emph{differentials}: $\dd y = f'(x)\,\dd x$ is the tangent
line's rise, and $\dd y/\dd x$ is a true ratio.
\item It powered \emph{error propagation}: the same $\dd y = f'(x)\,\dd x$, read
as ``output wobble equals slope times input wobble.''
\item It produced \emph{Newton's method}: solve the tangent line instead of the
curve, and iterate.
\end{itemize}

The same idea runs through the rest of the book. The \emph{integral} is the same idea
inverted: chop a curved region into slivers so thin that each is a straight-sided
rectangle (smooth $=$ linear on a sliver), then add the slivers up. The
\emph{Fundamental Theorem of Calculus} (Chapter~21) ties the slope picture to the area picture. A
\emph{differential equation} is a recipe that gives you the local slope $\dd
y/\dd x$ at every point and asks you to reassemble the global curve -- which you do
by stepping along tangent lines (this is Euler's method). Taylor series
(Chapter~30) extend linear approximation by matching every derivative at a point,
not only the first.

\begin{aestheticbox}
\textbf{Up close, a smooth function behaves like a straight line.} Differentiation \emph{finds} that local line; integration \emph{adds up} local linear pieces into a total. The chain rule, substitution, Euler's method and Taylor series each turn a curved problem into a locally straight one.
\end{aestheticbox}

\subsection{Limits of the Tangent Line, and the Bridge to Chapter~15}
\label{ch14:what-unlocked}

\begin{connectionbox}
A straight line does not bend. A tangent line assumes that the slope at the anchor point $a$ persists unchanged forever. The moment you step away from $a$, the curvature of the real curve pulls it away from the line, opening a quadratic error gap:
\[
  E(x) = f(x) - L(x) = \frac{f''(c)}{2}\,(x-a)^2.
\]
If a curve bends sharply (large $\abs{f''}$) or if the step $h = x - a$ is large, the straight line quickly becomes inaccurate. A tangent line cannot turn corners, cannot track waves, and cannot anticipate asymptotes.

\textbf{Why we need the Mean Value Theorem next.}
We used Theorem~\ref{ch14:thm-error} to put guaranteed error bars on our estimates and to explain the quadratic convergence of Newton's method. But \emph{why must such a point $c$ exist at all?} Could a smooth curve wander away from its tangent line between $a$ and $x$ without its second derivative showing it?

Answering this needs a theorem that connects the slopes at individual points of an interval to the total change of the function over that interval. That theorem is the \textbf{Mean Value Theorem}, the subject of Chapter~15. Every error guarantee in this chapter rests on it.

\textbf{Looking ahead: allowing approximations to bend.}
Once the Mean Value Theorem puts our error bounds on firm ground, we can generalize the tangent line. In Chapters~30 and~31 (\emph{Power Series and Taylor's Theorem}), we will allow our approximations to bend by matching the second derivative (curvature), third derivative, and beyond:
\[
  P_n(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2 + \cdots + \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n.
\]
Here $f^{(n)}$ is the $n$-th derivative and $n! = 1\cdot 2\cdots n$. With these extra powers of $(x-a)$, these \emph{Taylor polynomials} stay close to the curve over wider intervals; the tangent line is the case $n=1$. Figure~\ref{ch14:fig-taylor} shows this for $\sqrt{x}$ at $a=4$.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch14:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: linearization at a glance]
Without picking up a pen or using paper, consider $f(x) = x^4$ anchored at $a = 1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item What are the value $f(1)$ and the derivative $f'(1)$? Write the linearization $L(x)$ purely in your head.
\item Mentally estimate $(1.002)^4$.
\item Because $f''(x) = 12x^2 > 0$ for all $x$, does your mental estimate sit slightly above or slightly below the exact value?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: relative error scaling]
Answer each of the following using only the rule that the exponent is the error-amplification factor:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If you measure the side $x$ of a square with a $1.5\%$ uncertainty, what is the resulting percentage uncertainty in the computed area $A = x^2$?
\item If you measure the volume $V$ of a sphere and calculate its radius $r$, which is proportional to $V^{1/3}$, does the percentage error in $r$ grow or shrink compared to the error in $V$? By what factor?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Warm-up linearizations]
For each function, find the linearization $L(x)$ at the given anchor $a$, and use
it to estimate the requested value. Compare with a calculator.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}$ at $a=27$; estimate $\sqrt[3]{27.5}$.
\item $f(x)=\sin x$ at $a=0$; estimate $\sin(0.1)$ (radians).
\item $f(x)=\ln x$ at $a=1$; estimate $\ln(1.05)$.
\item $f(x)=(1+x)^{10}$ at $a=0$; estimate $(1.02)^{10}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Differentials directly]
A cube has side length measured as $s = 5.00\,\text{cm}$ with possible error
$\dd s = \pm 0.02\,\text{cm}$. Use the differential of $V=s^3$ to estimate the largest possible
error in the computed volume, and the corresponding \emph{relative} error
$\dd V/V$. State the relative error as a percentage and confirm it equals
$3\,(\dd s/s)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Sign of the error]
Without computing the true value, decide whether the linear approximation of
$f(x)=e^x$ at $a=0$ \emph{over}- or \emph{under}-estimates $e^{0.2}$, and justify
your answer using the sign of $f''$. Then check numerically.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A guaranteed error bar]
Let $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ and anchor at $a=9$. Use the linearization to estimate
$\sqrt{9.3}$, then use the bound $\abs{E}\le \tfrac{M}{2}h^2$ (with $M$ an upper
bound for $\abs{f''}$ on $[9,9.3]$) to produce a guaranteed interval that
contains the true value of $\sqrt{9.3}$. Verify the true value lies inside.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Relative error in a power law]
The kinetic energy of a moving mass is $E = \tfrac12 m v^2$. If the speed $v$ is
known to within $1.5\%$ and the mass $m$ to within $0.4\%$, bound the relative
error in $E$. (Hint: take logarithms first, then differentiate.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Newton by hand]
Apply Newton's method to $f(x) = x^3 - 7$ to estimate $\sqrt[3]{7}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Write the iteration $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ explicitly for this
$f$.
\item Starting from $x_0 = 2$, compute $x_1$ and $x_2$ to five decimal places.
\item How many correct digits did each step gain? Relate this to the
$h\mapsto h^2$ error law.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[When Newton stumbles]
Consider $f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2$ with starting guess $x_0 = 0$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute $x_1$ and $x_2$.
\item Show that the iteration falls into a $2$-cycle ($0 \to 1 \to 0 \to \cdots$)
and never approaches the real root near $x\approx -1.77$. Explain geometrically,
in terms of where the tangent lines point, why the method fails to converge here.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual: what is $\dd y$?]
A student writes: ``$\dd y$ is the change in $y$ when $x$ changes by $\dd x$.''
Correct this sentence so that it is precise. In your corrected version, explain
the difference between $\dd y$ and $\Delta y$, draw the small picture that
distinguishes them, and identify which one the derivative $\dv{y}{x}$ is a ratio
of.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Prove the first-order property]
Let $f$ be differentiable at $a$ and let $L(x) = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$ be its
linearization. Prove directly from the definition of the derivative that
\[
\lim_{x\to a} \frac{f(x)-L(x)}{x-a} = 0,
\]
and explain why no line of a \emph{different} slope can satisfy this. (This is the
precise statement that the tangent line is the unique best linear approximation.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: error formula on a polynomial]
Let $f(x) = x^3$ and anchor at $a=1$.
\begin{enumerate}

\begin{exercise}[Linearization of a product]
Suppose $u$ and $v$ are differentiable at $a$, with values $u(a), v(a)$ and
derivatives $u'(a), v'(a)$. Using $\dd(uv) = u\,\dd v + v\,\dd u$, show that the
relative error of a product is the sum of the relative errors:
\[
\frac{\dd(uv)}{uv} = \frac{\dd u}{u} + \frac{\dd v}{v}.
\]
Interpret this as the differential of the identity $\ln(uv) = \ln u + \ln v$, and
state the analogous rule for a quotient $u/v$.
\end{exercise}
