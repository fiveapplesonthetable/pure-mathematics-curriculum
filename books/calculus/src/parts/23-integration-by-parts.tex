\chapter{Integration by Parts}

In the previous chapter we ran the chain rule backwards: whenever an integrand has
the shape $f(g(x))\,g'(x)$ of a differentiated composition, substitution unwinds it.
Yet many simple-looking integrals defeat substitution:
\[
\int x\,e^{x}\,\dd x, \qquad \int x\sin x\,\dd x, \qquad \int \ln x\,\dd x, \qquad
\int e^{x}\cos x\,\dd x.
\]
None of these arises from the chain rule. Direct substitution fails on
$\int x e^{x}\,\dd x$: setting $u=x$ reproduces the same integral, while
setting $u=e^{x}$ leaves an unsimplified factor of $x = \ln u$. These
integrands are \emph{products} of two unrelated functions -- a polynomial times an
exponential, a polynomial times a sine -- and substitution was designed to invert
compositions, not products.

There is exactly one differentiation rule that creates products: the
\emph{product rule}. So to integrate products, we run the product rule
backwards, as substitution ran the chain rule backwards. The technique that
comes out is called \emph{integration by parts}.
It does not finish an integral in one step the way a lucky substitution
sometimes does. Instead it trades the integral you have for a different one,
in the hope that the new one is easier. The skill is in arranging the
trade so that the new integral is easier.

\section{The Product Rule, Run Backwards}
\label{ch23:product-backwards}

Everything in this chapter comes from the product rule. If $u$
and $v$ are differentiable functions of $x$, the product rule says
\[
\ddx{u\,v} = u\,\dv{v}{x} + v\,\dv{u}{x}.
\]
Read it as a statement about how the area of a little rectangle $u\cdot v$
changes: when both sides are growing, the rectangle grows on two fronts at once,
one strip of width $\dd u$ down the side and one strip of height $\dd v$ along
the top. The product rule adds those two strips. (We drew that picture in
Chapter~11 when we first met the product rule; we will use it again in the next
section.)

Now take antiderivatives of both sides with respect to $x$. Antidifferentiation
undoes differentiation (any constant of integration can be absorbed into the
indefinite integrals on the right), so the left side becomes $u\,v$ itself:
\[
u\,v = \int u\,\dv{v}{x}\,\dd x + \int v\,\dv{u}{x}\,\dd x.
\]
Solve it for the first integral on the right:
\[
\int u\,\dv{v}{x}\,\dd x = u\,v - \int v\,\dv{u}{x}\,\dd x.
\]
Finally, abbreviate $\dd v = \dv{v}{x}\,\dd x$ and $\dd u = \dv{u}{x}\,\dd x$ --
the differentials of Chapter~14, representing the derivative multiplied by the
step $\dd x$. We arrive at the standard integration-by-parts formula:

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Integration by parts.} If $u$ and $v$ are differentiable, then
\[
\boxed{\;\int u\,\dd v \;=\; u\,v \;-\; \int v\,\dd u\;}
\]
and, between limits $a$ and $b$,
\[
\int_{a}^{b} u\,\dd v \;=\; \eval{u\,v}{a}^{b} \;-\; \int_{a}^{b} v\,\dd u.
\]
\end{focusbox}

In words: the integral of $u\,\dd v$ equals the product $uv$ minus the integral
of $v\,\dd u$. The term $uv$ is called the \emph{boundary term}, because in the
definite version it is evaluated only at the two ends (the boundary) of the
interval. You have not solved the integral; you have \emph{swapped} it for
another. The bet is that $\int v\,\dd u$ is easier than $\int u\,\dd v$.

\begin{whybox}
Substitution is the chain rule read right to left; integration by parts is the
product rule read right to left. There are only a handful of ways to
\emph{build} a function by differentiation (sums, products, compositions), so
there are only a handful of ways to \emph{take one apart} by integration. Sums
split term-by-term. Compositions are unwound by substitution. Products are unwound
by parts. When you face an unfamiliar integral, ask first:
``which differentiation rule could have produced an integrand shaped like this?''
The answer tells you which method to try.
\end{whybox}

\begin{happeningbox}
Each piece of the formula is an area. The product $u\,\dd v$ is a thin
horizontal strip of width $u$ and height $\dd v$; the product $v\,\dd u$ is a thin
vertical strip of height $v$ and width $\dd u$. The formula says the horizontal
strips and the vertical strips together fill the rectangle of area $uv$. The next
section draws this picture and uses it to explain \emph{why} the boundary term
$uv$ is there.
\end{happeningbox}

\section{The Picture: Slicing a Rectangle Two Ways}
\label{ch23:picture}

The boundary term $uv$ can look like an accident of the algebra. A second proof,
using only areas of rectangles, shows where it comes from.

Imagine a curve in the $u$--$v$ plane traced out as $x$ runs from $a$ to $b$. At
$x=a$ the point is $(u_a, v_a)$; at $x=b$ it is $(u_b, v_b)$. As $x$ increases,
both $u$ and $v$ change, and the point sweeps a path. Now look at
Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-rectangle}. The big rectangle reaching out to $(u_b,v_b)$
has area $u_b v_b$. The small rectangle reaching to $(u_a,v_a)$ has area
$u_a v_a$. The L-shaped region between them -- the big rectangle minus the small
one -- has area $u_b v_b - u_a v_a$, which is the boundary term
$\eval{uv}{a}^{b}$.

Now slice that L-shaped region two different ways. A \emph{vertical} strip has a
height $v$ and is built from a change in $u$; its area is $v\,\dd u$, and summing
them gives $\int v\,\dd u$, the orange region below the curve. A \emph{horizontal}
strip has a width $u$ and is built from a change in $v$; its area is $u\,\dd v$,
and summing gives $\int u\,\dd v$, the blue region to the left of the curve. The
path of $(u,v)$ is the boundary between the two regions, and together they tile
the entire L. Writing $\int_a^b u\,\dd v$ for $\int_a^b u(x)\,v'(x)\,\dd x$ (which
sweeps $v$ from $v_a$ to $v_b$ as $x$ runs from $a$ to $b$, matching the label
$\int_{v_a}^{v_b} u\,\dd v$ in Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-rectangle}), we get
\[
\int_a^b u\,\dd v \;+\; \int_a^b v\,\dd u \;=\; u_b v_b - u_a v_a
= \eval{uv}{a}^{b}.
\]
Move the second integral to the right and you have integration by parts, obtained
by cutting one region in two ways.

\begin{intuitionbox}
This is why the boundary term is $uv$ and not something else: $uv$ is the area
of the \emph{rectangle} that the two integrals fill between them. Whatever you
``give up'' by not integrating $u\,\dd v$ directly, you get back as the rectangle
minus the other part. No area is created or destroyed; it is only cut into
different strips.
\end{intuitionbox}

This geometric proof needs $u$ and $v$ to be monotonic (each always increasing
or always decreasing) to draw cleanly; otherwise the path doubles back and the
strips overlap. The
algebraic derivation in Section~\ref{ch23:product-backwards} has no such
restriction -- it works for any differentiable $u,v$. The picture explains why
the formula has the shape it has; the algebra is the proof.

\section{Choosing \texorpdfstring{$u$}{u} and \texorpdfstring{$dv$}{dv}}
\label{ch23:choosing}

The formula has a built-in decision. Faced with an integral, you must split the
integrand into two pieces: one piece you will call $u$ (and \emph{differentiate}
to get $\dd u$), and one piece you will call $\dd v$ (and \emph{integrate} to get
$v$). Every product can be split two ways, and usually only one split helps. So
how do you choose? Two principles decide it.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{$u$ should get simpler when you differentiate it.} The whole point
is that $\int v\,\dd u$ is easier than $\int u\,\dd v$. Differentiating $u$ is
your chance to make progress, so pick a $u$ whose derivative is tamer than
itself. A polynomial like $x^3$ is ideal: each derivative knocks the degree down,
and after enough rounds it vanishes. The logarithm $\ln x$ is even better: we
do not yet know how to integrate it, but its \emph{derivative} $1/x$ is
simple, so making $\ln x$ into $u$ converts a hard factor into an
easy one.

\item \textbf{$\dd v$ must be something you can integrate.} You need
$v=\int \dd v$ to be a function you know. There is no point assigning $\dd v$ to a
factor you cannot antidifferentiate. So $e^x\,\dd x$, $\sin x\,\dd x$,
$\cos x\,\dd x$, and powers $x^n\,\dd x$ all make fine $\dd v$'s; something like
$\ln x\,\dd x$ does not (you would be stuck integrating it -- which is the very
problem you started with).
\end{enumerate}

The two principles combine into a priority list, remembered by the made-up word
LIATE, whose letters stand for five kinds of function.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{LIATE} -- a priority order for choosing $u$. When a product mixes two of
these types, let $u$ be whichever appears \emph{earlier} in the list, and let the
rest be $\dd v$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cl}
\textbf{L} & \textbf{L}ogarithmic ($\ln x$, $\log x$) \\
\textbf{I} & \textbf{I}nverse trig ($\arctan x$, $\arcsin x$) \\
\textbf{A} & \textbf{A}lgebraic (powers $x^n$, polynomials) \\
\textbf{T} & \textbf{T}rigonometric ($\sin x$, $\cos x$) \\
\textbf{E} & \textbf{E}xponential ($e^x$, $a^x$) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Earlier in the list $=$ choose as $u$.
\end{focusbox}

Why this order? Going down the list, differentiating helps less and integrating
gets easier (Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-liate}). Logs and inverse trig functions are at
the top because they are hard to integrate but have simple derivatives
($1/x$, $1/(1+x^2)$), so you want them as $u$. Exponentials are at the bottom
because they are easy to integrate and never get simpler when differentiated, so
you want them as $\dd v$. Polynomials sit in the middle: they are easy to
differentiate and to integrate, but differentiating lowers their degree, so when
paired with a trig or exponential factor they go on the $u$ side.

\begin{trapbox}
LIATE is a \emph{heuristic}: a rule of thumb that usually works, with no
guarantee. There are integrals where it points the wrong way or where
\emph{either} choice works. The test is always the two principles
above: did $\int v\,\dd u$ come out simpler than what you started with? If a
choice leads to a worse integral, swap your choice and try the other split.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Ten-second mental check.} Before calculating, look at three test integrands:
$\int x^4 \ln x\,\dd x$, $\int x^3 e^{2x}\,\dd x$, and $\int \arcsin x\,\dd x$. Which
factor should be $u$, and what is $\dd v$?

\emph{Pause and decide before reading on.}
By LIATE:
\begin{itemize}
\item In $\int x^4 \ln x\,\dd x$, the logarithm beats the polynomial (L before A), so $u = \ln x$ and $\dd v = x^4\,\dd x$.
\item In $\int x^3 e^{2x}\,\dd x$, the polynomial beats the exponential (A before E), so $u = x^3$ and $\dd v = e^{2x}\,\dd x$.
\item In $\int \arcsin x\,\dd x$, there is no second factor, so invent one: $u = \arcsin x$ (I beats everything available) and $\dd v = 1\,\dd x$.
\end{itemize}
If your instincts matched all three, you are ready for the worked examples.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{historybox}
The mnemonic LIATE is modern---introduced in a 1983 paper by Herbert Kasube to help
students choose $u$ and $\dd v$. The underlying technique dates to the development of
calculus in the late seventeenth century.

In 1694, Johann Bernoulli observed that Leibniz's product rule,
\[
  \dd(uv) = u\,\dd v + v\,\dd u,
\]
could be integrated to yield $\int u\,\dd v = uv - \int v\,\dd u$, shifting the derivative
from one factor to the other. Brook Taylor published the same method independently in
his 1715 treatise \emph{Methodus Incrementorum Directa et Inversa}, written in
Newton's language of fluxions (rates of change).

Continental mathematicians, using Leibniz's differential notation, adopted the technique
readily. Euler applied it repeatedly to obtain \emph{reduction formulas}
(Section~\ref{ch23:reduction}) such as the one for
$\int \sin^n x\,\dd x$, which give a modern route to Wallis's 1655 formula for $\pi/2$
(Wallis himself found it decades earlier by interpolating tables of integrals). Later
mathematicians used the same idea to solve differential equations and to give a
meaning to the derivative of functions whose graphs have corners or jumps.
\end{historybox}

\section{First Worked Examples}
\label{ch23:examples}

Each example follows four steps: choose $u$ and $\dd v$; differentiate
to get $\dd u$ and integrate to get $v$; assemble $uv - \int v\,\dd u$; finish
the leftover integral.

\begin{example}[The archetype: $\int x e^{x}\,\dd x$]
\label{ch23:ex-xex}
Here the two factors are algebraic ($x$) and exponential ($e^x$). LIATE says: A
beats E, so $u=x$ and $\dd v = e^x\,\dd x$. Then
\[
u = x \;\Rightarrow\; \dd u = \dd x, \qquad
\dd v = e^x\,\dd x \;\Rightarrow\; v = e^x.
\]
Assemble:
\begin{align*}
\int x e^{x}\,\dd x &= uv - \int v\,\dd u = x e^{x} - \int e^{x}\,\dd x\\
&= x e^{x} - e^{x} + C = (x-1)e^{x} + C.
\end{align*}
Differentiating $u=x$ turned it into $1$, leaving the elementary integral
$\int e^x\,\dd x$.
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Check Example~\ref{ch23:ex-xex} by differentiating the answer. By the product
rule, $\ddx{(x-1)e^x} = 1\cdot e^x + (x-1)e^x = e^x + xe^x - e^x = xe^x$. It
matches the integrand. While you are learning, verify every by-parts answer this
way: differentiation is fast, and it catches sign errors, which the minus sign in
the formula makes easy to commit.
\end{labbox}

\begin{trapbox}
What if you had chosen the other way, $u=e^x$ and $\dd v = x\,\dd x$? Then
$\dd u = e^x\,\dd x$ and $v = x^2/2$, and the formula gives
\[
\int x e^x\,\dd x = \frac{x^2}{2}e^x - \int \frac{x^2}{2}e^x\,\dd x.
\]
The new integral has $x^2$ where the old one had $x$ -- it got \emph{worse}. The
polynomial went up in degree because we integrated it instead of differentiating
it. This is the most common wrong turn, and LIATE warns you off it:
never integrate the polynomial when there is an exponential or trig factor
waiting to take the $\dd v$ role. Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-choice} sets the two
choices side by side.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{example}[A single function: $\int \ln x\,\dd x$]
\label{ch23:ex-lnx}
Here there is no second factor to work with. The trick is
to \emph{invent} one by writing $\ln x = (\ln x)\cdot 1$. Now it is a product, and
LIATE is decisive: L beats everything, so $u = \ln x$ and $\dd v = 1\,\dd x$.
Then
\[
u = \ln x \;\Rightarrow\; \dd u = \frac{1}{x}\,\dd x, \qquad
\dd v = \dd x \;\Rightarrow\; v = x.
\]
Assemble:
\begin{align*}
\int \ln x\,\dd x &= (\ln x)(x) - \int x\cdot\frac{1}{x}\,\dd x\\
&= x\ln x - \int 1\,\dd x = x\ln x - x + C.
\end{align*}
The key simplification is the cancellation $x\cdot\frac{1}{x}=1$: differentiating $\ln x$
produces $1/x$, which pairs with the factor $x$ from $v$ to leave the basic integral
$\int 1\,\dd x$. The same approach works for other functions that are easy to
differentiate but have no obvious antiderivative: logarithmic
and inverse trigonometric functions all yield to $u = f(x)$ and $\dd v = \dd x$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Inverse trig: $\int \arctan x\,\dd x$]
\label{ch23:ex-arctan}
Same trick. Write $\arctan x = (\arctan x)\cdot 1$, take $u=\arctan x$ and
$\dd v = \dd x$, so $\dd u = \dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\,\dd x$ and $v=x$. Then
\[
\int \arctan x\,\dd x = x\arctan x - \int \frac{x}{1+x^2}\,\dd x.
\]
The leftover integral is a substitution problem. Set $w = 1+x^2$, so
$\dd w = 2x\,\dd x$ and
\[
\int \frac{x}{1+x^2}\,\dd x = \frac12\int\frac{\dd w}{w} = \frac12\ln(1+x^2).
\]
(No absolute value is needed, since $1+x^2>0$.) Hence
\[
\int \arctan x\,\dd x = x\arctan x - \tfrac12\ln(1+x^2) + C.
\]
Parts turned the problem into a new integral, and substitution finished it.
Many integrals need more than one method in sequence like this.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A polynomial that needs two rounds: $\int x^2 \sin x\,\dd x$]
\label{ch23:ex-x2sin}
Here $u=x^2$ (algebraic, beats trig) and $\dd v = \sin x\,\dd x$, so
$\dd u = 2x\,\dd x$ and $v = -\cos x$. One round:
\begin{align*}
\int x^2\sin x\,\dd x &= -x^2\cos x - \int(-\cos x)(2x\,\dd x)\\
&= -x^2\cos x + 2\int x\cos x\,\dd x.
\end{align*}
We have not finished, but we have made progress: the power dropped from $x^2$ to
$x$. Attack $\int x\cos x\,\dd x$ by parts again, with $u=x$,
$\dd v = \cos x\,\dd x$, $\dd u = \dd x$, $v = \sin x$:
\[
\int x\cos x\,\dd x = x\sin x - \int \sin x\,\dd x = x\sin x + \cos x.
\]
Substitute back:
\begin{align*}
\int x^2\sin x\,\dd x &= -x^2\cos x + 2\big(x\sin x + \cos x\big) + C\\
&= -x^2\cos x + 2x\sin x + 2\cos x + C.
\end{align*}
Each application of parts lowered the polynomial's degree by one. A degree-$n$
polynomial times $\sin x$ or $e^x$ reduces to a basic integral after exactly $n$
rounds. For such integrals there is a shortcut, tabular integration, which we
meet in Section~\ref{ch23:tabular}. Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-x2sin} plots the
integrand and the answer.
\end{example}

\begin{walkbox}
Integration by parts comes from the
product rule, yet $\ln x$ is a single function, not a visible product. Why did
writing $\ln x = (\ln x)\cdot 1$ make progress instead of going in circles?
Because integrating $1$ introduces $x$, while differentiating $\ln x$ produces
$1/x$ -- and those two factors cancel in $v\,\dd u = x\cdot(1/x)\,\dd x$. What
other single functions have derivatives simple enough to cancel $x$ cleanly?
\end{walkbox}

\section{Reduction Formulas: Parts as a Machine}
\label{ch23:reduction}

When the integrand contains a \emph{parameter} -- a fixed number such as a power
$n$ that we may set to $0, 1, 2, \dots$, giving a whole family of integrals -- a single
application of parts can express the integral with parameter $n$ in terms of the
\emph{same} integral with a smaller parameter. Such a formula is called a
\emph{reduction formula}. It is a \emph{recursive} rule: it gives each member of
the family from an earlier one. Once you know the first one or two members, the
formula produces all the others, for every $n$.

\begin{example}[The reduction formula for $\int x^{n} e^{x}\,\dd x$]
\label{ch23:ex-redxn}
Let
\[
I_n = \int x^n e^x\,\dd x,
\]
where $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ is a whole number.
Take $u = x^n$ and $\dd v = e^x\,\dd x$, so $\dd u = n x^{n-1}\,\dd x$ and
$v = e^x$. Parts gives
\begin{align*}
I_n &= x^n e^x - \int e^x \cdot n x^{n-1}\,\dd x\\
&= x^n e^x - n\int x^{n-1}e^x\,\dd x = x^n e^x - n\,I_{n-1}.
\end{align*}
So
\[
\boxed{\,I_n = x^n e^x - n\,I_{n-1}.}
\]
Each use of parts lowers the exponent by one. Starting from
$I_0 = \int e^x\,\dd x = e^x$, we can climb the ladder to any $n$
(Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-ladder}). For instance (leaving out $+C$ until the end),
\begin{align*}
I_1 &= x e^x - 1\cdot I_0 = xe^x - e^x,\\
I_2 &= x^2 e^x - 2 I_1 = x^2 e^x - 2(xe^x - e^x)\\
    &= x^2 e^x - 2x e^x + 2e^x,\\
I_3 &= x^3 e^x - 3 I_2 = x^3 e^x - 3x^2 e^x + 6x e^x - 6 e^x.
\end{align*}
The signs alternate, and the coefficients for $I_3$ are
$1$, $3$, $3\cdot 2$, $3\cdot 2\cdot 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{whybox}
Why do reduction formulas exist at all? Because parts can trade a hard factor
for a smaller version of itself. When you differentiate $x^n$ you get
$n x^{n-1}$ -- structurally the same kind of object, one notch simpler. The
integral on the right is therefore a copy of the integral on the left with the
parameter lowered by one. Whenever differentiating one factor gives a smaller
copy of that factor -- powers $x^n$, $(\ln x)^n$, and (with a trig identity)
$\sin^n x$ and $\cos^n x$ -- parts will give you a reduction formula.
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[A trig reduction: $\int \sin^{n}x\,\dd x$]
\label{ch23:ex-redsin}
This one needs one extra idea beyond parts, so we go through it in full. Let
\[
J_n = \int \sin^n x\,\dd x \qquad (n = 0, 1, 2, \dots).
\]
Split off one factor of sine: write $\sin^n x = \sin^{n-1}x\cdot \sin x$, and take
\[
u = \sin^{n-1}x, \qquad \dd v = \sin x\,\dd x,
\]
so that $\dd u = (n-1)\sin^{n-2}x\,\cos x\,\dd x$ and $v = -\cos x$. Parts:
\[
J_n = -\sin^{n-1}x\,\cos x + (n-1)\int \sin^{n-2}x\,\cos^2 x\,\dd x.
\]
Now use the Pythagorean identity $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$ to turn the leftover
integral back into sines:
\[
\int \sin^{n-2}x\,\cos^2 x\,\dd x
= \int \sin^{n-2}x\,\dd x - \int \sin^{n}x\,\dd x = J_{n-2} - J_n.
\]
Substitute:
\[
J_n = -\sin^{n-1}x\,\cos x + (n-1)\big(J_{n-2} - J_n\big).
\]
The integral $J_n$ has \emph{reappeared on the right} -- this is the recurring
integral trick we will name in the next section. Collect the $J_n$ terms on the
left:
\begin{align*}
J_n + (n-1)J_n &= -\sin^{n-1}x\,\cos x + (n-1)J_{n-2},\\
n\,J_n &= -\sin^{n-1}x\,\cos x + (n-1)J_{n-2},
\end{align*}
and divide by $n$:
\[
\boxed{\,J_n = -\frac{1}{n}\sin^{n-1}x\,\cos x + \frac{n-1}{n}J_{n-2}.}
\]
This drops the power by \emph{two} each time, so there are two starting
points: $J_0 = \int \dd x = x$ and
$J_1 = \int \sin x\,\dd x = -\cos x$. Every even power reduces down to $J_0$,
every odd power down to $J_1$ (Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-parity}).
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
The reduction formula for $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^n x\,\dd x$ leads to a famous
formula for $\pi$. Write
\[
W_n = \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^n x\,\dd x \qquad (n = 0, 1, 2, \dots);
\]
these numbers are called the \emph{Wallis integrals}. Evaluated between $0$ and
$\pi/2$, the boundary term $-\frac1n\sin^{n-1}x\cos x$ is zero at both ends
($\sin 0 = 0$ and $\cos(\pi/2) = 0$), so the recursion involves only numbers:
\[
W_n = \frac{n-1}{n}\,W_{n-2}.
\]
As
$n$ increases, the graph of $\sin^n x$ is pushed closer to the $x$-axis
everywhere except near $x=\pi/2$ (Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-wallis}), so $W_n$ shrinks.
The even and odd cases end differently: even powers reduce to
$W_0 = \int_0^{\pi/2} 1\,\dd x = \frac{\pi}{2}$ and so carry a factor of $\frac{\pi}{2}$,
whereas odd powers reduce to $W_1 = \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x\,\dd x = 1$ and are
fractions. Since $0\le\sin x\le 1$ on $[0,\pi/2]$, higher powers are smaller:
\[
\sin^{2k+1}x \le \sin^{2k}x \le \sin^{2k-1}x,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
W_{2k+1} \le W_{2k} \le W_{2k-1}.
\]
Dividing by $W_{2k+1}$ gives $1 \le W_{2k}/W_{2k+1} \le W_{2k-1}/W_{2k+1} = \frac{2k+1}{2k}$.
The right-hand side tends to $1$, so the ratio $W_{2k}/W_{2k+1}$
is squeezed to $1$ as $k\to\infty$. Writing out that ratio gives John
Wallis's 1655 product
\[
\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{2}{1}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac{4}{5}
\cdot\frac{6}{5}\cdot\frac{6}{7}\cdots.
\]
This is an \emph{infinite product}: multiplying the first few fractions gives a
number near $\pi/2$, and multiplying more of them gets as close to $\pi/2$ as you
like. Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-wallisproduct} shows the running products zig-zagging
in on $\pi/2$, from above and below in turn.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{The Recurring-Integral Trick}
\label{ch23:recurring}

Sometimes applying integration by parts twice causes the original integral to
\emph{reappear} on the right-hand side. Then we can solve for the unknown
integral by algebra, as we solve $K = A - K$ to get $K = A/2$. We saw this in
the $\sin^n$ reduction formula. The simplest example is an integrand combining
an exponential and a trigonometric function.

\begin{example}[Solving for the integral: $\int e^{x}\cos x\,\dd x$]
\label{ch23:ex-excos}
Call it $K = \int e^x\cos x\,\dd x$. Neither factor gets simpler when
differentiated -- $e^x$ stays $e^x$, and $\cos x$ cycles to $-\sin x$ to
$-\cos x$. LIATE would pick $u=\cos x$ (T before E), but here either choice
works, as long as we keep it. Let us take $u = e^x$, $\dd v = \cos x\,\dd x$. Then
$\dd u = e^x\,\dd x$, $v = \sin x$:
\[
K = e^x\sin x - \int e^x\sin x\,\dd x.
\]
The new integral is no simpler, but it is the same \emph{type}. Apply parts again
to $\int e^x\sin x\,\dd x$, keeping the same choice $u = e^x$,
$\dd v = \sin x\,\dd x$, so $v = -\cos x$:
\[
\int e^x\sin x\,\dd x = -e^x\cos x + \int e^x\cos x\,\dd x = -e^x\cos x + K.
\]
Substitute this back into the expression for $K$:
\[
K = e^x\sin x - \big(-e^x\cos x + K\big) = e^x\sin x + e^x\cos x - K.
\]
Now $K$ appears on both sides. (Treat $K$ here as one fixed antiderivative; the
general constant $+C$ is added at the end.) Add $K$ to both sides and divide by $2$:
\begin{gather*}
2K = e^x\sin x + e^x\cos x,\\
\boxed{\,K = \int e^x\cos x\,\dd x = \frac{e^x(\sin x + \cos x)}{2} + C.}
\end{gather*}
We never ``finished'' an integral in the usual sense; we caught the unknown
integral in an equation and solved for it. Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-cycle} shows the
loop, and Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-excos} plots the integrand with the answer.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
A common error in the recurring-integral technique is to \emph{switch the assignment of $u$
midstream}. If you set $u = e^x$ in the first round and then switch roles by setting
$u = \sin x$ (with $\dd v = e^x\,\dd x$) in the second, the second application
undoes the first: you arrive at $K = K$, or $0=0$. To avoid this, keep the same role
assignment in both rounds: if you choose the exponential as $u$ in the first step,
choose the exponential as $u$ in the second step as well. That consistency is what
produces $-K$ rather than $+K$, so you can solve.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{happeningbox}
Why does the integral come back rather than terminating? Differentiating
$\sin x$ gives $\cos x$, and differentiating $\cos x$ gives $-\sin x$: the pair
never leaves itself, and $e^x$ is its own derivative. So no factor
ever simplifies to zero the way a polynomial eventually does; the process can only
cycle through a few shapes. After two rounds the trig
factor is back where it started, up to a sign and the boundary terms that
piled up along the way.
\end{happeningbox}

\section{Tabular Integration: Parts on Autopilot}
\label{ch23:tabular}

When one factor is a polynomial that differentiates down to zero -- like $x^3$ or
$x^2$ -- and the other is something you can integrate over and over without effort
-- like $e^x$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$ -- repeated integration by parts becomes
mechanical. A bookkeeping shortcut, often called the \emph{tabular
method} or the \emph{DI method}, does all the rounds at once and keeps the
signs organized.

The recipe: make two columns. In the $D$ column (for
\emph{D}ifferentiate) put your polynomial $u$ and keep differentiating down to
$0$. In the $I$ column (for \emph{I}ntegrate) put the $\dd v$ function and keep
integrating, one row for each row of the $D$ column. Then draw diagonal arrows
from each entry in $D$ to the entry one row \emph{down} in $I$, attach alternating
signs $+,-,+,-,\dots$ starting with $+$, and read off the products.

\begin{example}[Tabular: $\int x^3 e^{x}\,\dd x$]
\label{ch23:ex-tabx3}
Differentiate $x^3$ down to zero; integrate $e^x$ (it never changes):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c c c c}
\toprule
sign & $D$ (differentiate) & & $I$ (integrate) \\
\midrule
$+$ & $x^3$ & $\searrow$ & $e^x$ \\
$-$ & $3x^2$ & $\searrow$ & $e^x$ \\
$+$ & $6x$ & $\searrow$ & $e^x$ \\
$-$ & $6$ & $\searrow$ & $e^x$ \\
& $0$ & & $e^x$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Multiply each $D$ entry by the $I$ entry one row down, with the sign on its left:
\begin{align*}
\int x^3 e^x\,\dd x
&= +x^3 e^x - 3x^2 e^x + 6x e^x - 6 e^x + C\\
&= (x^3 - 3x^2 + 6x - 6)e^x + C.
\end{align*}
This matches the reduction formula's $I_3$ in Example~\ref{ch23:ex-redxn}, with
far less writing than three separate rounds of parts. Figure~\ref{ch23:fig-tabular} diagrams the complete flow.
\end{example}

\begin{whybox}
Tabular integration is repeated integration by parts organized in a table. The first
diagonal $+x^3\cdot e^x$ is the boundary term $uv$. The next row down is exactly
the $-\int v\,\dd u$ that parts hands you, but instead of writing the integral
sign you immediately apply parts to \emph{it}, producing the next $uv$ term and
the next leftover -- and so on. The alternating signs are the chain of minus signs
from each application of the formula. The table terminates precisely because
differentiating the polynomial eventually gives $0$, which makes the final leftover
integral zero. So the table carries out every step of the recursion in
Example~\ref{ch23:ex-redxn} at once.
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[Tabular with a trig factor: $\int x^2\cos x\,\dd x$]
\label{ch23:ex-tabx2cos}
Differentiate $x^2$ to zero; integrate $\cos x \to \sin x \to -\cos x \to
-\sin x$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c c c c}
\toprule
sign & $D$ & & $I$ \\
\midrule
$+$ & $x^2$ & $\searrow$ & $\cos x$ \\
$-$ & $2x$ & $\searrow$ & $\sin x$ \\
$+$ & $2$ & $\searrow$ & $-\cos x$ \\
& $0$ & & $-\sin x$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Read the diagonals with signs:
\begin{align*}
\int x^2\cos x\,\dd x
&= +x^2\sin x - 2x(-\cos x) + 2(-\sin x) + C\\
&= x^2\sin x + 2x\cos x - 2\sin x + C.
\end{align*}
The signs in the answer come from \emph{two} sources -- the alternating $\pm$ of
the table and the minus signs that appear inside the $I$ column as $\cos$
integrates to $\sin$ to $-\cos$. Write out both signs explicitly before
simplifying so neither one gets dropped.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
Tabular integration in its simplest form requires the $D$ column to terminate at
$0$ -- that is, $u$ must be a polynomial. If \emph{neither} factor differentiates
to zero (as with $\int e^x\cos x\,\dd x$, where both columns cycle indefinitely), the
table does not end. You can still use it: stop at the row where the product of
the two columns is the original integrand again (up to a constant factor), and
solve for the integral by algebra as in Section~\ref{ch23:recurring}.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Putting It All Together: A Harder Example}
\label{ch23:harder}

\begin{example}[$\int x\arctan x\,\dd x$]
\label{ch23:ex-xarctan}
Two factors: algebraic $x$ and inverse-trig $\arctan x$. LIATE places I above A, so
$u = \arctan x$ and $\dd v = x\,\dd x$. Then
\[
\dd u = \frac{1}{1+x^2}\,\dd x, \qquad v = \frac{x^2}{2}.
\]
Parts:
\[
\int x\arctan x\,\dd x
= \frac{x^2}{2}\arctan x - \frac12\int \frac{x^2}{1+x^2}\,\dd x.
\]
The leftover is a rational function (a ratio of polynomials) whose top has the
same degree as its bottom. Divide, as in polynomial long division: add and
subtract $1$ on top to get
\[
\frac{x^2}{1+x^2} = \frac{(1+x^2) - 1}{1+x^2} = 1 - \frac{1}{1+x^2}.
\]
Hence
\[
\int \frac{x^2}{1+x^2}\,\dd x = \int 1\,\dd x - \int\frac{1}{1+x^2}\,\dd x
= x - \arctan x.
\]
Assemble the final answer:
\begin{align*}
\int x\arctan x\,\dd x
&= \frac{x^2}{2}\arctan x - \frac12\big(x - \arctan x\big) + C\\
&= \frac{x^2+1}{2}\arctan x - \frac{x}{2} + C.
\end{align*}
Three techniques in one problem: parts to break it open, division to
simplify the rational leftover, and the known antiderivative of $1/(1+x^2)$.
Each step turns the problem into a slightly easier one until no integral is left.
\end{example}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Look at what happened to $\arctan x$ in Example~\ref{ch23:ex-xarctan}: differentiating it
traded a transcendental function (one not built from polynomials and roots, as in
Chapter~2) for the algebraic fraction $\frac{1}{1+x^2}$, so the remaining integral
needed only algebra. Whenever a function has an
algebraic derivative -- $\ln x$, $\arcsin x$, $\arctan x$ -- integration by parts converts a
transcendental integral into a rational or algebraic one.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Integration by parts is used throughout mathematics and science.
\begin{itemize}
\item In \textbf{probability}, the average value of a waiting time (how long until
a bulb burns out, say) can be computed in two ways: from how the waiting times are
spread out, or from the chance of still waiting at each time $x$. Integration by
parts turns one formula into the other.
\item In \textbf{physics}, the equations of motion of classical mechanics (the
Euler--Lagrange equations) are derived by integrating by parts to move a
derivative off a small trial change in a path and onto a known function.
\item In \textbf{signal processing} and the study of differential equations, the
same move gives a meaning to the ``derivative'' of a signal with a sudden jump,
which has no derivative in the usual sense: it is \emph{defined} as whatever
integration by parts would produce.
\item In \textbf{numerical work}, applying parts again and again gives accurate
approximations to integrals that have no formula.
\end{itemize}
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Why It Works, One More Time}
\label{ch23:whyworks}

With the examples behind us, here is what every one of them has in common.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{The one-sentence summary.} Integration by parts moves a derivative from
one factor of a product to the other, paying a boundary term $uv$ for the move.
\end{intuitionbox}

To see that, rewrite the formula with the differentials spelled out:
\[
\int u\,v'\,\dd x = uv - \int u'\,v\,\dd x.
\]
On the left the prime (the derivative) sits on $v$, and on the right it sits on
$u$. The two
integrals
\[
\int u\,v'\,\dd x \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \int u'\,v\,\dd x
\]
are the same product with the derivative \emph{relocated} from one factor to the
other, and the cost of relocation is the rectangle $uv$ evaluated at the
endpoints. You apply integration by parts whenever moving the
derivative makes the integrand simpler:
\begin{itemize}
\item Move the derivative \emph{onto} a polynomial repeatedly and it becomes
$0$ ($x^n \to \dots \to 0$); the process ends. (This is tabular integration.)
\item Move the derivative \emph{off} a log or inverse-trig function, which we
could not integrate, and it becomes a simple rational factor. (This is
the $\int\ln x\,\dd x$ trick.)
\item Move the derivative around a closed family like $\{e^x,\sin x,\cos x\}$ and
it cycles back, letting you solve for the integral algebraically. (This is the
recurring trick.)
\end{itemize}
Every example in this chapter is one of these three motions. The formula is one
line; the judgment is in seeing which direction to push the derivative so the
problem gets smaller.

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Statement and proof, for the record.} Let $u,v:[a,b]\to\R$ be continuous
with continuous derivatives $u',v'$ on $[a,b]$. Then $uv$ is differentiable and,
by the product rule, $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$, which is continuous. By the Fundamental
Theorem of Calculus,
\[
\eval{u(x)v(x)}{a}^{b} = \int_a^b (uv)'\,\dd x = \int_a^b u'v\,\dd x + \int_a^b uv'\,\dd x.
\]
Rearranging gives the definite form
\[
\int_a^b uv'\,\dd x = \eval{uv}{a}^{b} - \int_a^b u'v\,\dd x;
\]
dropping the
limits and writing $\dd v = v'\,\dd x$, $\dd u = u'\,\dd x$ gives the indefinite
form. The only hypothesis is that $u'$ and $v'$ are continuous so that the
Fundamental Theorem applies -- everything else is algebra. $\qed$
\end{formalbox}

\section{Limitations and next steps}

\begin{connectionbox}
Integration by parts succeeds when shifting the derivative produces a simpler integrand.
For products like $x e^x$ or $x\sin x$, differentiating the polynomial factor eventually
reduces it to a constant. For solitary functions like $\ln x$ and $\arctan x$, choosing
$\dd v = \dd x$ trades a transcendental function for an algebraic rational function.

Parts does not help with everything:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Non-simplifying products:} For integrals such as $\int \frac{e^x}{x}\,\dd x$,
  differentiating $1/x$ produces higher negative powers ($-\int \frac{e^x}{x^2}\,\dd x$),
  while integrating $1/x$ produces $\int e^x \ln x\,\dd x$. Neither choice leads to an
  elementary antiderivative.
  \item \emph{Rational functions:} Integrands consisting of polynomial fractions,
  such as $\int \frac{x^3 + 5x - 2}{x^4 - 16}\,\dd x$, cannot be simplified effectively by parts.
\end{enumerate}

To integrate rational functions systematically, Chapter~24 develops the method of
\emph{partial fractions}. By factoring the denominator, any rational function can be
decomposed into simpler terms whose antiderivatives involve only logarithms and arctangents.
\end{connectionbox}

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Warm-ups]
\label{ch23:exr-warmup}
Before putting pencil to paper, do a mental check on each part: identify which
factor should be $u$, which should be $\dd v$, and whether a single round will
suffice. Then evaluate each indefinite integral, stating your choices clearly.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\displaystyle\int x\cos x\,\dd x$
\item $\displaystyle\int x e^{-x}\,\dd x$
\item $\displaystyle\int x\,\ln x\,\dd x$
\item $\displaystyle\int \arcsin x\,\dd x$
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Two rounds and tabular]
\label{ch23:exr-tworounds}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Evaluate $\displaystyle\int x^2 e^{x}\,\dd x$ by applying parts twice.
\item Now redo it with the tabular method and confirm you get the same answer.
\item Use the tabular method to evaluate $\displaystyle\int x^3\sin x\,\dd x$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Definite integrals]
\label{ch23:exr-definite}
Evaluate, simplifying fully:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x e^{x}\,\dd x$
\item $\displaystyle\int_1^{e} x^2\ln x\,\dd x$
\item $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi} x\sin x\,\dd x$
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The recurring trick]
\label{ch23:exr-recurring}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Evaluate $\displaystyle\int e^{x}\sin x\,\dd x$ by applying parts twice and
solving for the integral. (Be consistent in your choice of $u$.)
\item Evaluate $\displaystyle\int e^{2x}\cos(3x)\,\dd x$ the same way.
\item Explain in one or two sentences why choosing $u$ inconsistently makes the
method collapse to $0=0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Reduction formulas]
\label{ch23:exr-reduction}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Derive the reduction formula
$\displaystyle\int (\ln x)^n\,\dd x = x(\ln x)^n - n\int(\ln x)^{n-1}\,\dd x$.
\item Use it to evaluate $\displaystyle\int (\ln x)^2\,\dd x$.
\item Derive the reduction formula
\[
\int\cos^n x\,\dd x = \frac{1}{n}\cos^{n-1}x\,\sin x
+ \frac{n-1}{n}\int\cos^{n-2}x\,\dd x,
\]
mirroring the $\sin^n$ derivation in Example~\ref{ch23:ex-redsin}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Combining with substitution]
\label{ch23:exr-combine}
Each of these needs a substitution \emph{first}, then parts. Find them.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\displaystyle\int \cos(\sqrt{x})\,\dd x$ \quad (try $w=\sqrt{x}$).
\item $\displaystyle\int x^3 e^{x^2}\,\dd x$ \quad (try $w=x^2$, then parts).
\item $\displaystyle\int \sin(\ln x)\,\dd x$ \quad (try $w=\ln x$, then the
recurring trick).
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual]
\label{ch23:exr-concept}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item A student integrates $\int x e^x\,\dd x$ by choosing $u=e^x$,
$\dd v = x\,\dd x$ and gets a \emph{worse} integral. Explain, in terms of the two
principles in Section~\ref{ch23:choosing}, exactly what went wrong.
\item Explain why $\int \sec x\,\dd x$ cannot be cracked by the
``$u=\sec x,\ \dd v=\dd x$'' trick that worked for $\ln x$ and $\arctan x$. What is
different about $\sec x$? (You do not have to evaluate it.)
\item Why does the geometric ``two ways of slicing a rectangle'' proof in
Section~\ref{ch23:picture} require $u$ and $v$ to be monotonic, while the
algebraic derivation does not?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A proof]
\label{ch23:exr-proof}
Prove the \textbf{Wallis recursion} for the definite integrals
$\displaystyle W_n = \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^n x\,\dd x$: show that
$W_n = \dfrac{n-1}{n}\,W_{n-2}$ for $n\ge 2$. Begin from the reduction formula in
Example~\ref{ch23:ex-redsin} and explain carefully why the boundary term
$-\frac1n\sin^{n-1}x\cos x$ vanishes at \emph{both} endpoints $x=0$ and
$x=\pi/2$. Then compute $W_0$ and $W_1$, and use the recursion to find
$W_4$ and $W_5$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Going further]
\label{ch23:exr-further}
Use integration by parts to establish the reduction formula for powers of
$\sec$:
\[
\int \sec^n x\,\dd x = \frac{\sec^{n-2}x\,\tan x}{n-1}
+ \frac{n-2}{n-1}\int \sec^{n-2}x\,\dd x \qquad (n\ge 2).
\]
\textit{Hint:} write $\sec^n x = \sec^{n-2}x\cdot\sec^2 x$, let
$\dd v = \sec^2 x\,\dd x$ (so $v=\tan x$), and use $\tan^2 x = \sec^2 x - 1$. You
will need to solve for the recurring integral, just as in
Section~\ref{ch23:recurring}.
\end{exercise}
