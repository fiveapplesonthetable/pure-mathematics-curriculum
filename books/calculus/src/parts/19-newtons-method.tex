\chapter{Newton's Method}

We have spent many chapters learning to differentiate. We can take a function,
find its derivative, read off the slope of its tangent line at any point, and use
that tangent line as the best straight-line stand-in for the curve nearby. In the
previous chapter, on L'H\^opital's rule, the same principle resolved
indeterminate forms: when a ratio collapsed into $\tfrac{0}{0}$, replacing the
curves by their local linear rates revealed the true limit. In this chapter the
same tangent line gives a practical \emph{algorithm} (a fixed recipe of steps that
can be followed mechanically, by hand or by a computer) for solving equations. We
met it briefly in Section~\ref{ch14:newton}; here we study it properly.

Some equations can be solved by rearranging: from $2x - 6 = 0$ you read off
$x = 3$. Others cannot, for example
\[
  x^5 - 3x + 1 = 0, \qquad \cos x = x, \qquad e^{-x} = x^2.
\]
For these there is \emph{no formula}: nothing like the quadratic formula, and no
algebraic trick, will produce the answer. Yet these equations do have real
solutions; if you sketch the curves you can see roughly where one lies. The
question is: how do you \emph{find} the number, to as many decimal places as you
please, when no formula exists?

\begin{focusbox}
Given a function $f$, find a number $r$ with $f(r) = 0$ -- a \textbf{root} of $f$ --
when there is no formula for $r$. Many practical problems take this form once
everything is moved to one side of the equation: finding a balance point
(equilibrium), a break-even point, where two orbits cross, or the interest rate
that makes a loan's payments add up. Newton's method is the standard
\emph{numerical} method for such equations, meaning one that produces the answer
as a decimal number, to whatever accuracy you ask for, rather than as a formula.
\end{focusbox}

The strategy is built entirely out of the tangent line. You guess. Your guess is
wrong, but instead of throwing it away you \emph{improve} it: you replace the
curve with its tangent line, solve the resulting linear equation exactly, and take
that as your next guess. Then you repeat. The guesses close in on the true root
quickly, often doubling the number of correct digits at every step.

\section{The idea: replace the curve with its tangent}
\label{ch19:the-idea}

Start with a picture and a guess. We want a root of some function $f$ -- a
place where its graph crosses the horizontal axis. Suppose we have a current guess,
call it $x_0$. Almost certainly $x_0$ is not the root: the point $(x_0, f(x_0))$
sits above or below the axis, not on it.

The curve near $x_0$ is hard to work with, but the \emph{tangent line} at $x_0$
is easy: it is a straight line, and finding where a straight line crosses the axis
takes one line of algebra. So we make a bargain: we pretend, for one step, that
the function \emph{is} its tangent line. We slide
down (or up) that tangent line until it hits the axis, and wherever it lands we
call our new, improved guess $x_1$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The whole method in one sentence: \emph{you cannot easily find where the curve hits
the axis, so instead you find where its tangent line hits the axis, and you bet
that this is closer.} The tangent line hugs the curve near $x_0$, so if $x_0$ is
already in the neighborhood of the root, the place the tangent crosses is a much
better estimate than $x_0$ was. Repeat the bargain from the new point and the
estimates close in.
\end{intuitionbox}

Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-onestep} shows a single step. The guess $x_0$ is off to the
right of the true root $r$. We climb up to the point on the curve, draw the tangent
there, and follow it down to the axis. It lands at $x_1$, noticeably closer to $r$
than $x_0$ was.

What remains is to turn ``follow the tangent to the axis'' into a formula, and
then to turn the formula into a loop.

\section{Deriving the iteration formula}
\label{ch19:formula}

We are standing at $x_0$. The point on the curve is $(x_0, f(x_0))$, and the slope
of the tangent there is $f'(x_0)$. The tangent line is therefore
\[
  y = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)\,(x - x_0).
\]
This is the linear approximation of $f$ at $x_0$ -- the same
formula we built in the chapter on differentials. We want the place where this line
crosses the horizontal axis, that is, where $y = 0$. Set the right-hand side to zero
and solve for $x$:
\begin{align*}
  0 &= f(x_0) + f'(x_0)\,(x - x_0),\\
  x - x_0 &= -\frac{f(x_0)}{f'(x_0)},\\
  x &= x_0 - \frac{f(x_0)}{f'(x_0)}.
\end{align*}
That landing point is our new guess $x_1$. Doing the same from $x_1$ gives $x_2$,
and so on. Written for a general step, this is a \textbf{recurrence}: a rule that
produces each term of a sequence from the one before it.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Newton's iteration.} Starting from a guess $x_0$, generate a sequence by
\[
  \boxed{\,x_{n+1} = x_n - \dfrac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}\,}
  \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
\]
Each new estimate is the old one corrected by the amount $f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$. When the
$x_n$ stop changing (to the number of digits you care about) and $f(x_n)$ is
zero to that accuracy, you have your root.
\end{focusbox}

The correction term $f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ has a simple meaning. The numerator
$f(x_n)$ is how far off the axis you are -- your ``vertical error.'' The denominator
$f'(x_n)$ is the slope, the rate at which the function climbs. Dividing height by
slope gives a \emph{horizontal} distance: ``if I am this high up and the ground
rises at this rate, I must back up this far to reach ground level.'' It is the
base of the triangle in Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-onestep}: run equals rise divided by
slope.

\textbf{Pause and think:} What happens if your starting guess $x_0$ happens to be
the exact root $r$? Then $f(x_0) = 0$, the correction term $f(x_0)/f'(x_0)$
vanishes, and $x_1 = x_0$. The iteration immediately stands still; an exact root
is a resting point of the method.

\begin{happeningbox}
Newton's method \emph{inverts the linear
approximation}. Linear approximation answered the forward question: ``given a small
step $\Delta x$, how much does $f$ change?'' -- the answer being $\Delta f \approx
f'(x)\,\Delta x$. Newton's method runs that backwards. It asks: ``I want $f$ to
change by a specific amount, namely by $-f(x_n)$ so as to land on zero -- what step
$\Delta x$ achieves that?'' Solving $f'(x_n)\,\Delta x = -f(x_n)$ gives $\Delta x =
-f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$, and stepping by that amount is the update. So the iteration is
linear approximation solved for the input instead of the output. The reason it is
only \emph{approximate} -- the reason you must iterate rather than land on the root
in one shot -- is that the curve is not a straight line, so the real $f$ is
not yet zero at $x_{n+1}$. But it is closer, and from the closer point the linear
approximation is even better, so the next correction is smaller still.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{whybox}
Why does following the tangent get you closer to the root at all? Because at the
point of tangency the tangent line and the curve agree to \emph{first order},
meaning they have the same value and the same slope. So over a short horizontal span they part
company only slowly, by an amount governed by the curvature (the second
derivative). If your guess is already reasonably near the root, the stretch between
guess and root is short, the tangent and curve are nearly indistinguishable across
it, and the tangent's crossing is nearly the curve's crossing. The error you make
by using the line instead of the curve is small, and, as we will prove
shortly, it is small \emph{compared to the error you started with}: each step's
leftover error is roughly proportional to the \emph{square} of the previous error.
Squaring a small number gives a much smaller one, and that is where the speed
comes from.
\end{whybox}

\section{A first worked example: square roots by hand}
\label{ch19:sqrt-example}

A good way to see the method work is to compute a number you already know. Take
$\sqrt2$. It is the positive root of
\[
  f(x) = x^2 - 2, \qquad f'(x) = 2x.
\]
Plug into the iteration:
\begin{align*}
  x_{n+1} &= x_n - \frac{x_n^2 - 2}{2 x_n}
           = x_n - \frac{x_n}{2} + \frac{1}{x_n}\\
          &= \frac{x_n}{2} + \frac{1}{x_n}
           = \frac{1}{2}\left(x_n + \frac{2}{x_n}\right).
\end{align*}
That last form is known as the \emph{Babylonian method} for square roots, used
thousands of years before Newton: ``average your guess with $2$ divided by your
guess.''

Start, deliberately, with a poor guess: $x_0 = 2$.

\begin{labbox}
Carry these out yourself with a calculator; watch the digits lock in.
\begin{align*}
  x_0 &= 2 \\
  x_1 &= \tfrac12\!\left(2 + \tfrac{2}{2}\right) = \tfrac12(3) = 1.5 \\
  x_2 &= \tfrac12\!\left(1.5 + \tfrac{2}{1.5}\right) = \tfrac12(1.5 + 1.3333\ldots) = 1.41666\ldots \\
  x_3 &= \tfrac12\!\left(1.416667 + \tfrac{2}{1.416667}\right) = 1.4142156\ldots \\
  x_4 &= 1.41421356237\ldots
\end{align*}
The true value is $\sqrt2 = 1.41421356237\ldots$
\end{labbox}

Compare the errors -- the distance from each iterate to $\sqrt2$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cl}
\toprule
$n$ & error $\abs{x_n - \sqrt2}$ \\
\midrule
$0$ & $0.586$ \\
$1$ & $0.0858$ \\
$2$ & $0.00245$ \\
$3$ & $0.0000021$ \\
$4$ & $0.0000000000016$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Reading down the column, the error goes from
about half, to about a tenth, to a few thousandths, to a couple of millionths, to a
couple of \emph{trillionths}. Roughly speaking, the number of correct decimal digits
\emph{doubles} at every step: $0$ correct, then $1$, then $2$--$3$, then $5$, then
$11$. After four steps from a lazy guess of $2$ we have $\sqrt2$ to eleven places.
This doubling of correct digits is the mark of \textbf{quadratic convergence},
which Section~\ref{ch19:quadratic} explains and proves.

\begin{applicationbox}
The square-root key on your calculator, and the
\texttt{sqrt} function in every programming language's math library, does
not consult a table. It runs an iteration of this kind, typically
Newton's method or a close relative, started from a good first guess and run
enough times to fill the available digits. Division itself, on some
processors, is computed by Newton's method applied to $f(x) = 1/x - a$, which finds
$1/a$ using only multiplication and subtraction -- no division at all.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Watching convergence in pictures}
\label{ch19:pictures}

The formula is short, but the method is geometric, so watch several steps unfold
on a graph. Take a cubic equation that cannot be factored into simpler pieces with
rational coefficients (coefficients that are fractions of whole numbers):
\[
  f(x) = x^3 - x - 2 = 0.
\]
There is a formula for the roots of a cubic (Cardano's formula, from the 1500s,
the cubic cousin of the quadratic formula), but it writes the root as nested cube
roots and square roots and is too unwieldy for quick numerical work. A sketch shows
a single real root a little past $1.5$. Start at $x_0 = 2$.
Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-cubic} shows the first two steps.

The numbers behind the picture:
\[
  x_0 = 2, \quad x_1 = 1.63636\ldots, \quad x_2 = 1.53039\ldots,
  \quad x_3 = 1.52144\ldots, \quad x_4 = 1.52138\ldots
\]
By $x_4$ the digits have stopped moving at the precision shown. Each new point
sits closer to the root because each tangent is taken at a point closer to the
curve's actual crossing, where the line and the curve are harder to tell apart.

\begin{walkbox}
Take this on a walk. In Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-cubic} the curve is concave up (it
bends upward) and the root is approached \emph{from the right}, each guess landing
to the left of the last but never overshooting past the root. Picture instead a
curve that is concave \emph{down} near its root, approached from the right. Sketch
a tangent. Does the iterate stay on the same side of the root, or jump across it?
Convince yourself that the curvature and which side you start on together decide
whether Newton's method marches in steadily from one side or zig-zags across the
root closing in.
\end{walkbox}

\section{When it works -- and when it fails}
\label{ch19:failures}

Newton's method is fast, but it is not foolproof. It works only under certain
conditions, and there are four classic ways for those conditions to fail. You need
to know them to use the method safely.

\subsection{The happy case: a good start near a simple root}

When everything goes right, two conditions hold. First, the starting guess $x_0$ is
\emph{close enough} to a root $r$. Second, the root is \emph{simple}, meaning
$f'(r) \ne 0$: the curve crosses the axis at a slant rather than touching it
while flat. Under those conditions convergence is quadratic: the count of correct
digits roughly doubles at every step, as in the examples so far.

The trouble is the phrase ``close enough.'' Newton's method is a \emph{local}
method: it is guaranteed to work once you are in some neighborhood of the root,
but it says almost nothing about how to get into that neighborhood in the first
place. Starting far away, anything can happen.

\subsection{Failure 1: the derivative vanishes -- a flat spot}

Look again at the formula:
\[
  x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
\]
If ever $f'(x_n) = 0$, you are dividing by zero and the method breaks down.
Geometrically, where the derivative is zero the tangent line is \emph{horizontal},
and a horizontal line either never meets the axis or lies along it. There is no
``where the tangent crosses'' to step to. If your guess lands very near a flat
spot of $f$, the tangent is nearly horizontal and it throws your next guess a long
way off, possibly farther from the root than you began
(Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-flat}).

\subsection{Failure 2: a bad start -- running off to infinity}

Even with a perfectly nice function, a careless starting point can send the
iterates marching off to infinity instead of inward to the root. The classic
demonstration is
\[
  f(x) = \arctan x,
\]
whose only root is $x = 0$. Its graph flattens out toward the horizontal asymptotes
$y=\pm\pi/2$ as $\abs{x}$ grows, so far from the origin the slope is tiny. There is
a cutoff value $x^\star \approx 1.3917$: started exactly at $x_0 = x^\star$, the
tangent lands exactly at $-x^\star$, the next tangent lands back at $x^\star$, and
the guesses bounce between the two forever. Start with $\abs{x_0}$ larger than
$x^\star$ -- say $x_0 = 1.6$, as in Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-overshoot} -- and the
shallow tangent overshoots the root, landing farther out on the opposite side at
$x_1 \approx -2.0$. The next step overshoots further still to $x_2 \approx 3.55$,
and the guesses keep jumping from side to side, farther out each time, with
$\abs{x_n} \to \infty$. For $\abs{x_0} < x^\star$ Newton's method converges to
$0$; for $\abs{x_0} > x^\star$ it runs away. So ``close enough'' can have a sharp,
exact boundary. Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-arctan-basin} marks the two regions on the
number line.

\begin{trapbox}
A common and dangerous habit is to run a fixed number of Newton steps and
\emph{trust the result without checking}. If the start was bad, your final $x_n$ may
have nothing to do with any root: it might be drifting toward infinity, or
bouncing back and forth, or have settled near a different root than you wanted.
Always check by plugging back in: confirm that $f(x_n)$ is small. The fact that
the $x_n$ have stopped changing is no substitute for evaluating the function.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Failure 3: a cycle that never lands}

The guesses can also fall into a \emph{loop}, bouncing between two values forever
and never approaching any root, even though one exists. The standard example is
\[
  f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2.
\]
Take $x_0 = 0$. Then $f(0) = 2$ and $f'(0) = -2$, so
\[
  x_1 = 0 - \frac{2}{-2} = 1.
\]
Now from $x_1 = 1$: $f(1) = 1 - 2 + 2 = 1$ and $f'(1) = 3 - 2 = 1$, so
\[
  x_2 = 1 - \frac{1}{1} = 0.
\]
We are back to $0$. The next step sends us to $1$ again, then back to $0$, forever:
$0 \to 1 \to 0 \to 1 \to \cdots$ (Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-cycle}). A loop like this
through two values is called a \textbf{two-cycle}. The method never escapes it,
even though $f$ has a real root near $-1.77$.

\subsection{Failure 4: a double root slows everything to a crawl}

The final ``failure'' is gentler: the method still converges, but it loses its
speed. This happens at a \textbf{multiple root}, a root $r$ where $f(r) = 0$ and
also $f'(r) = 0$, so the curve does not cross the axis at a slant but touches it
while flat.
Polynomials make the name clear: a root $r$ is a \emph{double root} when the
factor $(x-r)$ appears twice, as in $(x-r)^2$, and its \textbf{multiplicity} is
the number of times that factor repeats.
Take
\[
  f(x) = (x-1)^2, \qquad r = 1 \text{ (a double root)}.
\]
The iteration becomes
\[
  x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{(x_n-1)^2}{2(x_n-1)} = x_n - \frac{x_n - 1}{2}
          = \frac{x_n + 1}{2}.
\]
Starting at $x_0 = 2$ this gives $1.5,\ 1.25,\ 1.125,\ 1.0625,\ \ldots$; the error
only \emph{halves} each step. That is steady but slow \textbf{linear}
convergence: the error shrinks by the same factor every step. Halving gains one
binary digit per step, or about one decimal digit every three to four steps, far
from doubling the digits. Near the root the derivative $f'(x_n)$ in the
denominator is heading toward zero along with the numerator $f(x_n)$, and
quadratic convergence is lost (Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-doubleroot}).
(There are modified versions of Newton's method that restore fast convergence at
multiple roots whose multiplicity is known, but the plain method crawls.)

\begin{intuitionbox}
To sum up: Newton's method is a \emph{local} method. Near a simple root,
with a good initial guess, it converges fast; but a
flat derivative, a distant start, an unlucky cycle, or a multiple root can stall
it, send it to the wrong place, or make it run away. The practical approach is to
find a reasonable starting guess first, often with a slow but \emph{reliable}
method such as bisection (Chapter~8), then apply Newton's method to compute the
root to full precision, and \emph{always check the answer} by evaluating $f$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{historybox}
The algorithm bears Isaac Newton's name, but it reached its modern form in stages. In 1669, Newton wrote a manuscript titled \emph{De analysi per aequationes numero terminorum infinitas} (circulated privately to colleagues and published only in 1711). In it, Newton demonstrated his method on a single cubic equation:
\[
  y^3 - 2y - 5 = 0.
\]
Newton noted that $y=2$ is a close guess (giving $8 - 4 - 5 = -1$). Setting $y = 2 + p$, he expanded
\[
  (2+p)^3 - 2(2+p) - 5 = -1 + 10p + 6p^2 + p^3 = 0.
\]
Treating the terms $6p^2$ and $p^3$ as negligibly small, he solved $-1 + 10p \approx 0$, giving $p = 0.1$. He then set $p = 0.1 + q$, substituted again, and repeated the process. Newton's version was purely algebraic, required expanding the polynomial anew at every stage, and made no reference to derivatives, slopes, or tangent lines.

In 1690, \textbf{Joseph Raphson} published \emph{Analysis aequationum universalis}. Raphson replaced Newton's chain of changing variables ($y \to p \to q$) with a single rule that produces each new approximation directly from the original polynomial. Newton's own manuscript was not printed until 1711, and the algorithm is often called the \textbf{Newton--Raphson method}.

The next step belongs to \textbf{Thomas Simpson}. In 1740, in \emph{Essays on Several Curious and Useful Subjects in Speculative and Mixed Mathematicks}, Simpson described the method using fluxions (Newton's name for derivatives), which let it apply to equations that are not polynomials. His rule is, in modern notation, $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$. Simpson also extended the method to two equations in two unknowns solved together. So it took about seventy years for Newton's algebraic procedure to become the calculus-based method we use today; the picture of following a tangent line was added by later writers.

Hard questions remained. In 1879, the British mathematician \textbf{Arthur Cayley} asked: if you run Newton's method on $z^3 - 1 = 0$ using \emph{complex numbers} (numbers $a + bi$ with $i^2 = -1$, pictured as points $(a,b)$ in a plane), which starting guesses lead to which of the three roots? The set of starting guesses that lead to a given root is called that root's \emph{basin of attraction}. Cayley could answer the question for quadratic equations but not for this cubic. Pierre Fatou and Gaston Julia studied such questions around 1918--1920, and once computers could draw the basins, in the 1980s, their boundaries turned out to be \emph{fractals}: shapes with endlessly repeating detail at every scale, where the smallest nudge to your starting guess can change which root you reach. A related question, how to choose starting points that are guaranteed to work quickly for systems of polynomial equations, was posed by Steve Smale in 1998 as the 17th problem on his list of open problems for the twenty-first century; it was largely settled between 2009 and 2017.
\end{historybox}

\section{Why it is so fast: quadratic convergence}
\label{ch19:quadratic}

We have repeatedly claimed that, in the happy case, the number of correct digits
\emph{doubles} each step. Here is the proof. It is short, and it shows exactly
which features of $f$ make the method fast.

The tool is the error formula for linear approximation,
Theorem~\ref{ch14:thm-error}: if $f$ has a continuous second derivative, then
\[
  f(x) = f(a) + f'(a)\,(x-a) + \tfrac12 f''(c)\,(x-a)^2
\]
for some point $c$ between $a$ and $x$. (This is \emph{Taylor's theorem to second
order}: linear approximation with its error term written out exactly.) Apply it
with $a = x_n$, the current guess, and $x = r$, the true root, and call the
in-between point $\xi$ (the Greek letter ``xi''):
\[
  f(r) = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)\,(r - x_n) + \tfrac12 f''(\xi)\,(r - x_n)^2.
\]
The left side is $f(r) = 0$,
because $r$ is the root. So
\[
  0 = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)\,(r - x_n) + \tfrac12 f''(\xi)\,(r - x_n)^2.
\]
Now divide the whole equation by $f'(x_n)$. This is allowed because we are
assuming a simple root, $f'(r) \ne 0$, so $f'$ is not zero for $x_n$ near $r$:
\[
  0 = \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} + (r - x_n) + \frac{f''(\xi)}{2 f'(x_n)}\,(r - x_n)^2.
\]
Move the first two terms to the left-hand side, noting that $-(r - x_n) = x_n - r$:
\[
  x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} - r = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2 f'(x_n)}\,(r - x_n)^2.
\]
The first two terms are precisely the definition of $x_{n+1}$:
\[
  \underbrace{x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}}_{=\,x_{n+1}} - r
  = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2 f'(x_n)}\,(r - x_n)^2.
\]
Define the error at step $n$ as $e_n = x_n - r$. Then $(r - x_n)^2 = e_n^2$, and the
left side is exactly $x_{n+1} - r = e_{n+1}$. We have arrived at the master equation
of Newton's method:
\[
  \boxed{\,e_{n+1} = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2 f'(x_n)}\; e_n^{\,2}\,}.
\]

\begin{focusbox}
The new error
$e_{n+1}$ is proportional to the \emph{square} of the old error $e_n$. Once $e_n$ is
small -- say $0.01$ -- its square is $0.0001$, which is a hundred times smaller. The
next error is about the square of \emph{that}, around $10^{-8}$, and the next around
$10^{-16}$. Each step roughly doubles the count of accurate digits. That squaring is
quadratic convergence, and it comes from the geometry: the tangent line matches the
curve's value \emph{and} slope, so the leftover disagreement is governed by the
next term, the curvature $f''$, multiplying the square of the gap.
\end{focusbox}

\textbf{Pause and think:} Why does squaring the error double the number of correct
decimal places? Write the error as a power of ten: an error of about $10^{-3}$
means about three digits after the decimal point are correct. Squaring it gives
$(10^{-3})^2 = 10^{-6}$, six correct places. Square again, and you reach
$10^{-12}$, twelve correct places. Squaring doubles the exponent.

Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-convergence} makes the contrast between quadratic and linear
convergence visible. We plot the number of correct digits against the step number
for our $\sqrt2$ run (a simple root, quadratic) and for the double-root crawl
$f(x)=(x-1)^2$ from Section~\ref{ch19:failures} (linear). The quadratic curve
bends sharply upward -- each step buys roughly twice the digits of the last -- while
the linear one climbs at a fixed, plodding pace.

The master equation also explains the failure modes:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The constant out front.} Near the root, $\xi$ and $x_n$ are both close
  to $r$, so the error is multiplied by about
  $C = \abs{f''(r)}/\bigl(2\abs{f'(r)}\bigr)$. If the curve crosses steeply --
  $f'$ large, $f''$ modest -- then $C$ is small and convergence is fast. If
  $f'(x_n)$ is near zero (the flat-tangent failure), the factor
  $f''(\xi)/\bigl(2f'(x_n)\bigr)$ is huge and a step can throw you far away.
\item \textbf{Why ``close enough'' matters.} The relation $e_{n+1} \approx C e_n^2$
  shrinks the error only if $C\,\abs{e_n} < 1$, i.e.\ only once $\abs{e_n} < 1/C$.
  Inside that radius each step shrinks the error and the squaring takes over; outside
  it, squaring a too-large number makes things \emph{worse}. That threshold is the
  precise meaning of ``start close enough.''
\item \textbf{Why double roots crawl.} At a double root $f'(r) = 0$, so the
  derivation above breaks -- you cannot divide by $f'(x_n)$ cleanly, because it too is
  heading to zero. A more careful analysis (which we leave to the exercises) shows
  the error then merely \emph{halves} each step: linear, not quadratic. The whole
  speed advantage came from $f'(r) \ne 0$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{aestheticbox}
The very same feature that made the
tangent line a \emph{good} approximation -- that it matches the curve to first order,
leaving an error of size $(\Delta x)^2$ -- is what makes Newton's method
\emph{fast}. The quadratic error of linear approximation
turns into the quadratic \emph{convergence} that
doubles the number of accurate digits per step.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Newton's method as fixed-point iteration}
\label{ch19:fixed-point}

Newton's method belongs to a larger family of root-finding algorithms, the
\textbf{fixed-point iterations}. Placing it there explains \emph{why} Newton
converges so much faster than the other members of the family.

A fixed-point iteration is any rule of the shape
\[
  x_{n+1} = g(x_n),
\]
for some function $g$. You feed in a number, the function gives the next number,
and you keep feeding the output back in. If the sequence settles down to a limit
$x^\star$ and $g$ is continuous, then taking limits on both sides gives
$x^\star = g(x^\star)$: the limit is a point that $g$ leaves \emph{fixed}. That is
what a \textbf{fixed point} is -- an input that $g$ returns unchanged.

Newton's method is exactly such an iteration with the particular choice
\[
  g(x) = x - \frac{f(x)}{f'(x)}.
\]
At a root $r$ of $f$ we have $f(r) = 0$, so $g(r) = r - 0 = r$. The
roots of $f$ (with $f' \ne 0$) are exactly the fixed points of Newton's $g$, so
hunting a root of $f$ is the same as hunting a fixed point of $g$.

\begin{connectionbox}
The standard picture of a fixed-point iteration is the \emph{cobweb diagram}: plot
$y = g(x)$ and the line $y = x$ on the same axes; a fixed point is where the curve
meets the diagonal. The iteration traces a path that goes vertically to the curve
(this computes $g(x_n)$), then horizontally to the diagonal (this makes $g(x_n)$ the
next input), then vertically to the curve again, and so on.
Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-cobweb-cos} shows one. Whether the path closes \emph{in on}
the fixed point or moves \emph{away} from it is governed by one number: the slope
$g'(x^\star)$ at the fixed point. If $\abs{g'(x^\star)} < 1$, then near the fixed
point $g$ is a \emph{contraction} (it pulls nearby points closer together) and the
iteration homes in; if $\abs{g'(x^\star)} > 1$ it pushes points apart and the
iteration moves away. This contraction idea reaches far beyond calculus -- into
differential equations, economics (market equilibria are fixed points), and many
convergence proofs in \emph{numerical analysis}, the study of methods for
computing numerical answers.
\end{connectionbox}

For a typical $g$, convergence is \emph{linear}: the error is multiplied by
roughly the constant factor $\abs{g'(x^\star)}$ each step. For example, the
iteration $x_{n+1} = \cos x_n$ has fixed point $x^\star \approx 0.739$ (the solution
of $\cos x = x$, which we will solve with Newton's method in
Section~\ref{ch19:full-example}), and there
$\abs{g'(x^\star)} = \sin(0.739) \approx 0.67$, so each step keeps about two thirds
of the error. That is fine but slow. Newton's iteration function $g$ is different:
its derivative at the root is not merely smaller than $1$ but
\emph{equal to zero}. To check this, differentiate
$g(x) = x - f(x)/f'(x)$ using the quotient rule on the fraction:
\begin{align*}
  g'(x) &= 1 - \frac{f'(x)\,f'(x) - f(x)\,f''(x)}{\bigl(f'(x)\bigr)^2}\\
        &= 1 - \frac{\bigl(f'(x)\bigr)^2 - f(x)\,f''(x)}{\bigl(f'(x)\bigr)^2}
         = \frac{f(x)\,f''(x)}{\bigl(f'(x)\bigr)^2}.
\end{align*}
Evaluate at the root $r$, where $f(r) = 0$:
\[
  g'(r) = \frac{f(r)\,f''(r)}{\bigl(f'(r)\bigr)^2} = \frac{0 \cdot f''(r)}{\bigl(f'(r)\bigr)^2} = 0.
\]

\begin{whybox}
Why does $g'(r) = 0$ explain the speed? Apply the second-order error formula
(Theorem~\ref{ch14:thm-error}) to $g$ at the root: since $x_{n+1} = g(x_n)$ and
$r = g(r)$,
\[
  e_{n+1} = g(x_n) - g(r) = g'(r)\,e_n + \tfrac12 g''(\eta)\,e_n^2
\]
for some point $\eta$ between $x_n$ and $r$. For an ordinary $g$ the first term
dominates and the error is multiplied by about $g'(r)$ each step (linear). Newton's
$g$ has $g'(r) = 0$, so the first term disappears and
$e_{n+1} \approx \tfrac12 g''(r)\,e_n^2$: the error is proportional to the square
of the previous error, which is the quadratic convergence we derived directly.
Newton's choice of $g$ is built so that $g'(r) = 0$ automatically, and that is what
upgrades linear convergence to quadratic. Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-cobweb} shows the
effect: the curve $y = g(x)$ is flat where it meets the diagonal.
\end{whybox}

\section{Newton's method in real computation}
\label{ch19:computing}

Writing the method as a program shows how the failure modes of
Section~\ref{ch19:failures} are handled in practice. Here it is in Python. The
inputs are the function \texttt{f}, its derivative \texttt{fprime}, the starting
guess \texttt{x0}, a tolerance \texttt{tol} (how small $\abs{f(x)}$ must be before
we accept $x$; here $10^{-12}$, written \texttt{1e-12}), and a cap
\texttt{max\_iter} on the number of steps.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def newton(f, fprime, x0, tol=1e-12, max_iter=50):
    x = x0
    for n in range(max_iter):
        fx = f(x)
        if abs(fx) < tol:          # close enough: f(x) is essentially zero
            return x
        dfx = fprime(x)
        if dfx == 0:               # flat tangent: bail out, do not divide by zero
            raise ValueError("zero derivative; Newton stalled")
        x = x - fx / dfx           # the one line that is the whole method
    raise ValueError("did not converge in max_iter steps")
\end{lstlisting}

Three parts of this program carry the lessons of the chapter. The
\texttt{tol} test checks the answer by evaluating $f$ rather than trusting the
guess. The zero-derivative guard refuses to divide by a vanishing slope. And the
\texttt{max\_iter} cap stops the loop from running forever if the start was bad and
the guesses are wandering off or cycling. The mathematics is one line; the rest is
safety checks around it. Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-flowchart} draws the same program as
a flowchart.

\begin{applicationbox}
Newton's method is used throughout modern computation, well beyond solving
single equations.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Optimization and machine learning.} To minimize a function $F$, you
  look for where its derivative is zero -- a root of $F'$. Applying Newton's method
  to $F'$ gives the update $x_{n+1} = x_n - F'(x_n)/F''(x_n)$, ``Newton's method for
  optimization,'' which uses the curvature $F''$ to choose better steps than plain
  gradient descent (which only uses the slope). With many variables, $F''$ is
  replaced by the \emph{Hessian matrix}, the square table of all second derivatives
  with respect to pairs of variables. Computing it is expensive, so large models
  are often trained with \emph{quasi-Newton methods} such as BFGS and L-BFGS (named
  after Broyden, Fletcher, Goldfarb and Shanno; ``L'' for limited memory), which
  build up an approximation to it from the slopes seen so far.
\item \textbf{Systems of equations.} When there are several equations in several
  unknowns, $f$ becomes a list of functions and $f'$ becomes the \emph{Jacobian
  matrix}, the table of rates of change of each function with respect to each
  unknown. Each step then requires solving a system of linear equations. This
  many-variable Newton's method is how engineers solve the large systems of
  nonlinear equations behind circuit simulation, fluid dynamics, and structural
  analysis.
\item \textbf{Inside the hardware.} As mentioned, reciprocals, square roots, and
  divisions are computed by Newton iterations chosen so that each step needs only
  multiplication -- the operation chips do fastest.
\end{itemize}
It is chosen again and again because of the quadratic convergence we proved: when
each step doubles your digits, you reach full machine precision (the roughly $16$
significant digits a computer stores for an ordinary number) in a handful of steps.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{A fully worked, non-algebraic example}
\label{ch19:full-example}

To close, we solve by hand a \emph{transcendental} equation, one built from a
function such as cosine that is not a polynomial, so no algebraic rearranging
can solve it:
\[
  \cos x = x.
\]
This asks for the place where the cosine curve crosses the diagonal line $y = x$
(Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-cobweb-cos}) -- a number sometimes called the \emph{Dottie
number}, which has no formula in terms of familiar constants. Rewrite it
as a root problem:
\[
  f(x) = \cos x - x = 0, \qquad f'(x) = -\sin x - 1.
\]
A quick sketch (or noting $f(0) = 1 > 0$ and $f(1) = \cos 1 - 1 \approx -0.46 < 0$)
shows the root lies between $0$ and $1$. Start at $x_0 = 1$ (radians throughout).

\begin{labbox}
The iteration is $x_{n+1} = x_n - \dfrac{\cos x_n - x_n}{-\sin x_n - 1}$. Step
through it:
\begin{align*}
  x_0 &= 1 \\
  x_1 &= 1 - \frac{\cos 1 - 1}{-\sin 1 - 1}
       = 1 - \frac{0.540302 - 1}{-0.841471 - 1}
       = 1 - \frac{-0.459698}{-1.841471}
       = 0.750364 \\
  x_2 &= 0.750364 - \frac{\cos(0.750364) - 0.750364}{-\sin(0.750364) - 1}
       = 0.739113 \\
  x_3 &= 0.739085 \\
  x_4 &= 0.739085\ldots
\end{align*}
The root is $0.7390851332\ldots$; $x_3$ already has it to nine places, and $x_4$
agrees.
\end{labbox}

The digits settle quickly: $x_1$ is right to one place, $x_2$ to four, and $x_3$
to about nine -- the error is squared at each step. Compare the plain iteration $x_{n+1} = \cos x_n$ of
Figure~\ref{ch19:fig-cobweb-cos}, which after six steps is still wrong in the
second decimal place. From one crude guess and three steps of arithmetic, Newton's
method has given, to nine decimal places, a number that no formula expresses.

\begin{recoverybox}
If you take one thing from this chapter, take the master equation $e_{n+1} \approx
C\, e_n^2$ and the picture behind it: a tangent line, followed down to the axis,
again and again. The formula $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ is that picture
written in symbols, and the squared error is the price paid for the curve not
being a straight line -- a price that, near a simple root, is small.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Convergence, chaos, and algorithmic roots}
\label{ch19:what-unlocked}

\begin{connectionbox}
Newton's method is our first example of a \textbf{nonlinear dynamical system}:
a rule where the output of one step is fed back in as the input to the next,
which can produce complicated behavior from a simple formula.

When conditions are favorable, that feedback loop converges fast. For starting
guesses in the basin of attraction of a simple root (the set of starting guesses
that lead to that root), the error is roughly squared at every step,
$e_{n+1} \approx C e_n^2$, so equations like $\cos x = x$ are solved to machine
precision in four or five steps. Calculations in orbital mechanics, GPS
positioning and circuit simulation all rely on this.

Outside those conditions the same loop can fail. A nearly flat tangent throws the
next guess far away. Some functions, like $x^3 - 2x + 2$ from $x_0 = 0$, trap the
guesses in a cycle. Flattening tails, as in $\arctan x$ with $\abs{x_0} > 1.3917$,
make the guesses run off to infinity. And with complex numbers, the boundaries
between different basins of attraction are fractals, where a tiny change in the
starting guess can change which root the method finds. This extreme sensitivity
to the starting point is what mathematicians mean by \emph{chaos}.

For these reasons, software libraries rarely run plain Newton's method on its own.
They use \emph{hybrid} methods that keep an interval known to contain a root (an
interval where $f$ changes sign, as in bisection and the Intermediate Value
Theorem of Chapter~8), take a fast step when it lands inside that interval, and
fall back to a safe bisection step when it does not. \emph{Brent's method}, a
widely used example, works this way with fast steps that need no derivative. In
optimization with many variables, the quasi-Newton methods mentioned above (such
as BFGS) play the same role, approximating the curvature instead of computing all
the second derivatives exactly.

\textbf{Looking ahead.} For ten chapters we have taken curves apart into
instantaneous slopes. In Chapter~20, we turn calculus around and begin adding things
up: the definite integral rebuilds a total amount from a changing rate.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch19:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: resting points]
What does Newton's formula produce if your starting guess $x_0$ is already an exact
root ($f(x_0)=0$)? What happens geometrically if the tangent line at your guess is
horizontal ($f'(x_0)=0$)? State in one sentence what each situation tells you about
the iteration.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: overshooting the domain]
Consider $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, defined for $x \ge 0$, whose only root is $x = 0$.
(a) Compute $f'(x)$ and show that Newton's update simplifies to $x_{n+1} = -x_n$.
(b) If you start at $x_0 = 4$, where does $x_1$ land? Why does the method immediately
fail on the very next step? Sketch the tangent line at $x_0=4$ to see why it
overshoots past the boundary of the domain.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Use Newton's method to estimate $\sqrt5$ by finding the positive root of
$f(x) = x^2 - 5$. Start at $x_0 = 2$ and carry out three iterations by hand, writing
each $x_n$ to six decimal places. Compare $x_3$ to the true value $2.2360679\ldots$
and count how many digits are correct at each step.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Derive, from the general iteration, the formula for computing the cube root of a
positive number $a$ by applying Newton's method to $f(x) = x^3 - a$. Simplify your
update into the form $x_{n+1} = \tfrac13\bigl(2x_n + a/x_n^2\bigr)$, and use it to
estimate $\sqrt[3]{2}$ starting from $x_0 = 1$ (two iterations).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show that applying Newton's method to $f(x) = \dfrac1x - a$ (for a positive constant
$a$) yields the update $x_{n+1} = x_n(2 - a x_n)$, which computes $1/a$ using only
multiplication and subtraction -- no division. Test it with $a = 4$ and $x_0 = 0.2$,
performing two steps, and check that you approach $0.25$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
The equation $x^3 - 2x + 2 = 0$ produces the two-cycle $0 \to 1 \to 0 \to \cdots$ from
the start $x_0 = 0$, as shown in the chapter. (a) Verify the cycle by direct
computation. (b) The equation has a real root near $x = -1.77$. Starting instead from
$x_0 = -2$, perform three Newton iterations and confirm the iterates head toward that
root. What does this contrast teach about the choice of starting point?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Find the unique real root of $x^3 + x - 1 = 0$ to six decimal places using Newton's
method, starting from $x_0 = 1$. Show your work for at least three iterations and
verify your answer by plugging it back into the function.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual]
Explain in your own words, using a sketch, why Newton's method can fail when the
starting guess lands near a point where $f'(x) = 0$. Then describe geometrically what
happens to the next iterate, and why this is a genuine breakdown of the method rather
than a minor inaccuracy.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual]
The function $g(x) = x - f(x)/f'(x)$ is the fixed-point map behind Newton's method. A
general fixed-point iteration $x_{n+1} = g(x_n)$ converges \emph{linearly} when
$0 < \abs{g'(x^\star)} < 1$. Using the formula $g'(x) = f(x)f''(x)/(f'(x))^2$ derived
in the chapter, explain why Newton's method converges \emph{faster} than a typical
fixed-point iteration, and what specifically goes right at a simple root.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Multiple root]
For the double root of $f(x) = (x - 3)^2$, show that Newton's iteration simplifies to
$x_{n+1} = \tfrac12(x_n + 3)$. Starting from $x_0 = 5$, compute the errors
$\abs{x_n - 3}$ for $n = 0,1,2,3,4$ and confirm that they merely \emph{halve} each
step (linear convergence) rather than the digits doubling. Explain, referring to the
master equation $e_{n+1} = \tfrac{f''(\xi)}{2f'(x_n)} e_n^2$, why the quadratic
behavior is lost at a double root.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof]
Let $f$ have a continuous second derivative and a simple root $r$ (so $f'(r) \ne 0$).
Starting from the second-order Taylor expansion of $f$ about $x_n$ evaluated at $r$,
derive the master equation
\[
  e_{n+1} = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2 f'(x_n)}\, e_n^2, \qquad e_n = x_n - r,
\]
for some $\xi$ between $x_n$ and $r$. Then argue that there exist a constant $M$ and a
radius $\delta > 0$ such that if $\abs{e_0} < \delta$, the errors satisfy
$\abs{e_{n+1}} \le M \abs{e_n}^2$, and conclude that the convergence is quadratic.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Application]
To minimize a smooth function $F(x)$, one seeks a root of $F'(x) = 0$. Write down the
Newton iteration for this root problem (it will involve $F'$ and $F''$). Apply it to
$F(x) = x^4 - 3x^2 + x$ starting from $x_0 = 1$, performing two iterations, and
interpret geometrically what the resulting point represents.
\end{exercise}
