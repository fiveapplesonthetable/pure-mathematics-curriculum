\chapter{Derivatives of Exponentials, Logarithms, and Trig}

In Chapter~11 we collected the rules of differentiation: the power rule, the
product rule, the quotient rule, and the chain rule. With those four tools we
can differentiate any algebraic function, meaning one built from $x$ by
adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing, and taking powers and roots. But
growth, decay, and oscillation are described by \emph{transcendental
functions}: functions that cannot be built from $x$ by any finite sequence of
those operations.
Populations grow like $e^{kt}$. Radioactive nuclei decay like $e^{-\lambda t}$.
A pendulum swings like $\cos(\omega t)$. (Here $t$ is time, and $k$, $\lambda$,
$\omega$ are fixed positive constants set by the particular population,
substance, or pendulum.) Our sense of pitch, loudness, and brightness is
logarithmic: equal \emph{ratios} of frequency or intensity feel like equal
steps. If calculus could not differentiate $e^x$, $\ln x$, $\sin x$, and their
relatives, it could not describe any of these.

This chapter computes the derivatives of all of them, and each one follows
from the shape of its function rather than needing to be memorized. The
fact that $\ddx{e^x} = e^x$ is the reason $e$ was chosen in Chapter~3: it is
the base whose curve has slope equal to its height. The formula
$\ddx{\sin x} = \cos x$ comes from the direction in which a point moves as it
travels around the unit circle.

\section{The Exponential: a Function Equal to Its Own Slope}
\label{ch12:exp-intro}

Chapter~3 already estimated the slope of an exponential curve; now we can
compute it with the derivative of Chapter~10.
Take any exponential function $f(x) = b^x$ with a fixed base $b > 0$ and
work out its rate of change straight from the difference quotient:

\begin{align*}
f'(x)
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{b^{x+h} - b^x}{h} \\[2pt]
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{b^x b^h - b^x}{h}
   \qquad\text{(law of exponents: } b^{x+h}=b^x b^h\text{)}\\[2pt]
&= \lim_{h \to 0}\; b^x \cdot \frac{b^h - 1}{h} \\[2pt]
&= b^x \cdot \underbrace{\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{b^h - 1}{h}}_{\textstyle =\, m(b)}.
\end{align*}

Because exponents convert addition in the input into multiplication in the
output ($b^{x+h} = b^x b^h$), the factor $b^x$ comes out of the limit,
since it does not depend on the step size $h$. What remains is a number
depending only on the base $b$. Chapter~3 called it the \emph{starting slope}
and wrote it $M(b)$; in this chapter we write it $m(b)$:
\[
   \ddx{b^x} \;=\; m(b)\cdot b^x,
   \qquad\text{where}\qquad
   m(b) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{b^h - 1}{h}.
\]

\begin{focusbox}
The derivative of \emph{any} exponential function is a constant multiple of
\emph{itself}: differentiating $b^x$ gives back $b^x$, multiplied by the fixed
number $m(b)$. This is why exponentials appear in so many models of growth.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why should the slope of $b^x$ be proportional to its height? Picture compound
interest or a dividing colony of bacteria. The more money deposited, the more
interest earned per second; the more bacteria present, the more new cells
divide per hour. Whenever a quantity's rate of change is proportional to its
current size, the curve is exponential. The multiplier $m(b)$ plays the role
of the ``interest rate'' built into base $b$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{The starting slope and the definition of $e$}

As in Chapter~3, setting $x = 0$ in $\ddx{b^x} = m(b)\,b^x$ gives
\[
   f'(0) = m(b)\cdot b^0 = m(b).
\]
So $m(b)$ has an immediate geometric meaning: it is \emph{the slope of $b^x$ at
the point $(0,1)$ where it crosses the $y$-axis}. Every exponential curve passes
through $(0,1)$; what separates them is how steeply they launch from that point.
Knowing the starting slope at the $y$-axis determines the slope everywhere on
the curve: at height $y$, the slope is $m(b)\cdot y$.

In Chapter~3 we estimated this starting slope numerically. For base $b = 2$,
the curve leaves $(0,1)$ with slope $m(2) \approx 0.693$, shallower than
$45^\circ$. For base $b = 3$, it leaves with slope $m(3) \approx 1.099$,
steeper than $45^\circ$. And we defined the number $e \approx 2.71828\dots$
as the unique base that leaves with slope exactly $1$.
Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-exp-family} shows the three curves.

\begin{labbox}
Recall the numerical experiment from Chapter~3 with step $h = 0.001$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $b = 2$: \; $(2^{0.001}-1)/0.001 \approx 0.6934$.
\item $b = 2.5$: \; $(2.5^{0.001}-1)/0.001 \approx 0.9167$.
\item $b = 2.718$:\; $(2.718^{0.001}-1)/0.001 \approx 1.0004$.
\item $b = 3$: \; $(3^{0.001}-1)/0.001 \approx 1.0992$.
\end{itemize}
The starting slope $m(b)$ slides smoothly from below $1$ to above $1$.
Compare these numbers with $\ln 2 \approx 0.693147$ and $\ln 3 \approx 1.098612$: the
constant $m(b)$ appears to be the natural logarithm $\ln b$. To prove this,
we first handle the base $e$.
\end{labbox}

\subsection{Defining $e$ as the base that makes $m(b)=1$}

How do we know without a calculator that a slope-$1$ base exists? Take a base
$b>1$. The curve $b^x$ bends upward (it is concave up, as in Chapter~3), so its
tangent line at $(0,1)$ is steeper than the secant line through $(-1,b^{-1})$
and $(0,1)$, and less steep than the secant line through $(0,1)$ and $(1,b)$.
Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-secant-trap} shows this for $b=2$. The two secant slopes
are $1-\tfrac1b$ and $b-1$, so
\[
   1-\frac{1}{b} \;<\; m(b) \;<\; b-1.
\]
At $b=2$ the right-hand bound gives $m(2) < 2-1 = 1$. At $b=4$ the left-hand
bound only gives $m(4) > \tfrac34$, so we use the secant over the shorter
interval $[-\tfrac12,0]$, which is also less steep than the tangent:
\[
   m(4) \;>\; \frac{1-4^{-1/2}}{1/2} \;=\; \frac{1-\tfrac12}{\tfrac12} \;=\; 1.
\]
Since $m(b)$ increases continuously with $b$, the Intermediate Value Theorem
(Chapter~8) guarantees exactly one base strictly between $2$ and $4$ whose
starting slope is $1$. That base is what we call $e$.

\begin{definition}[The number $e$, via slope]
\label{ch12:def-e}
$e$ is the unique base $b>0$ for which
\[
   \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{b^h-1}{h} = 1,
   \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
   \frac{\dd}{\dd x}b^x\Big|_{x=0} = 1.
\]
\end{definition}

With this base the constant $m(e)=1$, so the leftover limit is $1$ and
we obtain the main result of this section:

\begin{theorem}[The exponential is its own derivative]
\label{ch12:thm-ex}
\[
   \ddx{e^x} = e^x.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
From the general formula $\ddx{b^x}=m(b)\,b^x$ with $b=e$, and
$m(e)=1$ by Definition~\ref{ch12:def-e}, we get $\ddx{e^x}=1\cdot e^x = e^x$.
\end{proof}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} What is $\ddx{e^4}$?
Resist the reflex to write $4e^3$ or $e^4$. The quantity $e^4 \approx 54.598$
is a constant number, with no $x$ in it at all. The derivative of any constant
is zero: $\ddx{e^4} = 0$. The rule $\ddx{e^u} = e^u \cdot u'$ (the theorem
combined with the chain rule) applies only when the exponent contains a variable.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{happeningbox}
Look at what ``$\ddx{e^x}=e^x$'' says. At every point $(x, e^x)$
on the curve, the steepness of the graph equals its height above the axis.
At height $1$ the slope is $1$; at height $7$ the slope is $7$; at height
$100$ the slope is $100$. The curve climbs exactly as fast as it
is tall. This property -- ``the rate of growth equals the current
size'' -- is what makes $e^x$ grow so fast, and it is why the same function
appears whenever growth is proportional to the current amount: interest,
populations, and chemical reactions. In fact $e^x$ is the only function that
equals its own derivative and equals $1$ at $0$; every other property of
$e^x$ can be derived from these two facts.

Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-exp-subtangent} turns ``slope equals height'' into a
picture. A tangent line with slope $s$ at height $s$ drops $s$ units in a run of
$1$, so it reaches the $x$-axis exactly one unit to the left of the point of
tangency, wherever that point is.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{historybox}
Exponentials and logarithms entered mathematics long before derivatives did, as practical tools for calculation. In the early seventeenth century, navigation and astronomy (such as Johannes Kepler's calculations of the elliptical orbits of the planets) required multiplying and dividing eight-digit numbers by hand. In 1614, John Napier published \emph{Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio}, followed by Henry Briggs's 1624 tables of base-10 logarithms in \emph{Arithmetica Logarithmica}. Because $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$, a multiplication could be replaced by looking up two logarithms in a table, adding them, and looking up the result; logarithms were said to have ``doubled the lives of astronomers.''

For more than a century, logarithms were treated as computational tables and as areas under the hyperbola (the two-branched curve $y = 1/x$), not as functions, and not as the inverses of an exponential. The change came in 1748 with Leonhard Euler's two-volume book \emph{Introductio in analysin infinitorum}, which made \emph{functions}, rather than geometric curves, the basic objects of study. Euler showed that the compound-interest limit $\lim_{n\to\infty}(1 + 1/n)^n$, the infinite sum $1 + 1 + \tfrac{1}{2!} + \tfrac{1}{3!} + \cdots$, and the base whose exponential has slope $1$ at the origin all give the same number $e \approx 2.718281828459$.

Euler also argued with his former teacher Johann Bernoulli about the logarithm of negative numbers. Bernoulli reasoned that since $(-x)^2 = x^2$, taking logarithms gives $2\ln(-x) = 2\ln(x)$, so $\ln(-x) = \ln(x)$ and negative numbers would have ordinary, real logarithms. In 1749 Euler showed the flaw by working with \emph{complex numbers}, numbers of the form $a + bi$ where $i$ is a new number with $i^2 = -1$; they can be pictured as points $(a,b)$ in a plane. There the logarithm has infinitely many values: Euler showed that $\ln(-1) = (2k+1)\pi i$ for every integer $k$, because adding a full turn of $2\pi$ to an angle returns to the same point. This work rests on Euler's formula $e^{ix} = \cos x + i\sin x$, which ties the exponential to rotation.

Some simple questions are still open. In 1873, Charles Hermite proved that $e$ is transcendental---it is not a root of any polynomial equation with integer coefficients (such as $x^2 - 2 = 0$)---and Ferdinand von Lindemann proved the same for $\pi$ in 1882. Yet no one knows whether $e + \pi$, $e\pi$, or $e^e$ is rational or irrational, let alone whether they are transcendental. An unproved general statement called Schanuel's conjecture would settle many such questions at once.
\end{historybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Why does the slope-$1$ base equal the compound-interest limit? Suppose $b$ is
the base with $m(b)=1$, i.e.
$(b^h-1)/h \to 1$ as $h\to 0$. For small $h$ this says $b^h - 1 \approx h$,
so $b^h \approx 1+h$, hence $b \approx (1+h)^{1/h}$. Letting $h = 1/n$ and
$n\to\infty$ turns the right side into $\lim_{n\to\infty}(1+\tfrac1n)^n$ --
the compound-interest definition of $e$.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{The Natural Logarithm by Inverse Reasoning}
\label{ch12:ln}

With the derivative of $e^x$ known, the derivative of its inverse follows quickly. Recall that
$\ln x$ (the natural logarithm) is \emph{defined} as the inverse of $e^x$:
\[
   y = \ln x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e^y = x,\qquad (x>0).
\]
So $\ln$ undoes $e^{(\cdot)}$ and vice versa: $e^{\ln x} = x$ and
$\ln(e^x)=x$. We differentiate it two ways -- once by differentiating the
equation $e^y = x$, and once straight from the limit -- and the two methods
check each other.

\subsection{Derivative of $\ln x$ by implicit differentiation}

\begin{intuitionbox}
A function and its inverse are reflections of each other across the line
$y=x$. Reflection swaps the roles of $x$ and $y$, which means it swaps
horizontal ``run'' with vertical ``rise.'' If the original curve has slope
$m = \Delta y / \Delta x$ at some point, its reflection has slope equal to the
\emph{reciprocal} $1/m = \Delta x / \Delta y$ at the mirror-image point: a
steep curve becomes a gentle one. So the slope of the inverse function is the
reciprocal of the original slope, read at the matching point. Writing
$f^{-1}$ for the inverse function of $f$,
$\big(f^{-1}\big)'(x) = 1 / f'\!\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)$.
\end{intuitionbox}

Because $e^y$ is differentiable and strictly increasing with nonzero slope $e^y > 0$
everywhere, its reflection $y = \ln x$ across $y=x$ has no vertical tangents and
is differentiable for every $x>0$. Start from $e^y = x$ and differentiate both
sides with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as an unknown function of $x$ and using
the chain rule on the left. (Differentiating both sides of an equation that
ties $y$ to $x$, without first solving for $y$, is called \emph{implicit
differentiation}; Chapter~13 develops it in general.)
\[
   \ddx{e^y} = \ddx{x}
   \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
   e^y \cdot \dv{y}{x} = 1.
\]
Solve for $\dv{y}{x}$ and then replace $e^y$ by $x$ (that is what $e^y$
equals):
\[
   \dv{y}{x} = \frac{1}{e^y} = \frac{1}{x}.
\]
Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-ln-reflection} shows the reciprocal slopes at a pair of
mirror-image points.

\begin{theorem}[Derivative of the natural log]
\label{ch12:thm-ln}
For $x>0$,
\[
   \ddx{\ln x} = \frac{1}{x}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
The same argument can be phrased as differentiating the
identity $e^{\ln x}=x$. Apply the chain rule to the
left side: $\ddx{e^{\ln x}} = e^{\ln x}\cdot(\ln x)' = x\cdot(\ln x)'$. The
right side is $1$. So $x\cdot(\ln x)' = 1$, giving $(\ln x)' = 1/x$.
Whenever you know a function's derivative and want
its inverse's derivative, differentiate the ``they cancel'' identity and
solve. The reciprocal-of-the-slope picture and this one-line computation are
the same idea: $\big(f^{-1}\big)'(x) = 1/f'\!\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)$.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{The same result straight from the limit}

The same $1/x$ also comes out of the difference quotient directly,
without using the inverse, and the derivation uses the
compound-interest limit for $e$ along the way. We compute:
\begin{align*}
\ddx{\ln x}
&= \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\ln(x+h)-\ln x}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\ln\!\left(\frac{x+h}{x}\right)
   && \text{(}\ln A - \ln B = \ln(A/B)\text{)} \\
&= \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{h}{x}\right).
\end{align*}
Let $u = h/x$, so $h = ux$ and $u\to 0$ as $h\to 0$. Then $1/h = 1/(ux)$:
\begin{align*}
\ddx{\ln x}
&= \lim_{u\to 0}\frac{1}{ux}\,\ln(1+u) \\
&= \frac{1}{x}\,\lim_{u\to 0}\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u} \\
&= \frac{1}{x}\,\lim_{u\to 0}\ln\!\Big[(1+u)^{1/u}\Big]
   && \text{(}c\ln A = \ln A^{c}\text{)}.
\end{align*}
Inside the bracket sits $(1+u)^{1/u}$, which tends to $e$ as $u\to 0$ (the
compound-interest limit from Chapter~3 again, setting $u=1/n$). Since $\ln$ is
continuous we may pass the limit inside: the bracket becomes $\ln e = 1$. Therefore
\[
   \ddx{\ln x} = \frac{1}{x}\cdot \ln e = \frac{1}{x}.
\]
Both methods give $1/x$. The first used the slope definition of $e$, the
second the compound-interest limit; the connection box above showed that these
describe the same number.

\begin{aestheticbox}
The logarithm fills a gap in the power rule, a fact we will rely on throughout the
integration chapters. The power rule gives $\ddx{x^n} = n x^{n-1}$. To get
$x^{-1}=1/x$ as a derivative we would need $n-1=-1$, that is $n=0$; but
$x^0=1$ is a constant and its derivative is $0$, not $x^{-1}$. So no power of
$x$ has derivative $1/x$. The natural logarithm fills this role
(and for $x<0$, the chain rule gives $\ddx{\ln(-x)} = \tfrac{1}{-x}\cdot(-1) = \tfrac{1}{x}$
as well). A function whose derivative is a given function $f$ is called an
\emph{antiderivative} of $f$, written $\int f(x)\,\dd x$; the ``$+C$'' records
that adding any constant does not change the derivative. So
$\int \frac{1}{x}\,\dd x = \ln\abs{x}+C$: the logarithm is the antiderivative
that the power rule does not supply.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{General Bases: $a^x$ and $\log_a x$}
\label{ch12:general-base}

We built everything on base $e$ because it is the base whose derivative
formulas carry no extra constant. Other bases inherit their derivatives from
$e$ through one rewrite.

\subsection{Derivative of $a^x$}

Write \emph{any} exponential in terms of the natural one.
Since $a = e^{\ln a}$, we have $a^x = \big(e^{\ln a}\big)^x = e^{(\ln a)x}$.
Now apply the chain rule with inner function $(\ln a)x$:
\begin{align*}
   \ddx{a^x}
   &= \ddx{e^{(\ln a)x}} \\
   &= e^{(\ln a)x}\cdot \ddx{(\ln a)x}
   && \text{(chain rule)} \\
   &= a^x \cdot \ln a.
\end{align*}
The same rewrite settles the promise we left open in Chapter~11: the power rule
for \emph{any real exponent} $r$ (including irrational ones like $x^{\pi}$ or
$x^{\sqrt{2}}$). For $x > 0$, write $x = e^{\ln x}$ so that $x^r = e^{r\ln x}$,
and apply the chain rule:
\begin{align*}
   \ddx{x^r}
   &= \ddx{e^{r\ln x}} \\
   &= e^{r\ln x}\cdot \frac{r}{x}
   && \text{(chain rule)} \\
   &= x^r \cdot \frac{r}{x}
    = r\,x^{r-1}.
\end{align*}

\begin{focusbox}
\[
   \ddx{a^x} = a^x \ln a.
\]
Compare with our very first computation, $\ddx{a^x}=m(a)\,a^x$. We have now
\emph{identified} the constant: $m(a) = \ln a$. The slope of $a^x$ at
the $y$-axis is the natural log of its base. So $m(2)=\ln 2\approx 0.6931$
and $m(3)=\ln 3\approx 1.0986$, close to the finite-difference estimates
from the lab box ($0.6934$ and $1.0992$). The small gap is
the overshoot from using $h=0.001$ instead of the true limit; shrink $h$ and
the estimates move onto $\ln 2$ and $\ln 3$.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{labbox}
Test the formula $m(a)=\ln a$ on a fresh base: take $a=10$, where $\ln 10 \approx 2.302585$.
At step $h=0.001$, $(10^{0.001}-1)/0.001 \approx 2.3052$; shrinking the step tenfold to
$h=0.0001$ gives $(10^{0.0001}-1)/0.0001 \approx 2.3029$, cutting the overshoot tenfold
and closing in on $\ln 10$.
\end{labbox}

\subsection{Derivative of $\log_a x$}

The general logarithm reduces to the natural one by the change-of-base
formula $\log_a x = \dfrac{\ln x}{\ln a}$. Since $\ln a$ is a constant,
\[
   \ddx{\log_a x}
   = \frac{1}{\ln a}\,\ddx{\ln x}
   = \frac{1}{(\ln a)\,x}.
\]
Once more $e$ is the standard base: with $a=e$ the factor $\ln a=1$ drops out
and we recover $1/x$.

\section{Logarithmic Differentiation}
\label{ch12:logdiff}

The logarithm is also a tool for differentiating other functions. It
converts products into sums and exponents into multipliers,
\[
   \ln(fg) = \ln f + \ln g,
   \qquad
   \ln\!\big(f^{\,g}\big) = g\,\ln f.
\]
Differentiation passes directly through sums, whereas products and variable
exponents need more complicated rules. So
the plan is: take $\ln$ of both sides \emph{first} to break the expression apart,
differentiate the simpler pieces, then multiply back.

\begin{whybox}
The method rests on the chain rule applied to $\ln y$. If $y=y(x)$, then
\[
   \ddx{\ln y} = \frac{1}{y}\,\dv{y}{x} = \frac{y'}{y}.
\]
The quantity $y'/y$ is the \emph{logarithmic derivative}, also known as the
\emph{relative} or \emph{fractional} rate of change -- growth measured as a
percentage of current size. For example, if $y'/y = 0.05$, then $y$ is
growing at $5\%$ of its current value per unit of $x$. So whenever you
compute $\ddx{\ln y}$ you are computing $y'/y$; multiply by $y$ at the end to
recover $y'$ itself. Logarithmic differentiation uses this identity in reverse:
build $y'/y$ out of easy log-pieces, then scale up by $y$.
\end{whybox}

\begin{example}[A product of three factors]
\label{ch12:ex-logdiff-product}
Differentiate
\[
   y = \frac{(x^2+1)^3\,\sqrt{x-4}}{(x+2)^5}.
\]
Doing this with the quotient and product rules directly would mean many
terms to track. Instead take logs (for $x>4$, where all factors are positive):
\[
   \ln y = 3\ln(x^2+1) + \tfrac12\ln(x-4) - 5\ln(x+2).
\]
Differentiate term by term (each is an easy chain-rule piece):
\[
   \frac{y'}{y}
   = 3\cdot\frac{2x}{x^2+1} + \frac12\cdot\frac{1}{x-4} - 5\cdot\frac{1}{x+2}
   = \frac{6x}{x^2+1} + \frac{1}{2(x-4)} - \frac{5}{x+2}.
\]
Finally multiply back by $y$:
\[
   y' = \frac{(x^2+1)^3\sqrt{x-4}}{(x+2)^5}
        \left[\frac{6x}{x^2+1} + \frac{1}{2(x-4)} - \frac{5}{x+2}\right].
\]
The original function appears as a factor out front, multiplied by a
sum of simple terms inside the brackets.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A variable base \emph{and} a variable exponent]
\label{ch12:ex-tower}
Differentiate $y = x^x$ for $x>0$. Neither the power rule
($\ddx{x^n}=nx^{n-1}$, exponent constant) nor the exponential rule
($\ddx{a^x}=a^x\ln a$, base constant) applies -- here \emph{both} the base
and the exponent vary. Take $\ln$ of both sides:
\[
   \ln y = x\ln x.
\]
Differentiate the right side with the product rule:
\[
   \frac{y'}{y} = 1\cdot \ln x + x\cdot\frac1x = \ln x + 1.
\]
Multiply back: $y' = x^x(\ln x + 1)$. As a sanity check, $x^x$ has its
minimum where $y'=0$, i.e. $\ln x = -1$, i.e. $x = 1/e \approx 0.368$ --
which is indeed where the graph of $x^x$ bottoms out
(Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-xtox}).
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
Do not write $\ddx{x^x} = x\cdot x^{x-1}$ (treating the exponent as
constant), and do not write $\ddx{x^x} = x^x\ln x$ (treating the base as
constant). \emph{Both} are wrong because \emph{both} the base and the
exponent move. The correct derivative $x^x(\ln x+1)$ contains a piece from
each: the $1$ comes from varying the base, the $\ln x$ from varying the
exponent. When in doubt with anything of the form
$(\text{stuff})^{(\text{stuff})}$, take logs.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} How do you differentiate $x^5$, $5^x$, $x^x$, and $5^5$?
Sort them by their anatomy before putting pencil to paper:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $x^5$: Variable base, constant power $\implies$ Power Rule: $5x^4$.
  \item $5^x$: Constant base, variable power $\implies$ Exponential Rule: $5^x\ln 5$.
  \item $x^x$: Variable base, variable power $\implies$ Logarithmic Differentiation: $x^x(\ln x + 1)$.
  \item $5^5$: Constant base, constant power $\implies$ Constant Rule: $0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Sine and Cosine: the Two Limits That Decide Everything}
\label{ch12:trig}

Sine and cosine are the basic \emph{periodic functions}: their
values repeat every $2\pi$. Their derivatives rest on two limits,
with the angle $\theta$ measured in \emph{radians} (the length of arc it cuts
on a circle of radius $1$, so a full turn is $2\pi$ radians). The two limits are:
\[
   \lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1,
   \qquad
   \lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\cos\theta - 1}{\theta} = 0.
\]
The first limit was proved in Chapter~7 with the squeeze
theorem, by trapping $\sin\theta/\theta$ between two expressions that both tend
to $1$. The second limit follows from the first by multiplying top and bottom
by the conjugate $\cos\theta + 1$:
\begin{align*}
   \frac{\cos\theta - 1}{\theta}
   &= \frac{(\cos\theta - 1)(\cos\theta + 1)}{\theta(\cos\theta + 1)} \\
   &= \frac{\cos^2\theta - 1}{\theta(\cos\theta + 1)} \\
   &= \frac{-\sin^2\theta}{\theta(\cos\theta + 1)}
   && \text{(since } \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\text{)} \\
   &= -\left(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\right)\cdot \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta + 1}.
\end{align*}
As $\theta \to 0$, the first factor $\tfrac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \to 1$, while the
second factor heads to $\tfrac{0}{1+1} = 0$. Their product is $1 \cdot 0 = 0$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
On the unit circle, the \emph{angle} $\theta$ (in radians) is the arc length
swept out along the circumference, and $\sin\theta$ is the vertical height of
the endpoint. For a tiny angle, the curved circular arc and the straight vertical
rise are nearly indistinguishable, so $\sin\theta \approx \theta$---their ratio
heads to $1$. Meanwhile, $\cos\theta$ is the horizontal coordinate. Near $\theta=0$,
it is barely less than the full radius $1$: it falls short by about
$\theta^2/2$ ($\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2$), and for small $\theta$ the
square $\theta^2$ is tiny even compared with $\theta$ itself (for
$\theta = 0.01$, $\theta^2/2 = 0.00005$). So the height changes in step with
the angle, while the width hardly changes at all.
Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-small-angle} shows both facts on a graph.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Differentiating sine}

Now we run the difference quotient for $\sin x$ and use the angle-addition
formula $\sin(x+h) = \sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h$.

\begin{align*}
\ddx{\sin x}
&= \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sin(x+h)-\sin x}{h}\\[2pt]
&= \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h - \sin x}{h}\\[2pt]
&= \lim_{h\to 0}\left[\sin x\cdot\frac{\cos h - 1}{h}
                    + \cos x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h}\right]\\[2pt]
&= \sin x\cdot\underbrace{\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h-1}{h}}_{=\,0}
 + \cos x\cdot\underbrace{\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}}_{=\,1}\\[2pt]
&= \sin x\cdot 0 + \cos x\cdot 1 \;=\; \cos x.
\end{align*}

\begin{theorem}[Derivatives of sine and cosine]
\label{ch12:thm-sincos}
\[
   \ddx{\sin x} = \cos x,
   \qquad
   \ddx{\cos x} = -\sin x.
\]
\end{theorem}

The cosine derivative falls out the same way, with
$\cos(x+h)=\cos x\cos h - \sin x\sin h$. Splitting and taking limits,
\begin{align*}
\ddx{\cos x}
&= \cos x\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h-1}{h}
 - \sin x\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h} \\
&= \cos x\cdot 0 - \sin x\cdot 1 \;=\; -\sin x.
\end{align*}

\begin{happeningbox}
Each of the two limits has its own job. The limit
$\sin h/h\to 1$ is the reason $\ddx{\sin x}$ is the \emph{whole} cosine and not
some multiple of it. That ``$1$'' is the trigonometric analogue of $m(e)=1$ for
the exponential, and it comes from measuring angles in radians. The other
limit, $(\cos h-1)/h\to 0$, says cosine is \emph{flat} at its peak, which is
why no extra $\sin x$ term appears in the answer. If you measured angles in
degrees instead, $\sin h/h$ would tend to $\pi/180$, not $1$, and every trig
derivative would carry a factor of $\pi/180$. Radians are the natural unit for
the same reason $e$ is the natural base: they make the leftover constant
equal to $1$. Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-sin-cos} compares the graph of $\sin x$
with the graph of its derivative.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{trapbox}
If your calculator is in degree mode,
$\ddx{\sin x}\neq\cos x$ -- you would need $\frac{\pi}{180}\cos x$. All of
calculus assumes radians. Many wrong answers in physics
and engineering trace back to a calculator left in degree mode.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} What is $\ddx{\sin(\pi/3)}$?
Resist writing $\cos(\pi/3)$. The value $\sin(\pi/3) = \sqrt{3}/2$ is a constant number.
Its derivative is zero: $\ddx{\sin(\pi/3)} = 0$. The derivative measures how an output
responds to a changing input; if the input is fixed, the rate of change is zero.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Differentiating sine and cosine sends you around a little cycle of length
four:
\[
   \sin x \xrightarrow{\;d\;} \cos x \xrightarrow{\;d\;} -\sin x
   \xrightarrow{\;d\;} -\cos x \xrightarrow{\;d\;} \sin x.
\]
Four derivatives bring you back to the start
(Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-deriv-cycle}). This is the source of oscillation:
any function satisfying $y'' = -y$ (its second derivative is the negative of
itself) must be built from sines and cosines, and equations of this form
describe a mass bouncing on a spring, the small swings of a pendulum, and
many waves. Compare the
exponential, whose derivative cycle has length \emph{one} ($e^x\to e^x$):
exponentials grow, trig functions circle. Later, Euler's formula
$e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ (where $i$ is the imaginary unit, a
number with $i^2=-1$) will show that these are two views of one function: the
exponential with an imaginary exponent goes around a circle.
\end{aestheticbox}

\subsection{The rest of the trig functions}

With $\sin$ and $\cos$ in hand, the other four come from the quotient rule
and the Pythagorean identity. Take the tangent:
\begin{align*}
   \ddx{\tan x}
   &= \ddx{\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}} \\
   &= \frac{(\cos x)(\cos x) - (\sin x)(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x}
   && \text{(quotient rule)} \\
   &= \frac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} \\
   &= \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x
   && \text{(since } \sin^2 x+\cos^2 x=1\text{)}.
\end{align*}
Since $\sec^2 x \ge 1$, the tangent function rises everywhere on
$(-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2)$, with slope $1$ at $x=0$ and slopes growing without
bound near $\pm\tfrac\pi2$ (Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-tan}).
For the secant, apply the reciprocal rule $\big(1/g\big)' = -g'/g^2$ to $\sec x = 1/\cos x$:
\begin{align*}
   \ddx{\sec x}
   &= \ddx{\frac{1}{\cos x}}
    = -\frac{-\sin x}{\cos^2 x} \\
   &= \frac{1}{\cos x}\cdot\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}
    = \sec x\tan x.
\end{align*}
Cotangent ($\cos x/\sin x$) and cosecant ($1/\sin x$) follow by the same steps.

\centering

\begin{focusbox}
\[
\begin{array}{ll}

\ddx{\sec x} = \sec x\tan x, &\quad \ddx{\csc x} = -\csc x\cot x.
\end{array}
\]
Every ``co-'' function ($\cos,\cot,\csc$) carries a minus
sign in its derivative, which is a useful memory aid.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}[Using the chain rule with trig]
\label{ch12:ex-chain-trig}
Differentiate $g(x) = \sin(3x^2+1)$ and $h(x)=\sec(5x)$.
\begin{align*}
   g'(x) &= \cos(3x^2+1)\cdot \ddx{3x^2+1} = 6x\cos(3x^2+1),\\
   h'(x) &= \sec(5x)\tan(5x)\cdot 5 = 5\sec(5x)\tan(5x).
\end{align*}
The trig derivative supplies the ``outer'' factor; the chain rule supplies
the derivative of the inside.
\end{example}

\section{Derivatives of the Inverse Trig Functions}
\label{ch12:inverse-trig}

The inverse trig functions $\arcsin$, $\arccos$, $\arctan$ answer ``which
angle has this sine/cosine/tangent?'' We differentiate them the same way we
did $\ln x$: differentiate the equation $\sin y = x$ (or $\tan y = x$) on both
sides, then use a Pythagorean identity to write the answer in terms of $x$.
The resulting derivatives are \emph{algebraic}: no trig functions are left in
the answer.

\subsection{Arcsine}

Let $y = \arcsin x$, so $\sin y = x$ with $y\in[-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2]$.
Differentiate $\sin y = x$ implicitly:
\[
   \cos y\cdot\dv{y}{x} = 1
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
   \dv{y}{x} = \frac{1}{\cos y}.
\]
Now convert $\cos y$ into something in terms of $x$. From
$\sin^2 y + \cos^2 y = 1$ and $\sin y = x$, we get
$\cos y = \sqrt{1-x^2}$. (We take the $+$ root because cosine is
nonnegative on $[-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2]$, the restricted range of
$\arcsin$.) Therefore
\[
   \ddx{\arcsin x} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}},\qquad -1<x<1.
\]
At $x=\pm1$ the denominator is $0$: the graph of $\arcsin$ becomes vertical at
its two ends, and the derivative grows without bound there
(Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-arcsin}).


\begin{whybox}
Where did the square root come from? The implicit step gives
$1/\cos y$, but the answer should be a function of $x$, not of the hidden
angle $y$. The right triangle with opposite side $x$ and hypotenuse $1$ has
$\sin y = x$, so by Pythagoras its adjacent side is $\sqrt{1-x^2}$, and that
adjacent-over-hypotenuse is $\cos y$ (Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-invtrig-triangles},
left). Drawing the triangle is the direct way
to turn ``$\cos(\arcsin x)$'' into ``$\sqrt{1-x^2}$.'' Every inverse-trig
derivative comes out algebraic for this reason: a trig function of an
inverse-trig function is a side ratio in a right triangle whose sides are
algebraic in $x$.
\end{whybox}

\subsection{Arctangent}

Let $y=\arctan x$, so $\tan y = x$. Differentiate:
\[
   \sec^2 y\cdot\dv{y}{x} = 1
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
   \dv{y}{x} = \frac{1}{\sec^2 y}.
\]
Use the identity $\sec^2 y = 1 + \tan^2 y = 1 + x^2$ (or read it off the right
triangle in Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-invtrig-triangles}). So
\[
   \ddx{\arctan x} = \frac{1}{1+x^2},\qquad x\in\R.
\]
This derivative is defined for every real $x$, and Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-arctan}
shows it next to $\arctan x$. In the integration chapters it will give the
area under the curve $y = 1/(1+x^2)$; the total area under the whole curve
turns out to be exactly $\pi$.

\begin{focusbox}
The inverse-trig derivative table (each on its natural domain). Here
$\operatorname{arccot}$, $\operatorname{arcsec}$, and $\operatorname{arccsc}$ are
the inverses of $\cot$, $\sec$, and $\csc$, each restricted to a range where it
takes every value once, just as $\arcsin$ inverts $\sin$ on
$[-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2]$.
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\ddx{\arcsin x} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}, &\quad
\ddx{\arccos x} = -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}, \\[10pt]
\ddx{\arctan x} = \dfrac{1}{1+x^2}, &\quad
\ddx{\operatorname{arccot} x} = -\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}, \\[10pt]
\ddx{\operatorname{arcsec} x} = \dfrac{1}{\abs{x}\sqrt{x^2-1}}, &\quad
\ddx{\operatorname{arccsc} x} = -\dfrac{1}{\abs{x}\sqrt{x^2-1}}.
\end{array}
\]
Again the ``co-'' partners differ only by a sign, since
$\arccos x = \tfrac\pi2 - \arcsin x$: complementary angles sum to a constant,
so their derivatives are negatives. The absolute value in the derivative of
$\operatorname{arcsec} x$ (whose standard range is $[0,\pi]$ with $\tfrac\pi2$
removed) is needed because for $x<-1$, both $\sec y = x$ and
$\tan y$ are negative, so their product $\sec y\tan y = \abs{x}\sqrt{x^2-1}$ stays
positive across both branches.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
These derivatives matter most read \emph{backwards}, as integration
formulas. Differentiating the inverse trig functions is how we discover that
\[
   \int \frac{\dd x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} = \arcsin x + C,
   \qquad
   \int \frac{\dd x}{1+x^2} = \arctan x + C.
\]
Two algebraic \emph{integrands} (the functions being integrated) turn out to
have \emph{inverse trigonometric} antiderivatives. This is the reason a method
called trigonometric substitution works for integrals containing
$\sqrt{1-x^2}$ or $1+x^2$; we reach it in the integration part.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{example}[Exponential times trig]
\label{ch12:ex-damped}
Differentiate $f(x) = e^{-2x}\cos(3x)$, the shape of a \emph{damped
oscillation}: a back-and-forth motion whose swings shrink over time, like a
plucked string dying away. The factor $\cos(3x)$ supplies the swinging and the
factor $e^{-2x}$ shrinks it (Figure~\ref{ch12:fig-damped} shows this shape,
with slower shrinking so the swings are visible).
Use the product rule, with each factor needing the chain rule:
\begin{align*}
   f'(x) &= \underbrace{(-2)e^{-2x}}_{\ddx{e^{-2x}}}\cos(3x)
         + e^{-2x}\underbrace{\big(-3\sin(3x)\big)}_{\ddx{\cos(3x)}} \\
         &= -e^{-2x}\big(2\cos 3x + 3\sin 3x\big).
\end{align*}
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Log of a composite]
\label{ch12:ex-log-comp}
Differentiate $f(x) = \ln\!\big(\sqrt{x^2+1}\big)$. Simplify with log laws
\emph{before} differentiating: $\ln\sqrt{x^2+1} = \tfrac12\ln(x^2+1)$. Then
\[
   f'(x) = \frac12\cdot\frac{2x}{x^2+1} = \frac{x}{x^2+1}.
\]
Always check whether a log law can break an expression apart before differentiating;
a quick algebraic rewrite often saves a page of quotient-rule work.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Putting the base change to work]
\label{ch12:ex-base-change}
Differentiate $f(x) = 5^{\sin x}$. Rewrite as $e^{(\ln 5)\sin x}$, or
apply $\ddx{a^u} = a^u\ln a\cdot u'$ directly with $a=5$, $u=\sin x$:
\[
   f'(x) = 5^{\sin x}\,(\ln 5)\,\cos x.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Inverse trig inside a chain]
\label{ch12:ex-arctan-chain}
Differentiate $f(x) = \arctan(e^x)$. Outer derivative $1/(1+u^2)$ with
$u=e^x$, times $u' = e^x$:
\[
   f'(x) = \frac{1}{1+(e^x)^2}\cdot e^x = \frac{e^x}{1+e^{2x}}.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Logarithmic differentiation with a root tower]
\label{ch12:ex-logdiff-2}
Differentiate $y = \dfrac{x^{3}\,e^{2x}}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$ for $x>0$. Take logs:
\[
   \ln y = 3\ln x + 2x - \tfrac12\ln(1+x^2).
\]
Differentiate:
\[
   \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{3}{x} + 2 - \frac{x}{1+x^2}.
\]
Multiply back:
\[
   y' = \frac{x^{3}e^{2x}}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}
        \left(\frac{3}{x} + 2 - \frac{x}{1+x^2}\right).
\]
\end{example}

\begin{walkbox}
Something to think about on a walk. An \emph{operator} is a rule that turns
one function into another, as $\ddx{}$ turns $f$ into $f'$. An
\emph{eigenfunction} of an operator is a function that the operator only
multiplies by a constant, leaving its shape unchanged. The exponential $e^x$
is an eigenfunction of $\ddx{}$ (it returns itself), while the pair $\sin x$
and $\cos x$ rotate among themselves in a cycle of four derivatives. Is there
a function whose derivative is the function shifted, or scaled by $x$, in some
equally clean way? What kind of equation would ``$y' = $ (something simple
involving $y$)'' have to be for its solution to be \emph{elementary}, meaning
built from powers, exponentials, logarithms, and trig functions by finitely
many operations? These are questions about \emph{differential
equations}, equations that tie an unknown function to its own derivatives,
like $y'=y$. They are the subject the book builds toward. Every function in this
chapter is the answer to one: $y'=y$ gives $e^x$, $y''=-y$ gives $\sin$ and
$\cos$, and $y'=1/x$ gives $\ln$.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
These derivatives appear throughout science and engineering.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Radioactive decay / drug clearance.} If $N(t)=N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$
($N_0$ is the starting amount and $\lambda>0$ a constant set by the substance),
then $N'(t) = -\lambda N(t)$: the decay rate is proportional to how much is
left. This equation is the law of decay.
\item \textbf{Exponential growth.} $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(e^{kt})=ke^{kt}$ is why unchecked
populations, the early spread of an epidemic, and compound interest all follow
the same rapidly rising curve.
\item \textbf{Oscillation.} A mass on a spring, at distance $x(t)$ from its
resting position at time $t$, obeys $x''=-\omega^2 x$, where $\omega$ is a
constant set by the stiffness of the spring and the mass. The solutions are
$x(t)=A\cos(\omega t)+B\sin(\omega t)$ for constants $A$ and $B$, because of the
derivative cycle of sine and cosine.
\item \textbf{Machine learning.} The logistic ``sigmoid''
$\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})$ is an S-shaped curve that maps every real number to a
value between $0$ and $1$, often read as a probability. Its derivative is
$\sigma'(x)=\sigma(x)\big(1-\sigma(x)\big)$, as you can verify with the chain
rule (Exercise~\ref{ch12:exr-sigmoid}). Neural networks are computer models
that are trained by repeatedly computing derivatives and adjusting their
parameters (the many numbers inside the model that set its output); with this formula, the derivative of $\sigma$ costs one
multiplication once $\sigma(x)$ itself is known.
\end{itemize}
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Summary}
\label{ch12:summary}

Every derivative in this chapter comes from a geometric picture together with a limit:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\ddx{b^x} = (\ln b)\,b^x$, and $\ln b = \lim_{h\to0}(b^h-1)/h$ is the
slope of $b^x$ at the $y$-axis. The base $e$ is defined as the one making
that slope $1$, which is exactly why $\ddx{e^x}=e^x$.
\item $\ddx{\ln x}=1/x$ and $\ddx{\log_a x} = 1/(x\ln a)$, by inverse
reasoning or straight from the limit; $\ln x$ fills the gap the power rule
leaves at exponent $-1$.
\item Logarithmic differentiation turns products, quotients, and
variable-exponent towers into sums via $y'/y = \ddx{\ln y}$.
\item $\ddx{\sin x}=\cos x$ and $\ddx{\cos x}=-\sin x$, driven by
$\sin h/h\to1$ and $(\cos h-1)/h\to 0$ from the unit circle -- and radians
are what make those limits clean.
\item Inverse trig derivatives are \emph{algebraic}:
$(\arcsin x)'=1/\sqrt{1-x^2}$, $(\arctan x)'=1/(1+x^2)$, found by implicit
differentiation plus a Pythagorean triangle.
\end{itemize}
The recurring theme: $e$ and radians are the natural choices
because they remove extra constant factors from differentiation formulas.

\subsection{What These Derivatives Make Possible}
\label{ch12:what-unlocked}

\begin{connectionbox}
With exponentials, logarithms, and trigonometric functions added, calculus can now handle:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Natural growth and decay}: Any process whose rate is proportional to its size ($\dd y/\dd t = ky$) is solved by the explicit formula $y(t) = y_0 e^{kt}$, where $y_0$ is the starting amount. This models radioactive decay, compound interest, and population growth.
  \item \emph{Oscillation}: The derivative cycle $(\sin x)' = \cos x$ and $(\cos x)' = -\sin x$ explains why a mass on a spring, whose acceleration is proportional to its distance from rest and points back toward rest, satisfies $\dd^2 y/\dd t^2 = -\omega^2 y$ and moves like a sine wave.
  \item \emph{New antiderivatives}: Because $(\arctan x)' = 1/(1+x^2)$, we can find the antiderivative of $1/(1+x^2)$, which no algebraic function provides.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The bridge to Chapter~13: Implicit relations and related rates.}
We repeatedly used one idea in this chapter: when an explicit formula for $y$
was not available---as with $\ln x$, $\arcsin x$, and $\arctan x$---we rewrote the
relationship as an equation ($e^y = x$, $\sin y = x$, $\tan y = x$) and differentiated
both sides, treating $y$ as an unknown function of $x$.

In Chapter~13, we make this general: curves like the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ cannot be
written as a single formula $y = f(x)$, yet their tangent slopes can be found by differentiating the
equation directly. Differentiating such equations with respect to time then lets us solve
related-rates problems, where several linked quantities change together.

\textbf{Equations that algebra cannot solve.}
With polynomials, finding where a derivative is zero or where two
curves intersect means solving polynomial equations, which for low degrees
can be done by algebra (factoring, the quadratic formula). But once exponentials or trig
functions appear, these problems lead to \emph{transcendental equations}:
\[
  x\,e^x = 1, \qquad x = \cos x, \qquad e^{-x^2} = x.
\]
None of these can be solved for $x$ by a finite combination of
algebraic operations. We can write down the derivative in
seconds, yet algebra cannot tell us where it equals zero.
In Chapter~19 (\emph{Newton's Method}) we will use the derivative itself to
build a step-by-step procedure that homes in on the solutions of such equations.
Near a solution, each step roughly doubles the number of correct digits, so a
handful of steps gives twenty decimal places.
\end{connectionbox}

\item $g(x) = e^{3x}\sin x$
\item $h(x) = \dfrac{\cos x}{x^2}$
\item $p(x) = \tan(2x) + \sec(2x)$
\item $q(x) = 3^x + x^3$ (mind which rule each term needs)
\item $r(x) = \ln(\cos x)$
\item $s(x) = e^4 + \sin(\pi/3)$ (pause and think: is $x$ present?)
\item $t(x) = x^e + e^x + e^e$
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Chain-rule combinations]
\label{ch12:exr-chain}
Differentiate.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\sin(e^x)$
\item $e^{\sin x}$
\item $\arctan(3x)$
\item $\arcsin(x^2)$
\item $\ln\!\big(\ln x\big)$
\item $\cos^2(5x)$
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Logarithmic differentiation]
\label{ch12:exr-logdiff}
Use logarithmic differentiation.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $y = (\sin x)^x$ for $0<x<\pi$.
\item $y = x^{\ln x}$.
\item $y = \dfrac{(x+1)^4 (x-2)^3}{(x^2+5)^2}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Find the base]
\label{ch12:exr-base}
Numerically estimate $m(4)=\lim_{h\to0}(4^h-1)/h$ with $h=0.0001$, then
confirm it equals $\ln 4$ to three decimals. Explain in one sentence why the
estimate and $\ln 4$ should agree.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The sigmoid identity]
\label{ch12:exr-sigmoid}
Let $\sigma(x) = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$. Show that
$\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)\big(1-\sigma(x)\big)$. (Hint: write
$\sigma = (1+e^{-x})^{-1}$ and use the chain rule, then refactor.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Tangent line]
\label{ch12:exr-tangent}
Find the equation of the tangent line to $y=\ln x$ at $x=e$. Where does this
tangent line cross the $x$-axis?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A radian sanity check]
\label{ch12:exr-radian}
Suppose a student insists on degrees and defines $S(x)=\sin(x^\circ)$, i.e.
the sine of $x$ degrees. Using $x^\circ = \tfrac{\pi}{180}x$ radians and the
chain rule, show that $S'(x) = \tfrac{\pi}{180}\cos(x^\circ)$. Explain why
this confirms that radians are the natural unit for calculus.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual: why proportional growth is exponential]
\label{ch12:exr-proportional}
Suppose a function $y(t)$ satisfies $y'(t) = k\,y(t)$ for a constant $k$, and
$y(0)=y_0$. Verify by differentiation that $y(t) = y_0 e^{kt}$ works. Then
argue informally why \emph{no} function of the form $y_0 b^t$ with
$b\ne e^k$ can satisfy the equation. (This is the precise sense in which
``rate proportional to size'' means ``exponential.'')
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: derivative of $a^x$ from scratch]
\label{ch12:exr-proof-ax}
Without quoting $\ddx{a^x}=a^x\ln a$, prove it. Start from the difference
quotient, factor out $a^x$, and identify the leftover limit $m(a)$. Then,
using $a = e^{\ln a}$ and $\ddx{e^x}=e^x$ together with the chain rule, show
$m(a)=\ln a$. Comment on how the two methods give the same constant.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: arccosine and the sign]
\label{ch12:exr-proof-arccos}
Use the identity $\arccos x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \arcsin x$ to prove that
$\ddx{\arccos x} = -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$. Then re-derive the same result
\emph{independently} by implicit differentiation of $\cos y = x$, being
careful about the sign of $\sin y$ on the range $y\in[0,\pi]$. Confirm the
two agree.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Higher derivatives and the cycle]
\label{ch12:exr-higher}
Compute the first four derivatives of $f(x)=\sin x$ and confirm the
period-four cycle. Then find a simple formula for the $n$-th derivative
$f^{(n)}(x)$ (the result of differentiating $n$ times) in terms of
$n \bmod 4$, the remainder when $n$ is divided by $4$. What is $f^{(101)}(x)$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mixed challenge]
\label{ch12:exr-challenge}
Differentiate $y = x^{\sin x}$ and find all $x$ in $(0,\pi)$ where the
relative rate of change $y'/y$ equals zero. Interpret these points on the
graph of $\ln y = (\sin x)(\ln x)$.
\end{exercise}
