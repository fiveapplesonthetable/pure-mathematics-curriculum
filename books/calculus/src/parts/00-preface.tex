\section*{Understanding before memorizing}

Calculus textbooks often focus on procedure: memorize these formulas, practice these
templates, and you will pass. Here the goal is different: work through these ideas with pencil
in hand, and you will \emph{understand}. The \emph{derivative} measures how fast a quantity is
changing at one instant; you will see why it has the form it does and where it came from. A
\emph{limit} is the value a quantity approaches as you close in on a point; you will see exactly what
that claim says. You will see why the rule for rates of change of nested functions (the \emph{chain
rule}) works, and why the area under a curve and the slope of a curve turn out to be two sides of a
single fact. When you meet a symbol like $\dd x$, or a phrase like ``the limit as $x$ approaches
$a$,'' you will know what it means.

That kind of understanding comes from working through concrete examples yourself. We start with what a function represents, and we finish, many
chapters later, with differential equations: equations that connect a quantity to its own rate of
change, which physics, biology, and economics use to describe how things change over time. Every step along the way grows out of a concrete problem, so each new idea feels like something you could have uncovered on your own.

\begin{focusbox}
The single sentence that organizes this entire book: \emph{calculus is the mathematics of change and
accumulation, and the bridge between them is the limit.} If you keep that sentence in view, every
chapter will find its natural place.
\end{focusbox}

Figure~\ref{ch00:two-problems} shows the two problems behind that sentence. On the left, $\dd x$
stands for a tiny step along the $x$-axis and $\dd f$ for the matching change in the height
$f(x)$; their ratio $\dd f/\dd x$ is the steepness of the curve at one point. It is found from a
small but not-yet-tiny step $\Delta x$ (read ``delta $x$'') and the change $\Delta f$ it produces,
by letting $\Delta x$ shrink toward zero; the notation $\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}$ means ``the value
approached as $\Delta x$ shrinks to zero.'' On the right, the long S-shaped sign $\int$ (an
\emph{integral} sign, a stretched letter S for ``sum'') stands for adding up thin strips of
height $f(x)$ and width $\dd x$ from $x=a$ to $x=b$, which gives the area under the curve.

There is a practical reason to insist on understanding, too: memorized recipes fade fast. Within a year of finishing a procedural
course, many students cannot recall the rules they drilled, because nothing
anchored the rules in place -- they were an arbitrary list, and unanchored lists decay. Ideas
you have explored with pictures and reasons last longer, because each one is supported
by the pictures around it. If you forget the rule for the rate of change of a fraction (the
\emph{quotient rule}), you can sketch a small nudge and rederive it in two lines. If you forget the
formula for a \emph{Taylor polynomial} (a polynomial built to imitate a curve near one point), you
can rebuild it from the requirement that produced it: match the curve's height, slope, and bending
at that point.

\section*{How this book is built: intuition, then picture, then proof}

Every important idea in this book is introduced in the same three movements, and it will help you to
know the rhythm in advance.

\textbf{First, intuition.} Before any symbols, we ask what the idea \emph{is} in plain terms.
What is a derivative? It is how fast something is changing right now -- the speedometer reading, not
the trip average. What is an integral? It is a total accumulated from tiny
contributions -- the full distance built up from every instant's speed. Figure~\ref{ch00:speedometer}
draws both ideas for one car trip.

\textbf{Second, the picture.} Calculus was discovered with pictures, and it is still understood with
pictures. A derivative is the slope of a \emph{tangent line}: the line that touches a curve at one
point and runs in the same direction as the curve there. An integral is an area. A limit is a process
of closing in. Almost every idea in this book has an exact geometric meaning, and we will draw it.
You will see \emph{secant lines} (lines through two points of a curve) pivot down into a tangent, as
in Figure~\ref{ch00:secant-tangent}; you will see more and thinner rectangles fill out a curved
region, as in Figure~\ref{ch00:rectangles}; you will see the graphs of Taylor polynomials hug a curve
over a wider and wider stretch, as in Figure~\ref{ch00:taylor}. The figures are part of the argument, so read them as carefully as
the text.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: the secant pivot.} Close your eyes and picture the parabola $y = x^2$ with the point $(1,1)$ held fixed. If you pick a second point far to the right, say $(3,9)$, the secant line joining them is steep (slope $4$). As you slide that second point down the curve toward $(1,1)$ through $(2,4)$, then $(1.5, 2.25)$, then $(1.1, 1.21)$, does the secant line become steeper or flatter? What number does its slope approach?
\end{recoverybox}

\textbf{Third, the proof.} Intuition can mislead, and pictures can
hide special cases. So after we see why an idea should be true, we make it precise and we prove it.
A \emph{proof} is a step-by-step argument showing that a statement must be true, where each step
follows from definitions or from facts already proved. For every proof we first explain
the key idea -- the step that makes it work -- and then we walk through the
steps, explaining each one the way an instructor talks through a derivation at the board.

\begin{intuitionbox}
You can read this book at two depths. On a first pass, read the intuition, study the figures,
and follow the overall structure of the proofs to get the main idea. On a second pass, slow down and follow every line
of the derivations. Both passes are legitimate. Mathematics is rarely understood in one sitting, and
no one learns it in a straight line. Circling back is how understanding is built.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section*{The colored boxes: a visual language}

Throughout the book you will meet recurring colored boxes. Each one plays a fixed role, so once you
learn the color, you know what kind of thinking is coming. Two of them examine notation
and technique directly within the flow of the mathematics:

\begin{happeningbox}
A \textbf{What is really happening} box explains the concrete meaning behind a symbol or a computation. What
does the $\dd x$ in $\int f(x)\,\dd x$ represent? What does
$\lim_{x\to a}$ claim, given that $x$ never has to equal $a$? What does the exponential function
$e^x$ represent (here $e \approx 2.718$ is a constant you will meet in the early chapters), and why
does it equal its own derivative? These boxes exist because mathematical notation packs a lot of reasoning into a few symbols, and
that packing can hide what is going on. We unpack the meaning.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{whybox}
A \textbf{Why the technique works} box shows why a method works instead of handing you a list of steps to copy.
Every computational rule in calculus grows out of an earlier property of limits or functions.
For example, two methods for computing integrals, called substitution and integration by parts, are
the chain rule and the product rule (the rule for the rate of change of a product) run backward.
Once you see \emph{why} a rule works, you no longer have to memorize it as a separate trick.
\end{whybox}

The remaining boxes round out the conversation, and you will get to know them by sight:

\begin{itemize}
  \item An \textbf{Intuition} box (green) states an idea in plain language before we make it precise.
  \item A \textbf{History} box (yellow) shows where an idea came from: who invented it, what problem they were trying to solve, and how it was eventually made \emph{rigorous}, that is, given an airtight logical foundation in which every step is justified. You will meet the preface's History box in the section on rigor.
  \item A \textbf{Common trap} box (red) flags a mistake almost everyone makes -- a place where the
  intuition lies, or where the notation invites an error.
  \item A \textbf{Walking question} box (orange) hands you a question to chew on away from the page,
  on a walk or in the shower. The best calculus thinking often happens when you are not staring at
  symbols. These have no answers printed; they are yours to turn over.
  \item A \textbf{Work it yourself} box (purple) asks you to compute one small thing by hand, right
  then, because some ideas only make sense once you work them out on paper.
  \item An \textbf{Application} box (magenta) shows the idea doing real work in physics, biology,
  economics, or machine learning -- connecting the abstract theory to concrete problems.
  \item A \textbf{Connection} box (teal) links the idea to other parts of mathematics, so the
  subject feels unified rather than like a list of isolated topics.
  \item A \textbf{Why this matters} box (cyan) steps back to say why an idea is important and how it

  \item A \textbf{Focus} box (blue) marks the one thing in a section you should not lose.
  \item A \textbf{Pause} box (white) stops you for a quick self-check or a word of encouragement.
  \item A \textbf{Reading note} box (gray) gives practical advice about how to use the book.

\section*{The arc of the book: from functions to differential equations}

Here is the order of topics, and why they come in that order. Figure~\ref{ch00:arc} summarizes it.

\textbf{We begin with functions and the objects calculus acts on.} Calculus does not study numbers;
it studies \emph{functions} -- rules that turn an input into an output, machines for relating one
changing quantity to another. So before anything else, we make sure you understand functions and the two families that calculus cannot live without. First come the exponential functions and the number $e$, which we will meet in three ways: through compound interest (interest that is added to the balance and then earns interest itself), through a limit, and as the function that equals its own rate of change. Next come the trigonometric functions, which describe angles in triangles and also rotation and anything that repeats in cycles, such as a vibrating string. These early
chapters are about \emph{meaning}: what these objects are, before we ever differentiate them.

\textbf{Then we build the limit.} The limit is the foundation that makes the
whole subject possible and the idea that took mathematicians the longest to get right. We approach
it first through pure intuition and a long historical look at the centuries of productive confusion,
and only then through the precise ``$\eps$--$\delta$'' (read ``epsilon-delta'') definition that finally made it rigorous; the Greek letters $\eps$ and $\delta$ stand for small allowed errors. We
spend real time here because everything downstream -- derivatives, integrals, \emph{series} (sums of
infinitely many terms), all of it -- is built directly on limits.

\textbf{With limits in hand, we build the derivative.} The derivative is the limit of those pivoting
secant lines in Figure~\ref{ch00:secant-tangent}: instantaneous rate of change, the slope of the
tangent. We learn what it means, then we earn the rules for finding derivatives of powers, products,
fractions, and functions inside functions (the power, product, quotient, and chain rules) by deriving
each from the definition, so they are never arbitrary. Then
we put the derivative to work: approximating a curve near a point by its tangent line (\emph{linear
approximation}); the Mean Value Theorem, which says that over any stretch of a smooth curve there is a
point where the instantaneous rate equals the average rate; finding the largest and smallest values of
a function (\emph{maxima} and \emph{minima}) and using them to pick the best choice in a practical
problem (\emph{optimization}); and l'H\^opital's rule, a method for limits of fractions in which the
top and bottom both shrink to zero (so that plugging in gives the meaningless $0/0$), which works by
comparing how fast the top and bottom head to zero.

\textbf{Next we build the integral, and meet the great theorem.} An integral is an accumulation -- an
area, a total, a sum of more and more, thinner and thinner pieces. We define it carefully as a limit of
sums, and then we arrive at the central result of the first half of the book: the Fundamental Theorem of
Calculus (FTC), which shows that differentiation and integration undo each other. Finding slopes and finding
areas look completely unrelated at first, yet each runs the other in reverse. From there we develop the main methods for computing integrals by hand -- substitution, integration by parts, and
partial fractions (splitting a complicated fraction into simpler ones) -- pairing each method with a ``why the technique works'' box so no rule feels pulled out of thin air.

\textbf{Then we extend the reach: series, and then more than one variable.} We learn to work with
infinite lists of numbers (\emph{sequences}) and to add up infinitely many terms (\emph{series}), and
we discover Taylor series, which let us rebuild
familiar functions like $e^x$, $\sin x$, and $\cos x$ directly from their derivatives at a
single point. Then we step up a dimension to functions of several variables. We study partial derivatives (how an output responds to one input while the others are held fixed) and the gradient (an arrow pointing in the direction of steepest climb). From there we build multiple integrals, which add up volume or mass over two- and three-dimensional regions, and end with a first taste of vector calculus: calculus for quantities that have a size and a direction at every point, such as the velocity of a flowing fluid or a force field.

\textbf{Finally we arrive at differential equations.} Here calculus describes systems that change
over time. A differential equation relates an unknown function to its derivatives, and
its solution describes how the quantity evolves over time. Population growth,
radioactive decay, a swinging pendulum, a cooling object, and electrical circuits -- all are described
by differential equations.

\section*{How to read this book}

A few practical suggestions.

\textbf{Draw.} This is the most important advice in this preface. Keep paper and pencil beside you
and redraw the figures yourself -- not trace
them, draw them. Sketch the secant lines pivoting into the tangent. Shade the area under a curve and
chop it into rectangles. Plot a function and guess its derivative before you compute it. The act of
drawing forces you to decide what you believe about the shape, and it exposes vague
understanding at once. Mathematicians who look like they are ``just thinking'' are often
drawing in their heads, and they learned to do that by drawing on paper first.

\begin{walkbox}
A car's speedometer reads
$60$ miles per hour at a single instant -- say, exactly at noon. But speed is distance divided by
time, and at a single \emph{instant} no time passes and no distance is covered. So what does
``$60$ miles per hour at this exact moment'' mean? Resolving this question leads directly to the concept of the derivative,
the central idea of the first half of this book.
\end{walkbox}

\textbf{Ask ``why'' relentlessly, and refuse to be satisfied too early.} When the book states a rule,
stop and ask why it should be true before you read on. When it draws a picture, ask what would change
if the curve bent the other way. When a proof makes a move, ask why that move and not another. This
book is written to reward that interrogation -- nearly every ``why'' you can think to ask is answered
somewhere, often in a colored box -- but the answer means far more if you have felt the question
first. A question you have wrestled with is a hook the answer can hang on. An answer to a
question you never asked just slides off.

\textbf{Compute things by hand, especially when you would rather not.} Doing the algebra with your own pencil builds an intuition for how terms cancel that reading alone never gives you. When a ``Work it yourself'' box asks you to take a derivative or evaluate an
integral, work it out on paper before reading on.

\begin{labbox}
Try this one now, before you turn the page, using only arithmetic. Take the function that
squares its input, $f(x)=x^2$. Let $h$ be a small nonzero step. The \emph{average rate of change}
between $x=1$ and the nearby point $x=1+h$ is the change in output divided by the change in input,
which is the slope of the secant line through $(1,1)$ and $(1+h,(1+h)^2)$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(1+h)-f(1)}{h} &= \frac{(1+h)^2-1^2}{h} = \frac{(1+2h+h^2)-1}{h} \\
&= \frac{2h+h^2}{h} = \frac{h(2+h)}{h} \\
&= 2+h \qquad \text{for } h \neq 0.
\end{aligned}
\]
Now plug in $h=0.1$, then $h=0.01$, then $h=0.001$. You get $2.1$, then $2.01$, then $2.001$. The
numbers are visibly homing in on $2$. That $2$ is the slope of the red tangent line in
Figure~\ref{ch00:secant-tangent}, and you just computed your first derivative -- by watching a quotient
close in on a value it never quite reaches. Hold on to the feeling of that ``closing in.'' It is the
limit, and the whole book turns on it. Figure~\ref{ch00:closing-in} plots these secant slopes
against $h$.
\end{labbox}

\textbf{Do the exercises.} Every chapter ends with a set of them -- some computational, some
conceptual, and usually a proof or two. The computational ones build fluency; the conceptual ones
build understanding; the proofs build the ability to be \emph{sure}. You do not have to do all of
them, but do not do none of them. Reading a worked example
is not the same as doing one, in the way that watching someone swim is not
the same as getting wet.

\begin{trapbox}
The most common way to fail at calculus is to read it like a novel -- smoothly, continuously, never
stopping. It cannot be read that way. If you find yourself nodding along for three pages without
pausing, you have almost certainly stopped understanding and started skimming. Real reading of
mathematics is slow and full of stops: rereading a line, working a tiny case in the margin, drawing
the picture. If a sentence does not make sense, do not push past it hoping it will clear up. Stop,
draw a sketch, and test a numerical example to find the exact line where a derivation stops making sense.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
And when you get stuck -- you will get stuck, everyone gets stuck -- that is not a sign you are bad at
this. Getting stuck is what doing mathematics feels like from the inside, for everyone, at every
level. The difference between people who learn calculus and people who give up is almost never raw
talent. It is whether, on getting stuck, they draw a picture, try a smaller case, reread the
definition, and stay with the discomfort for five more minutes.
\end{recoverybox}

\section*{A word on rigor, and a word of welcome}

You may have heard that calculus comes in two flavors: an easy ``intuitive'' version for engineers
and a hard ``rigorous'' version for mathematicians, and that you must choose between them. This book rejects that
choice. Intuition and rigor work together, and each is incomplete
without the other. Intuition without rigor led Newton and Leibniz to brilliant results along with
occasional nonsense, with no systematic way to distinguish them. Rigor without intuition is how calculus
becomes a dry sequence of formal $\eps$ and $\delta$ manipulations that produce correct proofs of theorems you cannot
picture and do not believe. We will develop both side by side. We use intuition to discover and pictures to
see, and we use rigor to be \emph{certain} -- to know, not merely to hope, that the geometric insight is
sound. The history boxes will show you, again and again, that this is how the subject
truly developed: the insight first, followed by the rigorous foundation.

\begin{historybox}
The people who invented calculus did not understand its foundations as well as you are about to.
In the 1660s and 1670s, the Englishman Isaac Newton and the German Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently
confronted two central problems: how to calculate instantaneous speed (the slope of a tangent line)
and how to measure curved areas. Their main tools were algebra, coordinate geometry (describing curves
by equations in $x$ and $y$, as introduced by Ren\'e Descartes), and the
intuitive idea of an ``infinitesimal''---a quantity imagined to be smaller than any positive real number
yet not zero.

Their methods yielded correct answers, but their logical footing was fragile. In 1734, the philosopher
Bishop George Berkeley published \emph{The Analyst}, criticizing the foundation of their calculations:
in computing a derivative, mathematicians treated a small increment as nonzero in order to divide by it,
and then treated the same increment as zero to discard the remaining terms. Meanwhile, a bitter dispute broke out over
whether Newton or Leibniz had invented calculus first. Both had discovered the core ideas independently:
Newton approached calculus through physics and motion, while Leibniz crafted the notation ($\dd x$ and $\int$)
that we still use today.

A coherent foundation took another century. In the 1820s, Augustin-Louis Cauchy began rebuilding the subject
on the idea of limits, and in the 1860s and 1870s Karl Weierstrass completed the foundation with the precise
$\eps$--$\delta$ definition, replacing infinitesimals with inequalities. In the 1960s, Abraham Robinson proved
that infinitesimal numbers can also be made completely rigorous within an extended number system known as the
hyperreals. Knowing this story reminds us that confusion is normal: mathematicians required two centuries to
articulate what you are about to master. Figure~\ref{ch00:timeline} places these events on a timeline.
\end{historybox}

\begin{connectionbox}
One last orientation. Although this book is about calculus, you will keep seeing links to the rest of
mathematics. The limit connects to the structure of the real numbers (an
appendix at the end pursues this). The derivative is a linear approximation, which ties calculus to
linear algebra (the mathematics of lines, planes, and their higher-dimensional versions). Series
connect to the theory of approximation and to complex analysis (calculus with numbers built from
$\sqrt{-1}$). Differential equations open onto all of physics. We will not chase every thread, but we
will point at them, so you can see where calculus sits in the rest of mathematics.
\end{connectionbox}

In Chapter~1, we start with two concrete questions: how fast a falling ball is moving at a single instant,
and how much area sits under a curve. Watch how both questions force us toward the same new idea.

\section*{Exercises}

These are not computational drills -- the real exercises begin in Chapter~1. They are meant to fix
the habits, mental pictures, and central themes of this preface before you turn the page.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pause and think: the speedometer.} Suppose a car travels $60$ miles along a highway
  between 1:00~PM and 2:00~PM. Its average speed is $60$ miles per hour. Must the speedometer have
  read exactly $60$~mph at some instant during that hour, or could it have read $50$~mph the entire
  time? What if the car left home at 1:00~PM, drove out, and returned home at 2:00~PM (net distance zero)?
  (This is a first taste of the Mean Value Theorem, which we will prove in Chapter~15.)

  \item \textbf{The closing-in feeling from both sides.} Return to the ``Work it yourself'' box, where
  the quotient $\bigl((1+h)^2-1^2\bigr)/h$ simplified to $2+h$ for $h \neq 0$. Compute it for $h=-0.1$,
  $h=-0.01$, and $h=-0.001$ -- that is, approaching $x=1$ from \emph{below}. Do the values still close
  in on $2$? What does the simplified form $2+h$ predict they must do? (A limit requires that approaching
  a target from the left and from the right arrives at the very same destination.)

  \item \textbf{Constant rate versus changing rate.} Consider the linear function $f(x) = 3x + 5$.
  Compute the average rate of change between $x = 1$ and $x = 1+h$:
  \[
    \frac{f(1+h) - f(1)}{h} = \frac{\bigl(3(1+h) + 5\bigr) - (3 \cdot 1 + 5)}{h}.
  \]
  Simplify the fraction algebraically. You should find that $h$ cancels completely, leaving the constant $3$
  with no dependency on $h$ at all. Why does a straight line never need a limiting process ($h \to 0$)
  to find its slope, whereas a curved parabola like $y = x^2$ does?

  \item \textbf{Reading the secant picture.} Look carefully at Figure~\ref{ch00:secant-tangent}. Every gray
  secant passes through the same point $(1,1)$, yet each has a different slope. In one or two
  sentences, say in your own words what is sliding, what is staying fixed, and what the red line is
  the limit \emph{of}.

  \item \textbf{The two problems in miniature.} Look at Figure~\ref{ch00:two-problems}. In Panel~(a),
  we zoom into a single point to find a slope $\dd f / \dd x$. In Panel~(b), we add up thin strips
  across an interval $[a,b]$ to find an area $\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$. In your own words, why does finding
  a local rate feel like ``cutting,'' while finding a total area feels like ``gluing''?

  \item \textbf{The structure and the limit.} Without looking back at Figure~\ref{ch00:arc}, sketch the
  chain from functions to differential equations from memory. Which single concept did the preface identify as
  the foundation, and how do later concepts build on it?

  \item \textbf{Carry the walking question.} Write down, in one sentence, the speedometer paradox:
  how can an object have a definite speed at a single instant, when no time elapses and no distance
  is traversed at that moment? Keep this question in mind as you begin Chapter~1.
\end{enumerate}
