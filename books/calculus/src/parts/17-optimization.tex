\chapter{Optimization}

In the previous chapter, we learned how to read the shape of a graph: we used the
first derivative to detect where a curve climbs and falls, the second derivative
to feel its curvature, and the Mean Value Theorem to prove that where a smooth peak
turns around, the tangent line must be flat. This chapter uses those tools to answer
an old question: \emph{what is the best you can do?}

Make the box hold the most. Use the least metal. Build the cheapest fence. Run the
shortest distance. Earn the highest profit. Questions like these helped drive the
invention of calculus. Fermat was solving maximum and minimum problems with a
slope-like idea decades before Newton and Leibniz, and the derivative grew partly out
of the wish to find peaks and valleys without guessing.

Once a problem is written as a function, the calculus is familiar: find where the
derivative is zero and check the candidates. Most of the work happens \emph{before}
you differentiate: turning a geometric or physical setup into a function of one
variable on a clear domain.

\section{What an optimization problem asks}

An optimization problem has exactly three ingredients.

\begin{focusbox}
Every applied max/min problem is built from:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item an \textbf{objective} --- the quantity you want to make as large or as small
    as possible (area, volume, cost, distance, time);
  \item one or more \textbf{constraints} --- the conditions tying your choices
    together (a fixed amount of fence, a fixed surface area, a point that must lie on
    a curve);
  \item a \textbf{domain} --- the set of choices that actually make sense (a length
    cannot be negative; a cut cannot be longer than the cardboard).
\end{enumerate}
Your job is to express the objective as a function of \emph{one} variable, find its
extreme value on the domain, and confirm you really found the best.
\end{focusbox}

Why aim for a single variable? We have one tool for finding peaks and valleys: set
the derivative to zero. That tool needs a function of one input. So no matter how many
quantities a problem mentions --- length, width, height, radius, angle --- our first
task is to collapse them down to one, using the constraint to express the others in
terms of it. The constraint is what \emph{makes the problem solvable}.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Picture the objective as a rolling surface of hills and valleys, and your constraint as
a hiking trail cut across it. Without the constraint you could wander across the whole
open map, and there might be no single summit at all. The constraint pins you to a
single trail. Now ``the best you can do'' is the highest point \emph{on that
trail}: a one-dimensional question about walking forward or backward along a line,
which is the kind of question a single derivative can answer. Much of optimization is
finding the trail.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{A method you can trust}

We follow the same recipe in every example. Doing the steps \emph{in order} keeps
a solution from turning into a tangle.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The modeling method for max/min problems.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Draw a picture} and name the quantities. Introduce letters for the
    things that can vary.
  \item \textbf{Write the objective} --- the thing to be maximized or minimized ---
    as a formula, even if it still has several variables in it.
  \item \textbf{Write the constraint(s)} as equations.
  \item \textbf{Reduce to one variable.} Use the constraint to eliminate every
    variable but one. Now the objective is a function $f$ of a single variable.
  \item \textbf{Find the domain} of that variable --- the interval of values that
    correspond to a real, physical configuration. Note the endpoints.
  \item \textbf{Differentiate and find the critical points}: solve $f'(x)=0$ and note
    where $f'$ fails to exist. (A critical point is a candidate location where the slope
    is zero or undefined --- a momentary flat spot or sharp crease where the curve could
    turn around.)
  \item \textbf{Test the candidates.} Compare the values of $f$ at the critical
    points \emph{and} at the endpoints of the domain. Use a first- or
    second-derivative test (Chapter~16), or the closed-interval method (next
    section), to be sure which candidate wins.
  \item \textbf{Answer the question that was asked} --- with units, and check it is
    sane.
\end{enumerate}
\end{focusbox}

Steps 1--5 contain no calculus. They are \emph{modeling}: translating the situation
into a function. If you get those right, step 6 is routine.

\begin{whybox}
Why does ``set the derivative to zero'' find the best? Because at an interior point
where $f$ is largest or smallest, the curve must, for that instant, be neither rising
nor falling --- if it were rising, you could nudge a hair to the right and do better;
if falling, a hair to the left. So the tangent there is horizontal: $f'(x)=0$. This
is \emph{Fermat's theorem}, proved in the chapter on maxima and minima: an
interior extreme of a differentiable function forces a \emph{stationary point} --- a
point where the tangent line flattens out and the rate of change is zero ($f'(x)=0$).
\end{whybox}

\begin{historybox}
The idea that nature itself optimizes goes back to Pierre de Fermat in 1662. For
decades, optics had rested on a rule found by experiment by Willebrord Snell in 1621:
when a ray of light passes from air into a denser medium like water, it bends toward
the perpendicular (this bending is called \emph{refraction}). Measure the angles
$\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ that the ray makes with the perpendicular to the surface,
before and after the bend; Snell found that the ratio $\sin\theta_1/\sin\theta_2$ is
always the same for a given pair of materials. But nobody knew \emph{why}. Ren\'e
Descartes had attempted a mechanical explanation by modeling light as tiny tennis
balls pushing through a membrane, but his model forced light to travel \emph{faster}
in dense water than in thin air, a conclusion that seemed physically backwards.

Fermat proposed a different hypothesis: light traveling between two points takes the
path of \emph{least time}, which need not be the path of shortest distance
(\emph{Fermat's Principle}). To test this, Fermat wrote the total travel time as a
function of one variable, the crossing point $x$ on the boundary
(Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-refraction}):
\[
  T(x) = \frac{\sqrt{a^2+x^2}}{v_1} + \frac{\sqrt{b^2+(d-x)^2}}{v_2}.
\]
Here the ray starts a height $a$ above the boundary and ends a depth $b$ below it, $d$
is the horizontal distance between start and end, and $v_1$ and $v_2$ are the speeds
of light in the two media. Each term is a distance (found by Pythagoras) divided by a
speed, which is a time. Fermat had no derivative or limit notation. Instead he used
his method of \emph{adequality}, an early version of the derivative: he nudged the
input $x$ by a tiny made-up amount $e$, set the new expression approximately equal to
the old one, canceled like terms, divided through by $e$, and finally discarded any
remaining terms containing $e$. Setting that rate of change to zero, Fermat derived
Snell's law,
\[
  \frac{\sin\theta_1}{v_1} = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{v_2},
\]
from pure mathematics. Nature, it appeared, was an optimizer.

Fermat's result caused a fierce dispute. Descartes' followers, led by Claude
Clerselier, protested that nature has no mind: how could a ray of light ``know'' in
advance which route takes the least time before setting out?

Thirty-four years later, in June 1696, Johann Bernoulli moved optimization from
numbers to whole curves by posing the \emph{brachistochrone problem}
(from the Greek \emph{brachistos}, shortest, and \emph{chronos}, time) in the journal
\emph{Acta Eruditorum}. Given two points $A$ and $B$ at different heights, he asked:
what curve carries a frictionless bead sliding under gravity from $A$ to $B$ in the
shortest possible time? A straight ramp gives the shortest distance, but a steeper
initial drop builds speed earlier; which path strikes the best balance?

Five mathematicians solved it: Johann Bernoulli, his older brother Jakob Bernoulli,
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, Guillaume de l'H\^opital, and Isaac Newton. Newton,
returning home tired from a day directing the Royal Mint in January 1697,
worked through the night and published his solution anonymously. Johann
Bernoulli recognized the author immediately, remarking: ``I recognize the lion by his
claw.'' The best curve was neither a straight line nor a circular arc, but a
\emph{cycloid}: the arch traced by a single point on the rim of a rolling
wheel (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-cycloid}). The contest started a bitter rivalry between the Bernoulli brothers, and it
began a new branch of mathematics, the \emph{calculus of variations}, in which the
unknown to be found is not a single number but a whole curve or function.

The idea spread through physics. Maupertuis and later Hamilton showed that classical
mechanics can be derived from the \emph{principle of least action}: moving particles
and orbiting planets follow paths that make a certain quantity, called the
\emph{action} (built from the energy of the motion added up over time), as small as
possible, or more precisely stationary. In the twentieth century Richard Feynman's
quantum theory of light described light as trying every possible path at once; the
paths near the least-time path reinforce each other while the others cancel out,
which is why we see light follow Fermat's path.

What remains open? For one variable on a closed interval, Fermat's method
finds every extreme. But modern optimization problems, such as training neural
networks with billions of adjustable numbers (weights), ask for the lowest point of a
surface in billions of dimensions. That surface has many separate valleys, ridges,
and \emph{saddle points} (spots that are flat but are a low point in some directions
and a high point in others). No method is known that is guaranteed to find the lowest
valley of such a surface in a reasonable time; computer scientists class the general
problem as \emph{NP-hard}, their label for problems believed to have no fast
solution method. Why simple step-downhill methods (gradient descent) nevertheless
find good solutions in practice is still not fully understood.
\end{historybox}

\section{The closed-interval method: making the answer certain}

One piece of theory turns a guess into a guarantee. Setting $f'(x)=0$ tells you where
the function \emph{might} peak. It does
not by itself tell you that a peak exists, nor that the candidate you found is the
global best. Two things can go wrong: the maximum might sit at an endpoint of the
domain rather than at a stationary point, or the function might not have a maximum at
all (think of $f(x)=x$ on all of $\R$ --- no biggest value).

The fix, which we put into checklist form at the end of Chapter~16, rests on the
\textbf{Extreme Value Theorem} from Chapter~8. In plain terms: on any unbroken
stretch of road of finite length that includes its two ends, there is guaranteed to
be a highest point and a lowest point.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Extreme Value Theorem.} If $f$ is continuous on a \emph{closed, bounded}
interval $[a,b]$, then $f$ attains both a maximum and a minimum value somewhere on
$[a,b]$.
\end{focusbox}

For optimization this means: as long as your objective is continuous
and your domain is a closed interval $[a,b]$, a best value is \emph{guaranteed to
exist}, and it can only live in one of two kinds of places: at a critical point
inside, or at an endpoint. That gives a finite checklist.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The closed-interval method.} To find the absolute max and min of a continuous
$f$ on $[a,b]$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Find all critical points in $(a,b)$ (where $f'=0$ or $f'$ does not exist).
  \item Evaluate $f$ at each critical point.
  \item Evaluate $f$ at the two endpoints $a$ and $b$.
  \item The largest of these values is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the
    absolute minimum.
\end{enumerate}
No derivative tests are needed; you only compare numbers. The theorem promises the
winner is in your list.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{whybox}
Why is comparing values \emph{enough}? Because the Extreme Value Theorem promises the
maximum is attained \emph{somewhere}. Wherever that somewhere is, it is either an
interior point or an endpoint. If interior, Fermat's theorem says it is a critical
point, so it is in your list. If an endpoint, it is also in your list. There is
nowhere else for it to hide. So you do not need to analyze concavity or signs of
$f'$ at all; you need to make sure your list of candidate points is complete and then
read off the largest value.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
The closed-interval method requires \emph{three} pieces, and beginners drop one:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Continuity}: a jump or a vertical asymptote in the domain breaks the
    guarantee. The function $1/x$ on $[-1,1]$ has no maximum --- it is not continuous
    there.
  \item \textbf{Closed} (endpoints included): on the open interval $(0,1)$ the
    function $f(x)=x$ has no maximum, because it can get arbitrarily close to $1$
    without reaching it.
  \item \textbf{Bounded}: on $[0,\infty)$ the function $f(x)=x$ runs off to infinity.
\end{itemize}
If your domain is not a closed bounded interval, you must argue existence another way
(often by the shape of the graph or a sign analysis of $f'$).
Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-evt-fail} draws the three failures.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{happeningbox}
When you ``set $f'(x)=0$ and solve,'' you are searching for \emph{stationary
configurations}: choices where a tiny change in your choice produces almost no change
in the objective (the change is much smaller than the nudge itself). Think of a ball
resting on a curved track. At the top of a hill, at the bottom of a valley, or on a
level shelf, the ball can sit still, because the track is flat there. The derivative
measures how sensitive your objective is to a small nudge. Setting it to zero says
``find the spot where I am insensitive to nudges.'' That spot is a candidate for
best, but you still have to check whether it is a peak (max), a valley (min), or
merely a flat shelf where the curve levels off and then keeps going the same way (as
$y=x^3$ does at $x=0$). That is why the second-derivative test and the
closed-interval comparison exist: the zero derivative locates the flat spots, and the
second derivative or the comparison of values sorts them.
\end{happeningbox}

\section{Worked example: the open-top box}

This classic problem runs every step of the method on something you can hold in
your hands.

\begin{example}[The fold-up box]\label{ch17:ex-box}
You have a square sheet of cardboard $30$ cm on a side. You cut a small square of
side $x$ from each corner, fold up the four flaps, and tape them to make an open-top
box. What size cut $x$ gives the box of \emph{largest volume}?
\end{example}

corner and folding up leaves a base that is a square of side $30-2x$ (you removed $x$
from each end of every side), and the height of the box is the cut $x$
(Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-box}).

  \draw[<->, >=stealth] (2.95,-0.1) -- (4.25,0.9) node[midway, below right, font=\footnotesize] {$30-2x$};
  \draw[<->, >=stealth] (-0.35,0) -- (-0.35,2.0) node[midway, left, font=\footnotesize] {$x$};
  \node[font=\small, anchor=south] at (2.05,3.4) {\textbf{Folded Open-Top Box}};

leaves a square base of side $30-2x$. Right: folding up the four flaps along the dashed
lines creates an open-top box of height $x$, base area $(30-2x)^2$, and volume
$V(x)=x(30-2x)^2$.}
\label{ch17:fig-box}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Step 2 --- objective.} The volume is base area times height:
\[
V = (30-2x)^2 \cdot x.
\]
This is already a function of the single variable $x$: the geometry built the
constraint in, so Steps 3 and 4 (write the constraint, eliminate variables) are not
needed here.

\textbf{Step 5 --- domain.} What values of $x$ make a real box? We need $x>0$ (a
genuine cut) and we need the base side $30-2x>0$, that is, $x<15$. At $x=0$ there is no
height; at $x=15$ there is no base. Including those \emph{degenerate} endpoints (cases
that are boxes only in a limiting sense, with zero volume) gives the closed interval
\[
0 \le x \le 15.
\]
We include the endpoints on purpose: now the domain is closed and bounded, $V$ is
continuous, and the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees a maximum exists. That is worth
the small fiction of allowing the zero-volume cases.

\textbf{Step 6 --- differentiate and find critical points.} Expand first to make the
derivative easy:
\begin{align*}
V &= x(30-2x)^2 \\
  &= x(900 - 120x + 4x^2) \\
  &= 900x - 120x^2 + 4x^3.
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{align*}
V'(x) &= 900 - 240x + 12x^2 \\
      &= 12\,(x^2 - 20x + 75) \\
      &= 12\,(x-5)(x-15).
\end{align*}
So $V'(x)=0$ at $x=5$ and $x=15$. The point $x=15$
is an endpoint (and gives zero volume), so the only interior critical point is
\[
x = 5.
\]

\textbf{Step 7 --- test.} Use the closed-interval method: evaluate $V$ at the
critical point and both endpoints.
\[
V(0)=0, \qquad V(5) = 5\cdot 20^2 = 5\cdot 400 = 2000, \qquad V(15)=0.
\]
The largest value is $V(5)=2000$, so the maximum volume is $\boxed{2000\text{ cm}^3}$,
achieved by cutting squares of side $\boxed{x=5\text{ cm}}$
(Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-box-volume}).

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} Look at the two critical numbers $x=5$ and $x=15$. Without
writing down any algebra, what physical shape does $x=15$ describe?
\emph{Answer}: Cutting $15\text{ cm}$ from each end of a $30\text{ cm}$ side consumes
the entire cardboard sheet ($2 \times 15 = 30$). The base collapses to a crease of
width zero, leaving a flat folded sheet with zero volume ($V=0$). The derivative
detected this flat, degenerate case as a critical point alongside the
genuine peak at $x=5$.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{labbox}
Confirm the peak yourself with the second-derivative test. We have $V'(x)=12x^2-240x
+900$, so $V''(x)=24x-240=24(x-10)$. At the critical point, $V''(5)=24(5-10)=-120<0$.
A negative second derivative means the graph is concave down at $x=5$, confirming a
local maximum (a hilltop, not a valley). This agrees with the
closed-interval comparison and gives a second, independent confirmation. Then sketch
the graph from $V(0)=0$, the peak at $(5,2000)$, and $V(15)=0$, and watch it match
Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-box-volume}.
\end{labbox}

\begin{trapbox}
A common slip on the box problem is to use the side of the base, $30-2x$, as the
height, or to forget that you remove $x$ from \emph{both} ends of each side, writing
the base as $30-x$ instead of $30-2x$. Draw the picture every time. The factor of two
comes straight from the two corners on each edge, and getting it wrong gives a wrong
answer even though the algebra that follows looks fine.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Worked example: the cheapest can}

Now a problem where the constraint does real work, because the objective and
the constraint involve different combinations of the variables.

\begin{example}[The minimal-surface can]\label{ch17:ex-can}
A cylindrical can must hold exactly $355$ cm$^3$ (a standard soda can). What radius
and height minimize the surface area --- that is, use the least metal?
\end{example}

\textbf{Step 1 --- picture and variables.} A cylinder has radius $r$ and height $h$
(Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-can-geometry}).

\textbf{Step 2 --- objective.} A closed can has a top disk, a bottom disk, and a
wrapped-around side (the lateral surface). The two disks contribute $2\pi r^2$; the
side, if you unroll it, is a rectangle of width $2\pi r$ (the circumference) and
height $h$, contributing $2\pi r h$. So the surface area is
\[
A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h.
\]
This has \emph{two} variables. We cannot differentiate it yet.

\textbf{Step 3 --- constraint.} The volume is fixed:
\[
\pi r^2 h = 355.
\]

\textbf{Step 4 --- reduce to one variable.} Solve the constraint for the variable
that is easiest to isolate. Here $h$ comes out cleanly:
\[
h = \frac{355}{\pi r^2}.
\]
Substitute into the objective so that $A$ depends on $r$ alone:
\[
A(r) = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r\cdot \frac{355}{\pi r^2}
     = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{710}{r}.
\]

\textbf{Step 5 --- domain.} Any positive radius gives a valid can (with $h$ adjusting
to keep the volume right), so the domain is $r>0$, the open interval $(0,\infty)$.
This is \emph{not} a closed bounded interval, so the closed-interval method does not
apply directly; we will instead use the shape of $A(r)$ to confirm the minimum.
At the two ends of the domain: as $r\to 0^+$, the term $710/r\to\infty$ (a tall
thin can has a huge side area); as $r\to\infty$, the term $2\pi r^2\to\infty$ (a wide
flat can has huge disks). So $A\to\infty$ at both ends, which already tells us a
minimum sits somewhere in the middle. Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-can-shapes} shows three
cans of the same volume.

\textbf{Step 6 --- differentiate.}
\[
A'(r) = 4\pi r - \frac{710}{r^2}.
\]
Set it to zero and solve for $r$:
\begin{align*}
4\pi r &= \frac{710}{r^2}\\
4\pi r^3 &= 710 &&\text{(multiply both sides by $r^2$)}\\
r^3 &= \frac{710}{4\pi} = \frac{177.5}{\pi}.
\end{align*}
So
\[
r = \left(\frac{177.5}{\pi}\right)^{1/3} \approx (56.50)^{1/3} \approx 3.837\text{ cm}.
\]
And the height is
\[
h = \frac{355}{\pi r^2} \approx \frac{355}{\pi (3.837)^2}
  \approx \frac{355}{46.25} \approx 7.675\text{ cm}.
\]

\textbf{Step 7 --- confirm it is a minimum.} Two independent checks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Second derivative.} $A''(r) = 4\pi + \dfrac{1420}{r^3} > 0$ for every
    $r>0$. The function is concave up everywhere, so the single critical point is a
    minimum --- in fact the \emph{global} minimum, since a function that is concave up
    on all of $(0,\infty)$ can have only one turning point.
  \item \emph{End behavior.} We saw $A\to\infty$ as $r\to0^+$ and as $r\to\infty$,
    with exactly one critical point in between. A continuous function that climbs to
    infinity at both ends and turns around once must turn at a minimum
(Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-can}).
\end{itemize}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Look at the answer: $h \approx 7.675$ and $r \approx 3.837$, so
$h = 2r$. The most efficient closed can is exactly as \emph{tall} as it is
\emph{wide}: its height equals its diameter, so it fits snugly inside a cube. This
ratio does not depend on the specific number $355$; you get $h=2r$ for \emph{any}
fixed volume $V$. To check, repeat the substitution with $355$ replaced by $V$:
\[
h=\frac{V}{\pi r^2}, \qquad A(r)=2\pi r^2 + \frac{2V}{r}.
\]
Setting $A'(r)=0$ gives
\begin{align*}
4\pi r &= \frac{2V}{r^2}\\
2\pi r^3 &= V = \pi r^2 h &&\text{(multiply by $r^2/2$)}\\
2r &= h &&\text{(divide by $\pi r^2$)},
\end{align*}
no matter what $V$ is. (Real soda cans are taller and thinner than this, because the seams and the
thicker top cost extra metal that the simple model ignores, and because a hand likes
a narrower grip. The calculation is right for the model; the model leaves those
costs out.)
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{whybox}
Why does substituting the constraint give the right answer, rather than some
distortion? Because the constraint equation $\pi r^2 h = 355$ does not throw any cans
away: it records that for each radius there is exactly one matching height.
The substitution walks along the \emph{curve of all valid cans} in the $(r,h)$ plane,
and $A(r)$ is the true surface area of the one valid can with that radius. We are
not approximating; we are looking only at the \emph{feasible set} (the set of choices
that satisfy the constraint) and reading off each one's true area. Minimizing along
this trail gives the minimum over all valid cans, which is what was asked. So
``reduce to one variable'' loses nothing: the eliminated variable was never free,
because the constraint had already decided it.
\end{whybox}

\section{Worked example: the fence with a free side}

A constraint can also be the thing in short supply. This is the classic farmer
problem, and it teaches a lesson about reading the geometry before reaching for
algebra.

\begin{example}[Maximum field, one free side]\label{ch17:ex-fence}
A farmer has $200$ m of fencing and wants to enclose a rectangular field. One side of
the field runs along a straight river and needs no fence. What dimensions enclose the
\emph{largest area}?
\end{example}

\textbf{Step 1 --- picture and variables.} Call the two sides perpendicular to the
river $x$ (there are two of them) and the side parallel to the river $y$ (there is
one of it --- the opposite, river side, is free); see Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-fence}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9]
% river
\draw[line width=2pt, blue!40] (-0.4,2.2)--(6.4,2.2);
\node[blue!50!black, font=\small] at (3,2.5) {river (no fence)};
% three fenced sides
\draw[thick] (0,2.2)--(0,0)--(6,0)--(6,2.2);
\node[font=\small] at (-0.35,1.1) {$x$};
\node[font=\small] at (6.35,1.1) {$x$};
\node[font=\small] at (3,-0.35) {$y$};
\node[font=\small] at (3,1.1) {area $=xy$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Three sides are fenced; the river bounds the fourth for free. Fencing used
is $2x+y$.}
\label{ch17:fig-fence}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Step 2 --- objective.} Maximize the area
\[
A = xy.
\]
\textbf{Step 3 --- constraint.} Only three sides use fence, and there are $200$ m of
it:
\[
2x + y = 200.
\]
\textbf{Step 4 --- reduce.} Solve the constraint for $y$: $y = 200 - 2x$. Substitute:
\[
A(x) = x(200-2x) = 200x - 2x^2.
\]
\textbf{Step 5 --- domain.} We need $x>0$ and $y = 200-2x>0$, so $0 < x < 100$;
including the degenerate endpoints (zero area) gives the closed interval $[0,100]$,
and again the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees a maximum.

\textbf{Step 6 --- differentiate.}
\[
A'(x) = 200 - 4x = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 50.
\]
Then $y = 200 - 2(50) = 100$.

\textbf{Step 7 --- test.} Closed-interval comparison:
\[
A(0)=0, \qquad A(50) = 50\cdot 100 = 5000, \qquad A(100)=0.
\]
The maximum area is $\boxed{5000\text{ m}^2}$, with the two short sides $x=50$ m and
the long side $y=100$ m. (The second derivative $A''=-4<0$ confirms concave down, a
maximum.)

\begin{intuitionbox}
The answer splits the fence in a memorable way: the single parallel side $y$
uses $100$ m and the two perpendicular sides together also use $2x=100$ m. The fence
is divided \emph{equally} between the direction along the river and the direction
across it (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-fence-shapes}). This even split shows up again and
again in rectangle problems like this one, and once you have seen it you can often
guess the answer before differentiating, then use calculus to confirm.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} Suppose the farmer had $300\text{ m}$ of fence instead of
$200\text{ m}$, still bordering a straight river. Using the ``split the resource equally
between the two directions'' rule from the intuition box above, can you find the
optimal dimensions in your head?
\emph{Answer}: Half the fence ($150\text{ m}$) goes to the single side $y$ parallel to
the river. The remaining half ($150\text{ m}$) is split equally between the two
perpendicular sides, giving $x = 75\text{ m}$. The maximum area is $75 \times 150 =
11{,}250\text{ m}^2$. Differentiating $A(x) = x(300-2x)$ gives $A'(x) = 300 - 4x = 0$,
confirming $x = 75$.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{trapbox}
The free side changes the constraint, not the area. A frequent error is to fence all
four sides out of habit, writing $2x+2y=200$, or to optimize the perimeter instead of
the area. Always ask: \emph{which} quantity is fixed (the constraint) and \emph{which}
am I trying to make extreme (the objective)? Here the fence is fixed and the area is
free to grow. Swapping their roles --- maximizing the fence --- is a question with no
answer, and that absurdity is your signal that you mislabeled them.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Worked example: the closest point}

Distance problems use a trick that gets rid of square roots, and they show how
to argue that a minimum exists when the domain is the whole real line.

\begin{example}[Nearest point on a parabola]\label{ch17:ex-distance}
Find the point on the parabola $y = x^2$ that is closest to the point $(0, 2)$.
\end{example}

\textbf{Steps 1--4 --- variables and objective.} The constraint ``the point lies on the
parabola'' is built in by writing a point on the parabola as
$(x, x^2)$. Its distance to $(0,2)$ is
\[
D(x) = \sqrt{(x-0)^2 + (x^2 - 2)^2} = \sqrt{x^2 + (x^2-2)^2}.
\]
We want to minimize this.

\textbf{The trick.} Minimizing the distance $D$ is the same as minimizing the
\emph{squared} distance $S = D^2$, because squaring is an increasing function on
$[0,\infty)$ --- wherever $D$ is smallest, $D^2$ is smallest too, and at the same
$x$. So we minimize the square-root-free
\[
S(x) = x^2 + (x^2 - 2)^2,
\]
which is far easier to differentiate. This substitution costs nothing and saves a
chain-rule-through-a-square-root mess.

\begin{whybox}
Why are you allowed to minimize $D^2$ instead of $D$? Because the squaring map
$t\mapsto t^2$ is strictly increasing for $t\ge 0$, and distances are never negative.
A strictly increasing function preserves order: if $D(a) < D(b)$ then $D(a)^2 <
D(b)^2$, and vice versa. So $D$ and $D^2$ rise and fall in lockstep; they have their
minimum at the \emph{same} $x$ (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-sq-distance} draws both
graphs). The only thing that changes is the messiness of the
derivative. This is a general and useful move: you may replace your objective by
any strictly increasing function of it (square it when it is nonnegative, take its
log when it is positive, and so on) without moving the location of the optimum.
People who solve optimization problems use logs and squares constantly for
exactly this reason.
\end{whybox}

\textbf{Step 5 --- domain.} Any real $x$ gives a point on the parabola, so the domain
is all of $\R = (-\infty, \infty)$. As $\abs{x}\to\infty$ the point on the parabola
shoots off to infinity, so $S\to\infty$ at both ends; a minimum exists in the middle.

\textbf{Step 6 --- differentiate and find critical points.} Expand:
\[
S(x) = x^2 + x^4 - 4x^2 + 4 = x^4 - 3x^2 + 4.
\]
Then
\[
S'(x) = 4x^3 - 6x = 2x(2x^2 - 3).
\]
So $S'(x)=0$ gives $x=0$ or $2x^2=3$, that is,
$x = \pm\sqrt{3/2} = \pm\sqrt{1.5} \approx \pm 1.225$.

\textbf{Step 7 --- test.} Compare the squared distances at the three critical points:
\begin{align*}
S(0) &= 0 - 0 + 4 = 4,\\
S\!\left(\pm\sqrt{1.5}\right) &= (1.5)^2 - 3(1.5) + 4 = 2.25 - 4.5 + 4 = 1.75.
\end{align*}
The smaller value $1.75$ wins, so the closest points are
$\left(\pm\sqrt{1.5},\, 1.5\right)$, at distance $D = \sqrt{1.75} \approx 1.323$
(Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-distance}). Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-sq-distance} shows the graph
of $S$ itself, together with $D$.

\begin{trapbox}
The vertex $x=0$ is a genuine critical point ($S'(0)=0$), but it is \emph{not} the
answer. In fact $S$ has a local \emph{maximum} there: points of the parabola just to
either side of the vertex are closer to $(0,2)$ than the vertex is
(Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-sq-distance}). This is why you never stop at ``I found where
$S'=0$.'' You must evaluate $S$ at \emph{all} the critical points and compare the
resulting values. Had you found only $x=0$ and quit, you would have reported a local
maximum instead of the minimum.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
There is a piece of geometry behind the algebra. At the true closest point, the
segment from $(0,2)$ to the curve is \emph{perpendicular} to the parabola's tangent
line. To see why: if the connecting segment met the curve at any other
angle, you could slide a little along the curve and get closer, because part of your
motion would be ``toward'' the target. Only when the segment is perpendicular is there
no closer direction to slide. The algebra says the same thing. The segment from
$(x,x^2)$ to $(0,2)$ points in the direction $(x,\,x^2-2)$, and the tangent line at
$(x,x^2)$ points in the direction $(1,\,2x)$, since its slope is $2x$. Two directions
are perpendicular when the products of matching components add to zero, and
\[
x\cdot 1 + (x^2-2)\cdot 2x = \tfrac12 S'(x),
\]
so $S'(x)=0$ and ``segment $\perp$ tangent'' are the same condition. The line
perpendicular to the tangent at a point is called the \emph{normal line} there, so the
rule reads: the shortest segment from a point to a curve lies along a normal line. It
returns in multivariable calculus as the idea behind the method of Lagrange
multipliers (Chapter~33).
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think.} Why did the vertex $x=0$ show up as a critical point of the
distance function $S(x)$ at all? If you stand at the target $(0,2)$ and look down at
the origin $(0,0)$, the distance is $2$. If you take a tiny step along the parabola to
either side, say to $(\pm 0.2, 0.04)$, does the distance to $(0,2)$ grow or shrink?
\emph{Answer}: The squared distance to $(\pm 0.2, 0.04)$ is
\[
(0.2)^2 + (0.04 - 2)^2 = 0.04 + 3.8416 = 3.8816 < 4.
\]
Stepping away from the vertex brings the curve \emph{closer} to $(0,2)$. The vertex is
a local peak of distance (locally farthest). The graph of $S$ is flat at the top of
that peak, which is why $S'(0)=0$ picked it up.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{A profit example: optimization beyond geometry}

The same method works outside geometry. Here is an example from economics.

\begin{example}[Maximizing profit]\label{ch17:ex-profit}
A workshop can sell $x$ handmade lamps per week. Market research says that to sell $x$
lamps the price per lamp must be $p(x) = 120 - 2x$ dollars (sell more, charge less).
Each lamp costs \$20 to make, plus \$300 in fixed weekly overhead (costs such as rent
that must be paid however many lamps are made). How many lamps
maximize weekly profit?
\end{example}

\textbf{Objective.} Profit is revenue (the money taken in from sales) minus cost.
Revenue is price times quantity:
\[
R(x) = x\,p(x) = x(120 - 2x) = 120x - 2x^2.
\]
Cost is overhead plus per-unit cost:
\[
C(x) = 300 + 20x.
\]
So profit is
\begin{align*}
P(x) &= R(x) - C(x)\\
     &= (120x - 2x^2) - (300 + 20x)\\
     &= -2x^2 + 100x - 300.
\end{align*}

\textbf{Domain.} We need $x\ge 0$, and keeping the price nonnegative ($120-2x\ge 0$)
gives $x\le 60$. Including the zero-output and zero-price boundaries gives $0 \le x \le 60$
(a closed bounded interval, so the Extreme Value Theorem applies).

\textbf{Differentiate.}
\[
P'(x) = -4x + 100 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 25.
\]
\textbf{Test.} Since $P''(x) = -4 < 0$ everywhere (and the endpoints lose money, with
$P(0)=-300$ and $P(60)=-1500$), $x=25$ is the global maximum on $[0,60]$. The profit is
\[
P(25) = -2(625) + 100(25) - 300 = -1250 + 2500 - 300 = 950.
\]
Make $\boxed{25\text{ lamps per week for \$950 profit}}$, at a price of
$p(25) = 120 - 50 = \$70$ each.

\begin{applicationbox}
Economists know the condition $P'(x)=0$ as the rule \emph{marginal
revenue equals marginal cost}. Since $P'=R'-C'$, $P'=0$ means $R'(x)=C'(x)$. The
derivative $R'$ is the extra revenue from selling one more unit (marginal revenue) and
$C'$ is the extra cost of making one more unit (marginal cost). The rule says: keep
producing as long as the next unit brings in more than it costs, and stop when
they balance (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-profit}). Past that point each extra lamp costs more
than it earns and profit falls. It is the same zero-derivative condition as for the
cardboard box, in the language of money.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
The same pattern drives modern machine learning. Training a model means choosing
its adjustable numbers (the \emph{weights}) to \emph{minimize} a \emph{loss
function}, a number that measures how wrong the model's answers are. The objective is
the loss, the variables are the millions of weights, and ``learning'' is optimization.
There is no constraint to substitute and no formula for the answer, so instead of
solving ``derivative of the loss $=0$'' exactly, the computer rolls downhill: it
repeatedly takes a small step in the direction that lowers the loss fastest. That
direction is given by the \emph{gradient}, the many-variable version of the
derivative, and the method is called \emph{gradient descent}. The target is the same
as in this chapter: a point where the derivative is zero, a stationary point of the
objective. Every example here is a small, hand-solvable version of what a neural
network training program does numerically.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{When the candidate is not the answer: pitfalls and checks}

The most common mistake, in exams and in real work, is to find a critical point and
\emph{assume} it is the extreme you want. It might be a minimum when you wanted a
maximum. It might be a local extreme beaten by an endpoint. It might be a flat spot
that is neither, where the curve levels off and then keeps going the same way.
Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-crit-types} shows the three possibilities.

The following checks tell these cases apart.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Three ways to confirm you found the right extreme.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Closed-interval method} (best when the domain is a closed bounded
    interval): evaluate the objective at every critical point and both endpoints,
    then read off the largest/smallest. The Extreme Value Theorem guarantees the
    winner is in your list. No further argument needed.
  \item \textbf{First-derivative test} (works on any interval): check the sign of
    $f'$ just left and just right of the critical point. Sign change $+\to-$ means a
    local max; $-\to+$ means a local min; no change means neither.
  \item \textbf{Second-derivative test} (quick when $f''$ is easy): at a critical
    point, $f''<0$ means local max (concave down), $f''>0$ means local min (concave
    up). If $f''=0$ the test is silent --- fall back to method 1 or 2.
\end{enumerate}
For an \emph{open} or \emph{unbounded} domain, also check the behavior at the open
ends (limits as $x$ approaches the boundary or $\pm\infty$) to be sure a max or min
exists at all.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The endpoint that wins.} Suppose you must minimize $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ on
$[0, 3]$. The critical points solve $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 0$, giving $x=\pm 1$; only
$x=1$ is in the domain, and there $f(1) = 1 - 3 = -2$. If you stopped there you would
report the minimum as $-2$ --- and you would be right. But if instead the problem asked
for the \emph{maximum} on $[0,3]$, the critical point $x=1$ is a local \emph{minimum},
not a maximum at all. The maximum lives at the endpoint: $f(3) = 27 - 9 = 18$ versus
$f(0)=0$. The lesson: the interesting answer is sometimes at the edge of the domain,
where the derivative never vanishes (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-endpoint}).
\emph{Always check the endpoints.}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{walkbox}
Take a walk and think about \emph{existence} versus \emph{location}. The derivative
is a tool for \emph{location}: it points at where an extreme could be. But it says
nothing about whether an extreme \emph{exists}. What guarantees existence? On a closed
bounded interval, continuity does (the Extreme Value Theorem). On an open or infinite
domain you need something else --- the function running off to infinity at both ends,
say, or a single turning point. Ask yourself: for each worked example in this chapter,
\emph{what} guaranteed the answer existed before I trusted the critical point? You
will find the closed-interval cases leaned on the theorem, while the can and the
distance problems leaned on $\to\infty$ end-behavior. Existence and location are two
separate questions, and a complete solution answers both.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{happeningbox}
When a problem has no maximum, the symptom is usually that the objective improves
forever as you slide toward a boundary the domain does not include. For instance, ``find the
rectangle of largest area with perimeter at most $100$'' has an answer, whereas ``find the
rectangle of largest area'' (with no constraint) does not --- you can always make a bigger
one. The constraint is what bounds the feasible set and lets a best exist. So when a
real optimization has no solution, the fix is usually not more calculus; it is
finding the missing constraint (the budget, the material limit, the physical wall)
that the problem forgot to state.
\end{happeningbox}

\section{A fully general template, worked symbolically}

Here is one more rectangle problem, done \emph{with letters instead of numbers}, so
you can see the structure of the method on its own.

\begin{example}[Largest rectangle inscribed under a parabola]\label{ch17:ex-inscribed}
A rectangle sits with its base on the $x$-axis and its top two corners on the
downward parabola $y = c - x^2$ (with $c>0$), symmetric about the $y$-axis. What width
makes its area largest?
\end{example}

\textbf{Variables.} By symmetry the top corners are at $(\pm x, c - x^2)$ for some
$x>0$. The rectangle then has width $2x$ and height $c - x^2$.

\textbf{Objective.}
\[
A(x) = (\text{width})(\text{height}) = 2x\,(c - x^2) = 2cx - 2x^3.
\]
\textbf{Domain.} We need the top corners above the axis, $c - x^2 \ge 0$, so
$0 \le x \le \sqrt{c}$ --- a closed bounded interval.

\textbf{Differentiate.}
\[
A'(x) = 2c - 6x^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2 = \frac{c}{3}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \sqrt{c/3}.
\]
\textbf{Test.} The endpoints give $A(0)=0$ and $A(\sqrt c)=0$, while the critical
point gives a positive value, so the critical point is the maximum. (Or use
$A''(x)=-12x<0$ for $x>0$.) The maximal area is
\begin{align*}
A\!\left(\sqrt{c/3}\right) &= 2\sqrt{\tfrac{c}{3}}\left(c - \tfrac{c}{3}\right)\\
&= 2\sqrt{\tfrac{c}{3}}\cdot \tfrac{2c}{3}\\
&= \frac{4c}{3}\sqrt{\frac{c}{3}}.
\end{align*}
Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-inscribed} shows the optimal rectangle.

\section{Summary: the shape of every solution}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{What to carry away.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Optimization $=$ objective $+$ constraint $+$ domain. The first and hardest
    job is \emph{modeling}: turning words and pictures into a one-variable function
    on a definite domain.
  \item The constraint is what lets you eliminate variables and
    reach a single-variable function the derivative can handle.
  \item Critical points (where $f'=0$ or $f'$ is undefined) are \emph{candidates},
    not answers. Endpoints are candidates too.
  \item On a closed bounded interval, the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees a best
    exists, and the closed-interval method finds it by comparing values --- no
    derivative test required.
  \item On open or infinite domains, you must separately argue that an extreme exists
    (end behavior, concavity, or a single turning point).
  \item Confirm with the closed-interval comparison, the first-derivative test, or
    the second-derivative test --- and always sanity-check units and plausibility.
  \item The same condition, Fermat's zero slope, recurs across
    geometry, physics, economics, and machine learning.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\subsection{Optimization and Its Limits}
\label{ch17:what-unlocked}

\begin{connectionbox}
Optimization is one of the main reasons calculus is used in science, engineering and
business. Fermat's zero-slope condition and the closed-interval method reach far:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Physics}: light rays, planetary orbits, and
    hanging chains follow paths that make a physical quantity (travel time,
    action, or energy) stationary.
  \item \emph{Engineering design}: arches, drink cans, and
    machine parts can be designed to use the least material, have the least air
    resistance (drag), or be as stiff as possible within given limits.
  \item \emph{Economics}: a business chooses the production level that
    maximizes profit by setting marginal revenue equal to marginal cost
    ($R'(q) = C'(q)$, where $q$ is the quantity produced).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The bridge to Chapter~18: Indeterminate forms at the boundary.}
When we optimized the soda can and the distance to the parabola, our domain was open or
unbounded ($r \in (0,\infty)$ or $x \in (-\infty,\infty)$). To guarantee a minimum, we
had to examine what happens at the boundary by taking limits. In those simple examples,
individual terms like $710/r$ or $2\pi r^2$ blew up to infinity. But in richer
models, such as balancing air drag against fuel use to find the best cruising speed
of an aircraft, the boundary limits often pit competing
effects against each other, giving ambiguous expressions like $\tfrac{0}{0}$,
$\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}$, or $0 \cdot \infty$.

Ordinary arithmetic cannot evaluate these ``indeterminate forms.'' To know whether an
objective explodes, collapses to zero, or levels off at the boundary, we
need a tool that compares the \emph{rates} at which competing factors grow or
shrink. That tool is \textbf{L'H\^opital's Rule}, the subject of Chapter~18.

\textbf{Looking further: when algebra stalls and variables multiply.}
Two further hurdles await in applied optimization:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Equations you cannot solve by algebra}: In our examples, $f'(x)=0$
    factored into neat polynomials. In real engineering, setting $f'(x)=0$ often
    produces transcendental equations, which mix powers of $x$ with exponentials,
    logarithms or trigonometric functions (such as $x e^x = 5$ or $x = \tan x$), and
    cannot be solved by factoring. In Chapter~19, we build \textbf{Newton's Method},
    which uses tangent lines over and over to home in on solutions of $f'(x)=0$;
    near a solution it roughly doubles the number of correct digits at each step.
  \item \emph{Many variables}: Real systems can depend on dozens or
    billions of linked variables, with constraints that cannot be solved for
    one variable in terms of the others. In Chapter~33, we reach the many-variable
    tool for constrained optimization: \textbf{Lagrange multipliers}. Instead of
    eliminating variables, it uses the idea we met in the closest-point example:
    at the optimum, the level curves of the objective (curves along which it is
    constant) touch the constraint curve without crossing it, so both share the same
    perpendicular direction there.
\end{enumerate}
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: where can the maximum hide?]\label{ch17:ex-mental-checklist}
A continuous function $f$ is defined on the closed interval $[-2, 4]$. If $f'(x) = 0$
only at $x = 1$, what is the complete list of candidate points where the absolute
maximum of $f$ could possibly occur? If instead the domain were the open interval
$(-2, 4)$, could you still guarantee that an absolute maximum exists?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: the square-root shortcut]\label{ch17:ex-mental-squaring}
You are asked to find the point on a curve $y = g(x)$ closest to the point $(3, 1)$.
Explain in one sentence why minimizing $(x - 3)^2 + (g(x) - 1)^2$ produces the exact same
optimal $x$ as minimizing $\sqrt{(x - 3)^2 + (g(x) - 1)^2}$, and why this simplifies your
work.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Open-top box, rectangular sheet]
A box with an open top is made from a $16\text{ cm}\times 10\text{ cm}$ rectangle of
cardboard by cutting equal squares of side $x$ from the corners and folding up the
sides. (a) Write the volume $V(x)$. (b) State the domain of $x$. (c) Find the cut $x$
that maximizes the volume, and give the maximum volume. (d) Confirm with the
second-derivative test.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The cheapest open-top can]
An \emph{open-top} cylindrical can (no lid) must hold $1000\text{ cm}^3$. (a) Write
the surface area as a function of the radius $r$ alone. (b) Find the radius and height
that minimize the metal used. (c) Show that for the open-top can the optimal height
equals the radius, $h=r$ --- contrast this with $h=2r$ for the closed can, and explain
in words why losing the lid makes the optimal can relatively shorter.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Fence with a divider]
A farmer has $240$ m of fencing to build a rectangular pen \emph{and} an internal
fence parallel to one side that splits the pen into two equal halves. (a) Let $x$ be
the length of the sides parallel to the divider (so three segments of length $x$ are
needed: two sides and the divider) and $y$ the length of the other two sides. Write the constraint and the area objective. (b) Find the dimensions of
maximum total area. (c) How does the answer compare to the no-divider case?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Closest point on a line]
Find the point on the line $y = 2x + 1$ closest to the origin. (a) Minimize the
\emph{squared} distance and explain why that is legitimate. (b) Verify that the
segment from the origin to your point is perpendicular to the line, and explain
geometrically why it must be.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Norman window]
A ``Norman window'' is a rectangle topped by a semicircle whose diameter equals the
rectangle's width (Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-norman}). The total perimeter (the outer
edge: the bottom, the two sides and the curved arc, but not the dashed line between
rectangle and semicircle) is fixed at $P$ meters. Find the dimensions admitting the
most light (that is, the largest area). Express the optimal rectangle width in terms
of $P$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Maximum-area triangle]
Among all right triangles whose two legs (the sides meeting at the right angle) have
lengths $p$ and $q$ with $p+q=L$ for a fixed length $L$, find the one with the largest area. What is special about its shape? Prove your
answer is a maximum, not a minimum.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[An endpoint maximum]
Find the absolute maximum and absolute minimum of $f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 2$ on the
closed interval $[0,5]$ using the closed-interval method. Identify which extreme
occurs at a critical point and which (if any) at an endpoint.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Time, not distance: the lifeguard problem]
A lifeguard at point $A$ on a straight beach must reach a swimmer at point $B$ in the
water. She runs along the sand at speed $v_1$ and swims at the slower speed $v_2 <
v_1$. Setting up coordinates so the shoreline is the $x$-axis, $A=(0,a)$ with $a>0$, and
$B=(d, -h)$ with $h>0$, she enters the water at the point $(x,0)$ (the picture is
Figure~\ref{ch17:fig-refraction} with $b$ renamed $h$). (a) Write the total
travel \emph{time} $T(x)$. (b) Show that $T'(x)=0$ leads to the condition
\[
\frac{\sin\theta_1}{\sin\theta_2} = \frac{v_1}{v_2},
\]
where $\theta_1,\theta_2$ are
the angles the running and swimming paths make with the line perpendicular to the
shore. (This is \emph{Snell's law of refraction} --- light minimizes travel time the
same way.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why $h=2r$, in general]
For a closed cylindrical can of fixed volume $V$, minimize the surface area
\emph{symbolically} (keep $V$ as a letter) and prove that the optimal can always
satisfies $h = 2r$, independent of $V$. Then prove the critical point is a minimum
using the second derivative.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual: existence]
Give an example of a continuous function on an \emph{open} interval that has a
critical point which is not its absolute maximum, and explain why the closed-interval
method cannot be applied. Then state precisely what additional information would let
you conclude that an absolute maximum exists.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: substitution preserves the optimum]
Suppose you minimize $f(x)$ over a domain $D$, and $g$ is a strictly increasing
function defined on the range of $f$. Prove that $f$ and $g\circ f$ attain their
minimum at exactly the same points of $D$. (This is the theorem that justifies
minimizing $D^2$ instead of the distance $D$, or maximizing the logarithm of a
positive function instead of the function itself.)
\end{exercise}
