\chapter{What a Differential Equation Is}

Chapters~32 through~35 dealt with functions of several variables: partial derivatives, gradients, multiple integrals, and the integral theorems of Green, Stokes, and Gauss. The vector fields there did not change with time.

This chapter goes back to functions of a single variable, usually time, and asks how one quantity changes as time passes. Before you can describe how a whole field changes in time (temperature spread through a metal bar, for instance), you need to understand how a single quantity does. Vector fields still appear: a differential equation assigns a direction to every point of the plane, and its solutions are curves that follow those directions, like the streamlines of Chapter~35.

Every chapter until now has handed you a function and asked questions about it. Here is a curve; what is its slope? Here is a region; what is its area or volume? The function came first, and calculus was the tool for studying it. This chapter turns the question around, and the reversed question is the one calculus was first invented to answer.

In the real world the function almost never comes first. What comes first is a \emph{law} -- a statement about how a quantity is changing right now, expressed in terms of how much of that quantity there is right now. A population grows faster when there are more individuals to reproduce. A hot cup of coffee cools faster when it is further above room temperature. A falling stone speeds up at a rate fixed by gravity. In each case nature does not tell you the function $y(t)$ directly. Nature tells you something about $y$ and its rate of change $\dv{y}{t}$, tangled together in a single equation, and leaves you the job of untangling it -- of finding the actual function that obeys the law.

An equation that ties a function to its own derivatives is called a \emph{differential equation} (in plain terms: a rule relating what a quantity is doing right now to how fast it is changing). Solving one means finding the functions that satisfy the law. This is the central problem of physics, population biology, engineering and economics. Newton developed the derivative in large part to write down and solve the equations of motion of the planets.

This chapter explains what these equations are, what it means to solve one, why one law has a whole family of solutions, and how a single starting value picks out one of them. It also shows how to draw the solutions as curves before doing any algebra.

\section{A law about change}
\label{ch36:sec-law}

Start with the simplest possible example, built from a sentence rather than from symbols.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\emph{A quantity grows at a rate equal to its current size.} That sentence states a differential equation in words. Piece by piece: ``A quantity'' is some function $y(t)$ -- maybe a population, maybe a bank balance. ``Grows at a rate'' is its derivative $\dv{y}{t}$. ``Equal to its current size'' means that rate equals $y$ itself. Put the three pieces together and the sentence becomes
\[
  \dv{y}{t} = y.
\]
This is a differential equation. It does not tell you what $y$ is. It tells you a \emph{relationship} that the true $y$ -- whatever it turns out to be -- must satisfy at every instant.
\end{intuitionbox}

Look at what kind of object an equation like $\dv{y}{t} = y$ is. An ordinary algebraic equation, like $x^2 = y$, relates \emph{numbers}: plug in a number for $x$, get a number for $y$. A differential equation relates a \emph{function} to its own derivative. Its unknown is not a number but an entire function. When we ask for a solution, we are asking: \emph{which function, when you differentiate it, gives back a result that fits this equation?}

For $\dv{y}{t} = y$, we need a function that is its own derivative, and we already know one: the exponential, since $\dv{}{t}e^t = e^t$. So $y = e^t$ solves the equation. And it is not the only one: $y = 3e^t$ works too, because its derivative is $3e^t$, which equals the function itself. So does $y = -\tfrac{1}{2}e^t$, and $y = Ce^t$ for \emph{any} constant $C$. This infinite collection $y = Ce^t$ is called the \emph{general solution} (the full family of formulas that satisfy the differential equation, indexed by an arbitrary constant). When a specific constant is chosen, such as $y = 3e^t$, we call it a \emph{particular solution} (a single concrete curve). The pattern to remember: one law, many solutions.

\begin{definition}[Differential equation; ordinary and partial]
\label{ch36:def-de}
A \emph{differential equation} is an equation relating an unknown function to one or more of its derivatives. If the unknown function depends on a single independent variable -- so that only ordinary derivatives appear -- the equation is called an \emph{ordinary differential equation}, abbreviated \textbf{ODE}. (An ordinary derivative tracks how a quantity changes along a single parameter, like time.) If the unknown depends on several variables and partial derivatives appear, it is a \emph{partial differential equation}, abbreviated \textbf{PDE}. (A partial derivative measures the slope of a surface in one chosen direction while all other coordinates remain constant.) This chapter, and the three that follow, are about ODEs.
\end{definition}

The distinction in the definition matters. A PDE such as the heat equation
\[
  \pdv{u}{t} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}
\]
involves a function $u(x,t)$ of two variables -- here the temperature $u$ at position $x$ along a bar and time $t$ -- and is much harder; PDEs are a subject of their own. By restricting to a single independent variable -- usually time $t$ or position $x$ -- we keep the unknown a curve in the plane, something we can draw. That restriction is exactly what makes the geometric picture in this chapter possible.

\begin{historybox}
The phrase ``differential equation'' goes back to Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, who in a 1676 manuscript was already manipulating equations relating a variable quantity to its differential $\dd y$ (his notation for an infinitesimal change). But the subject took off with Isaac Newton. In the \emph{Principia} (1687) and his earlier work on fluxions (Newton's term for rates of change, imagining quantities flowing smoothly through time), Newton's key insight was that the laws of nature are most naturally stated as equations about \emph{rates}. His second law of motion, $F = m\,\frac{\dd^2 x}{\dd t^2}$, where $F$ is the force, $m$ the mass, and $\frac{\dd^2 x}{\dd t^2}$ the second derivative of position (the acceleration), is a differential equation for the position $x$. Newton solved it for a planet pulled by the Sun with a force inversely proportional to the square of their distance (an \emph{inverse-square} pull), and \emph{derived} Kepler's observation that planets move in ellipses. A law about how motion changes from moment to moment reproduced the whole orbit.

In the decades that followed, the Swiss brothers Jakob and Johann Bernoulli turned differential equations into an active field of investigation. They wrestled with physical curve puzzles, notably the \emph{catenary} (the U-shaped curve naturally assumed by a heavy hanging chain supported at both ends) and the celebrated 1696 \emph{brachistochrone} problem (from the Greek for ``shortest time,'' asking what curve allows a frictionless bead to slide between two points in the least possible time). Armed with early infinitesimal methods and ingenious substitutions, the Bernoullis, Leibniz, Huygens, and Newton solved these problems (the brachistochrone turned out to be an inverted cycloid), and in the next generation Leonhard Euler turned such tricks into a systematic theory.

Yet the era was plagued by controversy. A bitter priority feud raged between Newton and Leibniz over who first discovered calculus. A second, deeper debate arose over what counted as a ``solution.'' When an equation could not be solved in terms of elementary functions (familiar algebraic combinations of polynomials, roots, sines, and logarithms), was it truly solved? Euler recognized that differential equations themselves define brand-new transcendental functions that expand mathematics beyond elementary algebra.

These methods became the mathematical basis of classical physics: celestial navigation, waves, sound, and the engineering of the Industrial Revolution (steam engines, bridges, and pumps). What remains open today? Most nonlinear differential equations (a term defined in Section~\ref{ch36:sec-classify}) cannot be solved by explicit formulas. Even the motion of three bodies under gravity (the \emph{three-body problem}) was shown by Henri Poincar\'e in the 1880s to have no general solution by formulas, and to show \emph{chaos}: tiny shifts in the starting positions lead to completely different futures. And for the three-dimensional Navier--Stokes equations of Chapter~35, which govern fluid flow such as air turbulence and ocean currents, nobody knows whether smooth solutions always exist for all time or whether the velocity can become infinite at some point. A proof either way would win a million-dollar prize offered by the Clay Mathematics Institute.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The general shape}

Most of the equations we meet can be written so that the highest derivative sits alone on the left:
\[
  \dv{y}{t} = f(t, y).
\]
Read aloud, this says that the rate of change of $y$ is a specified function of the current time $t$ and the current value $y$. The right-hand side $f(t,y)$ is the \emph{law}: hand it where you are ($t$) and what you have ($y$), and it returns how fast $y$ is changing. Our first example $\dv{y}{t} = y$ is the case $f(t,y) = y$ -- the rate depends on $y$ but not directly on $t$. The slightly different equation $\dv{y}{t} = t$ has $f(t,y) = t$ -- the rate depends on the clock but not on the current value. Both are legitimate, and their solutions behave differently.

\begin{happeningbox}
A differential equation $\dv{y}{t} = f(t,y)$ is a \emph{rule for the slope of a curve, given the curve's location}. It requires that at any point $(t,y)$ in the plane, a solution curve must have slope $f(t,y)$. A solution is a curve that satisfies this slope condition everywhere. Solving the equation means finding a function whose graph matches these local slopes at every point. This geometric interpretation is formalized by the slope field in Section~\ref{ch36:sec-slopefield}.
\end{happeningbox}

\section{What it means to solve one}
\label{ch36:sec-solve}

Solving a differential equation is unlike solving the equations you are used to. When you solve $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$ you are hunting for \emph{numbers} -- here $x=2$ and $x=3$. When you solve a differential equation you are hunting for \emph{functions}. The answer is not a value but a formula, a whole curve.

\begin{definition}[Solution of an ODE]
\label{ch36:def-solution}
A \emph{solution} of the differential equation $\dv{y}{t} = f(t,y)$ on an interval $I$ is a function $y = \varphi(t)$, differentiable on $I$, such that substituting $\varphi$ for $y$ makes the equation true for every $t$ in $I$. That is, $\varphi'(t) = f\big(t, \varphi(t)\big)$ for every $t$ in $I$.
\end{definition}

(Here $\varphi$, the Greek letter phi, is a name for the candidate function, and an \emph{interval} $I$ is an unbroken stretch of the number line such as $0 < t < 5$.) \emph{Checking} a proposed solution is always mechanical, even when \emph{finding} it was difficult: differentiate it, substitute, and see whether the two sides agree. Here is that check on our running example.

\begin{example}[Verifying a solution]
\label{ch36:ex-verify}
Claim: $y = Ce^t$ solves $\dv{y}{t} = y$ for every constant $C$. To check, differentiate the candidate:
\[
  \dv{y}{t} = \frac{\dd}{\dd t}(Ce^t) = Ce^t.
\]
And the right-hand side of the equation is $y = Ce^t$. The two agree -- $Ce^t = Ce^t$ -- for every $t$ and every $C$. So the claim is verified. We have not \emph{derived} this solution; we have only confirmed it. But confirmation is genuine knowledge: we now know with certainty that every function of the form $Ce^t$ obeys the law.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A solution by direct integration]
\label{ch36:ex-integrate}
Consider another simple equation, $\dv{y}{t} = t$. Here the right-hand side depends only on $t$. An equation of this form, $\dv{y}{t} = g(t)$, is solved by integration, because it asks for a function whose derivative is $g(t)$---the standard antiderivative problem. So
\[
  y = \int t \,\dd t = \tfrac{1}{2}t^2 + C.
\]
Again a constant of integration appears, and again it is a \emph{family} of solutions, one for each value of $C$. Every parabola $\tfrac{1}{2}t^2 + C$ is a vertical shift of every other, and each one has slope exactly $t$ at every point, as the equation demands.
\end{example}

Both examples are \emph{first-order} equations: only the first derivative appears (Section~\ref{ch36:sec-classify} makes this precise). They show the general rule: \textbf{a first-order differential equation has a one-parameter family of solutions}, one solution for each value of a free constant $C$. The constant comes from the problem itself, not from the method we happened to use.

\begin{whybox}
A free constant appears because the differential equation specifies only the \emph{slope} of the solution curve, not its \emph{height}. For $\dv{y}{t} = t$, the curve must have slope $t$ at position $t$, but this condition does not determine the vertical placement. Any solution can be shifted vertically by a constant without changing its slope. This vertical freedom is the integration constant $C$. (For equations like $\dv{y}{t}=y$, where the slope also depends on $y$, the curves are not vertical shifts of one another, but there is still one free constant: the value the solution starts from.)
\end{whybox}

\section{Initial conditions: pinning down one curve}
\label{ch36:sec-ic}

An \emph{initial condition} is a single anchoring fact of the form ``at this time, the quantity had this value,'' written
\[
  y(t_0) = y_0.
\]
Here $t_0$ is the chosen time and $y_0$ the value at that time. The differential equation supplies the slopes; the initial condition supplies one point the curve must pass through. Together they typically determine exactly one solution. A differential equation packaged with an initial condition is called an \emph{initial value problem}, or IVP.

\begin{example}[An initial value problem]
\label{ch36:ex-ivp}
Solve
\[
  \dv{y}{t} = y, \qquad y(0) = 5.
\]
We already know the general solution is $y = Ce^t$. Now impose the initial condition: at $t=0$, we need $y = 5$. Substitute:
\[
  y(0) = Ce^{0} = C\cdot 1 = C = 5.
\]
So $C = 5$, and the unique solution to the initial value problem is
\[
  y = 5e^t.
\]
The family $Ce^t$ collapsed to a single curve once we said where it started. Geometrically, of all the exponential curves obeying the law, exactly one passes through the point $(0,5)$ -- and that is our answer.
\end{example}

\begin{focusbox}
Solving an initial value problem takes two steps, and they recur in every chapter to come:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Find the general solution} -- the whole family, with its free constant(s). This is the hard, creative step, the one that needs the techniques of later chapters.
  \item \textbf{Apply the initial condition(s)} to solve for the constant(s). This is the easy, mechanical step -- plug in the known point and do algebra.
\end{enumerate}
Mixing these up -- trying to satisfy the initial condition before you have the general family -- is the single most common source of confusion for beginners.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{trapbox}
A tempting error: thinking the initial condition has to be given ``at the beginning,'' at $t=0$. It does not. The condition $y(t_0)=y_0$ can anchor the curve at \emph{any} time $t_0$. ``Initial'' is a name, not a requirement. If a problem tells you $y(3) = 7$, you use that point exactly the same way -- substitute $t=3$, set the result equal to $7$, solve for the constant. The word ``initial'' is a historical leftover from physics, where the natural anchor is the state of a system when you start the clock.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If you are told that $\dv{y}{t} = y$ and $y(2) = 10$, what is the unique solution?
\emph{Mental check:} The general solution is $y(t) = Ce^t$. Setting $t=2$ gives $Ce^2 = 10$, so $C = 10e^{-2}$. The particular solution is therefore
\[
  y(t) = 10e^{-2}e^t = 10e^{t-2}.
\]
Check: at $t=2$, $e^{2-2} = 1$, so $y(2) = 10$.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Why we expect exactly one}

Why should a single initial condition select a single curve? Imagine you are a tiny walker dropped onto the plane at the starting point $(t_0, y_0)$. The differential equation is a compass: at your current location it tells you which direction to face -- it gives you a slope. You take an infinitesimal step in that direction. Now you are at a slightly new point; consult the compass again; it gives a new slope; step again. Marching forward (and backward) this way, you trace out a curve, and at every point of it your heading is exactly the slope the law prescribes -- so the curve is a solution. And the curve is \emph{forced}: at each instant you had no choice about which way to step. Start at the same point and you trace the same curve: one starting point, one path. Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-walker} draws this.

This informal argument is correct in spirit. It is the basis of a numerical method (Euler's method, mentioned in Chapter~14, which takes small but finite steps along tangent lines) and of a theorem (the existence and uniqueness theorem of Section~\ref{ch36:sec-exist}). But it assumes the compass is well-behaved -- that the prescribed slope does not, for example, change infinitely fast as you move up or down. Section~\ref{ch36:sec-exist} shows that when the law misbehaves in this way, two different paths can leave the same starting point.

\section{Order and linearity: classifying equations}
\label{ch36:sec-classify}

Differential equations are sorted in two main ways: by \emph{order} and by \emph{linearity}. These two properties tell you which solution methods apply.

\begin{definition}[Order]
\label{ch36:def-order}
The \emph{order} of a differential equation is the order of the highest derivative that appears in it.
\end{definition}

Thus $\dv{y}{t} = y$ is \emph{first order} -- only the first derivative shows up. Newton's law of motion $m\,\frac{\dd^2 x}{\dd t^2} = F$, involving the second derivative (acceleration), is \emph{second order}. The order tells you how much starting data you need: a first-order equation needs one initial condition to pin down a solution, a second-order equation needs \emph{two} (typically an initial position \emph{and} an initial velocity), and in general an $n$th-order equation carries $n$ free constants in its general solution and needs $n$ conditions to fix them.

\begin{connectionbox}
The rule ``order $n$ means $n$ free constants'' is the differential-equation echo of a fact you already know from integration. Each time you integrate, you collect one constant of integration. A second-order equation is, very loosely, ``two integrations deep,'' so it accumulates two constants; an $n$th-order equation, $n$ of them. This is exactly why pinning down a thrown ball's motion ($\frac{\dd^2 x}{\dd t^2} = -g$, second order) requires two facts -- where you threw it from and how fast -- not one. Integrating $-g$ once gives velocity with a constant; integrating again gives position with a second constant. Two integrations, two constants, two initial conditions.
\end{connectionbox}

The second property, linearity, is subtler but matters even more, because the theory splits along this line. Linear equations can usually be \emph{solved} by a formula built from familiar functions (a \emph{closed-form} solution). Nonlinear ones usually cannot; we study them \emph{qualitatively} (describing the shape and long-run behavior of solutions without a formula) or \emph{numerically} (computing approximate values step by step).

\begin{definition}[Linear ODE]
\label{ch36:def-linear}
A differential equation is \emph{linear} if the unknown function $y$ and \emph{all} of its derivatives appear only to the first power, are never multiplied together, and never sit inside another function such as $\sin$ or $\exp$. A first-order linear ODE can always be written in the standard form
\[
  \dv{y}{t} + p(t)\,y = q(t),
\]
where $p$ and $q$ are given functions of $t$ alone. If the unknown appears in any other way -- squared, as $y^2$; multiplied by its own derivative, as $y\,\dv{y}{t}$; or inside a function, as $\sin y$ or $e^{y}$ -- the equation is \emph{nonlinear}.
\end{definition}

Linearity is a condition on how the unknown $y$ enters, not on how $t$ enters: the coefficients $p(t)$ and $q(t)$ may be any functions of $t$, such as $t^2$ or $\cos t$. Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-classify} sorts the examples below by both properties.

\begin{example}[Sorting a handful of equations]
\label{ch36:ex-classify}
Classify a few equations to make the definitions concrete.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\dv{y}{t} = y$ \quad -- first order, linear ($p(t) = -1$, $q(t)=0$).
  \item $\dv{y}{t} = t^2 y + \cos t$ \quad -- first order, linear; rewrite as $\dv{y}{t} - t^2 y = \cos t$, so $p = -t^2$, $q = \cos t$. The coefficient functions $p(t)$ and $q(t)$ may be nonlinear in $t$.
  \item $\dv{y}{t} = y^2$ \quad -- first order, \emph{nonlinear}: $y$ appears squared.
  \item $\dv{y}{t} = \sin y$ \quad -- first order, \emph{nonlinear}: $y$ sits inside $\sin$.
  \item $\frac{\dd^2 x}{\dd t^2} + 3\dv{x}{t} + 2x = 0$ \quad -- second order, linear.
  \item $\frac{\dd^2 \theta}{\dd t^2} + \sin\theta = 0$ \quad -- second order, \emph{nonlinear} (the pendulum equation; $\sin\theta$ is the culprit).
\end{itemize}
\end{example}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Why is $\dv{y}{t} = t^4 y$ a linear equation, whereas $\dv{y}{t} = t y^4$ is nonlinear? The power $4$ appears in both.
\emph{Mental check:} Linearity cares strictly about the \emph{unknown function} $y$ and its derivatives, never about the independent variable $t$. In $\dv{y}{t} = t^4 y$, the unknown $y$ appears purely to the first power; the factor $t^4$ is a time-dependent coefficient $p(t) = -t^4$. In $\dv{y}{t} = t y^4$, the unknown itself is raised to the fourth power, which breaks linearity.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Linearity matters because of the \emph{superposition principle}. Call a linear equation \emph{homogeneous} if its right-hand side is zero ($q(t)=0$ in the standard form). If $y_1$ and $y_2$ both solve a linear homogeneous equation, then so does every \emph{linear combination} $c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$, where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are any constants. For example, $\dv{y}{t} + p(t)\,y = 0$ gives
\[
  \dv{}{t}(c_1y_1 + c_2y_2) + p(t)(c_1y_1 + c_2y_2) = c_1\cdot 0 + c_2 \cdot 0 = 0.
\]
So a few basic solutions can be combined to build all the others, and the theory of linear ODEs is built on this. For nonlinear equations it fails in general: the sum of two solutions is usually not a solution (Exercise~\ref{ch36:ex-superposition1}).
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{The slope field: seeing solutions before solving}
\label{ch36:sec-slopefield}

A first-order equation $\dv{y}{t} = f(t,y)$ assigns a \emph{slope to every point} of the plane. So \emph{draw} those slopes.

At a grid of points $(t,y)$, compute the number $f(t,y)$ and draw a short line segment through the point with that slope. Do this at many points and you fill the plane with a texture of tiny tilted dashes -- a \emph{slope field} (also called a direction field). Each dash is a local instruction: ``a solution passing through here must be heading this way.'' A solution curve is then any curve that flows along the dashes, tangent to the field at every point it touches, like a leaf carried by a current.

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{The geometric turn of Poincar\'e.}
The idea of reading an equation through its field of directions is older than it looks. The eighteenth-century analysts already thought of $\dv{y}{t}=f(t,y)$ as prescribing a direction at each point, but it was the qualitative theory of differential equations -- pioneered by Henri Poincar\'e in the 1880s in his memoirs on curves defined by differential equations -- that made the geometric viewpoint a discipline in its own right. Faced with equations that no algebraic formula could ever solve, Poincar\'e shifted the question from ``can we write down an exact formula for the solution?'' to ``what does the family of solutions \emph{look like}?'' -- where the curves go, which constant solutions attract their neighbors (\emph{stable}) and which repel them (\emph{unstable}), and what happens in the long run. The slope field is the first tool of that approach; it grows into the phase-plane pictures of Chapter~39.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Drawing a slope field requires no solving: you only evaluate $f(t,y)$ at a grid of points. Yet once the field is drawn, any curve that stays tangent to the dashes is a solution, and different starting heights give the different members of the family.
\end{intuitionbox}

Take the slope field for our very first equation, $\dv{y}{t} = y$. Here $f(t,y) = y$: the prescribed slope at a point is its height. Above the $t$-axis (where $y>0$) slopes are positive and steepen as you rise; below it (where $y<0$) slopes are negative and steepen as you fall; right on the axis ($y=0$) the slope is $0$, flat. Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-slopefield-exp} shows the result, with three solution curves $y = Ce^t$ threaded through it.

\begin{walkbox}
Look again at Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-slopefield-exp} and ask yourself: through the single point $(0, 0.4)$, how many solution curves pass? Trace the dashes by eye starting there. You should find that the curve is forced -- there is exactly one streamline through that point (the red one, $y=0.4e^t$), because at every step the field dictates your heading. This is the geometric face of uniqueness: a well-behaved slope field carries exactly one solution curve through each point. Now ask the harder question: can two \emph{different} solution curves ever cross each other? If they did, they would share a point -- and at that shared point the field prescribes a single slope, so both curves would have to leave it in the same direction, and the forced-walker argument says they would then be the same curve. So solution curves of a well-behaved first-order equation (Section~\ref{ch36:sec-exist} says exactly which ones) never cross. This is what uniqueness looks like in a picture.
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{Reading a field with a \texorpdfstring{$t$}{t}-dependent slope}

The previous field had slopes depending only on $y$, so the pattern looked the same at every horizontal position. Now take one where the clock matters too:
\[
  \dv{y}{t} = t - y.
\]
Now the prescribed slope at $(t,y)$ is $t - y$, which changes as you move both horizontally and vertically. Where $t = y$ -- along the diagonal line $y=t$ -- the slope is zero, so the dashes lie flat there. Above that diagonal ($y > t$) slopes are negative; below it ($y<t$) slopes are positive. So curves above the diagonal are pushed down and curves below it are pushed up. Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-slopefield-ty} shows the field with two solution curves.

When you read a slope field, you build a whole solution curve out of local slope information, by eye. Euler's method does the same with arithmetic, joining short straight steps into a broken-line approximation. So a slope field shows the content of the equation itself. For an equation you cannot solve with formulas, the field often tells you most of what you want to know: where solutions level off, which way they move, and where they end up.
\end{happeningbox}

\subsection{A field we cannot solve, but can read: the logistic law}
\label{ch36:sub-logistic-field}

For the first two fields we could spot or check a formula almost by inspection, so the slope field only confirmed what algebra already knew. The picture is most useful when we do not yet have the algebra. The standard example is the \emph{logistic equation}, a law of population growth that we will derive in Section~\ref{ch36:sec-model}:
\[
  \dv{P}{t} = k\,P\!\left(1 - \frac{P}{M}\right).
\]
Here $P(t)$ is the population at time $t$, $k>0$ is its growth rate when the population is small, and $M>0$ is the \emph{carrying capacity}: the largest population the environment (food, space) can support. The right-hand side is nonlinear (expanding it gives a $-kP^2/M$ term), and its explicit formula will have to wait until Chapter~37 develops separation of variables. We cannot solve it yet, but we can draw its slope field right now and read off how its solutions behave.

Read the law as a rule for slope. Two heights make the right side vanish: $P=0$ and $P=M$. At those two heights the prescribed slope is zero, so the dashes lie flat. The horizontal lines $P=0$ and $P=M$ are \emph{equilibrium} solutions: constant solutions, where the population stays put forever. Between them, for $0 < P < M$, both factors $P$ and $1-P/M$ are positive, so the slope is positive and every solution climbs. Above the carrying capacity, $P > M$, the factor $1-P/M$ turns negative, the slope turns negative, and solutions fall. Both regions drain toward the line $P=M$: it is an attractor, just like the line $y=t-1$ was for the earlier field. Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-logistic} draws it, taking $k=1$ and $M=3$.

\section{Existence and uniqueness, intuitively}
\label{ch36:sec-exist}

We have been assuming, on the strength of the walker picture, that an initial value problem has exactly one solution. This section says when that is true, and shows an example where it fails.

\begin{theorem}[Existence and Uniqueness, informal]
\label{ch36:thm-exist}
Consider the initial value problem
\[
  \dv{y}{t} = f(t,y), \qquad y(t_0) = y_0.
\]
Suppose $f$ is continuous in some rectangle $a<t<b$, $c<y<d$ of the plane containing the point $(t_0, y_0)$. Then at least one solution passes through that point, defined for $t$ in some interval around $t_0$ (\emph{existence}). If, in addition, the partial derivative $\pdv{f}{y}$ is continuous in that rectangle, then the solution is \emph{unique} -- exactly one solution curve passes through $(t_0,y_0)$.
\end{theorem}

The theorem has two halves, and they ask different things of the law $f$. Continuity of $f$ alone gives \emph{existence}: some solution gets going. To also guarantee \emph{uniqueness} -- that the walker's path is forced, with no branching -- you need the extra condition that $\pdv{f}{y}$ be continuous. (The full proof uses a slightly weaker condition: $f$ must be \emph{Lipschitz} in $y$, in the sense of Chapter~15, meaning a change in $y$ changes $f$ by at most a fixed multiple of that change. Continuity of $\pdv{f}{y}$ is a simpler condition that implies it.) Intuitively, $\pdv{f}{y}$ controls how fast the prescribed slope changes as you move up and down; keeping it under control prevents nearby curves from being able to split apart or merge.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Read the theorem as a guarantee about the walker. \emph{Existence} says: if the compass (the law $f$) points somewhere sensible at and near your starting spot, you can always start walking -- a path exists. \emph{Uniqueness} says: if the compass also turns \emph{smoothly} as you move (no sudden infinite swings in the prescribed slope), then your path is completely determined -- there is no fork in the road, no moment where the law leaves you a choice. Together: a well-behaved law plus a starting point gives one and only one future.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} Does Theorem~\ref{ch36:thm-exist} guarantee that a unique solution passes through $(0, 2)$ for the equation $\dv{y}{t} = 3y + \cos t$?
\emph{Mental check:} Here $f(t,y) = 3y + \cos t$, which is continuous everywhere. Its partial derivative with respect to $y$ is $\pdv{f}{y} = 3$, which is a constant and therefore continuous across the entire plane. Both hypotheses hold everywhere, so exactly one solution curve passes through $(0,2)$ -- and through any other point you choose.
\end{recoverybox}

This matters for science. If a physical process is described by a differential equation and its starting state, we expect exactly one outcome. The theorem confirms this whenever its conditions hold. The same theorem, with the same conditions, also holds for systems of several equations, where $y$ is replaced by a list of unknown functions; Chapter~39 relies on this.

\subsection{When uniqueness fails -- and why that is not a paradox}

The following example illustrates why the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{ch36:thm-exist} are necessary. Consider the problem
\[
  \dv{y}{t} = \sqrt{\abs{y}}, \qquad y(0) = 0.
\]
Here $f(t,y) = \sqrt{\abs{y}}$ is continuous everywhere, so existence is satisfied -- a solution exists. But watch what $\pdv{f}{y}$ does near $y=0$. For $y>0$, $f = \sqrt{y}$ and $\pdv{f}{y} = \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}$, which blows up to infinity as $y\to 0$. The uniqueness hypothesis fails at the starting point, and uniqueness itself fails with it:

\begin{example}[Two solutions from one start]
\label{ch36:ex-nonunique}
The constant function $y(t) = 0$ solves the problem: its derivative is $0$, and $\sqrt{\abs{0}} = 0$, so the equation holds, and $y(0)=0$. But the function
\[
  y(t) = \tfrac{1}{4}t^2 \quad (t \ge 0)
\]
\emph{also} solves it. Check: $\dv{y}{t} = \tfrac{1}{2}t$, while $\sqrt{\abs{y}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}t^2} = \tfrac{1}{2}t$ for $t\ge 0$. The two sides match, and $y(0)=0$. So \emph{two different} solution curves emerge from the single point $(0,0)$ -- the flat one that stays put, and the rising parabola. In fact there are infinitely many: the curve can linger at zero for any length of time $a\ge 0$ and then peel up into the shifted parabola $y=\tfrac14(t-a)^2$. Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-branch} shows a few of them fanning out of the origin. Uniqueness does not hold here.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
The lesson is that the hypotheses of the theorem are essential.
When $\pdv{f}{y}$ is continuous, uniqueness is guaranteed; when that condition fails (here, an infinite partial derivative at $y=0$), solutions can branch. Never assume uniqueness for a nonlinear equation without checking the hypothesis. The square-root law above also appears in practice. \emph{Torricelli's law} says water leaves a hole in the bottom of a tank at a rate proportional to the square root of the water depth $h$, so $\dv{h}{t} = -c\sqrt{h}$ for a constant $c>0$; run time backward and this is our equation, up to constants. Its non-uniqueness has a physical reading: staring at an empty tank, you cannot run the clock backward to tell when it finished draining, because tanks that emptied at different times all look identical afterward.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Why differential equations exist: modeling}
\label{ch36:sec-model}

So far differential equations have been mathematical objects. Their importance comes from the physical world: a \emph{modeling} problem (describing a real process with equations) almost always arrives as a statement about rates, and translating that statement into symbols produces a differential equation. Here are three classical models built from basic principles.

\subsection{Population growth}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{The sentence:} \emph{A population grows at a rate proportional to its current size.} The reasoning: twice as many individuals means twice as many births per year. Let $P(t)$ be the population. ``Grows at a rate'' is $\dv{P}{t}$; ``proportional to its current size'' means that rate is some constant $k$ times $P$. So
\[
  \dv{P}{t} = kP.
\]
This is our first equation $\dv{y}{t}=y$ with a rate constant $k$ added. Its solution is $P(t) = P_0 e^{kt}$, where $P_0 = P(0)$ is the starting population: \emph{exponential growth}, the same model that describes continuously compounded interest and nuclear chain reactions. The constant $k$ is the \emph{per-capita} growth rate (growth per individual: $k = \frac{1}{P}\dv{P}{t}$); a larger $k$ means faster growth.
\end{applicationbox}

Exponential growth cannot last: no real population grows without bound. The fix is to multiply by a factor $1 - P/M$ that is close to $1$ when $P$ is small and falls to $0$ as $P$ approaches the carrying capacity $M$. That gives the logistic equation $\dv{P}{t} = kP(1 - P/M)$ of Section~\ref{ch36:sub-logistic-field}, whose slope field showed every positive solution leveling off at $M$. A refined sentence (``growth slows as the population fills the available space'') produces a refined equation.

\subsection{Newton's law of cooling}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{The sentence:} \emph{An object cools at a rate proportional to the gap between its temperature and the surrounding temperature.} Let $T(t)$ be the object's temperature and $A$ the \emph{ambient} (surrounding) temperature, assumed constant. The ``gap'' is $T - A$. The rate of cooling is $\dv{T}{t}$, and ``proportional to the gap'' (with cooling, so the constant is negative) gives
\[
  \dv{T}{t} = -k\,(T - A), \qquad k > 0.
\]
When $T > A$ the right side is negative and the object cools; when $T < A$ it is positive and the object warms; when $T = A$ the rate is zero and the temperature holds. The solution is $T(t) = A + (T_0 - A)e^{-kt}$, where $T_0 = T(0)$ is the starting temperature. The gap $T-A$ shrinks exponentially, so the temperature approaches $A$ from above or below (Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-cooling}): the constant solution $T=A$ is an attractor, like the line $y=t-1$ in Figure~\ref{ch36:fig-slopefield-ty}. Forensic scientists use this equation to estimate time of death from body temperature.
\end{applicationbox}

\subsection{Motion under a force}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{The sentence:} \emph{Force equals mass times acceleration.} Acceleration is the second derivative of position, $\frac{\dd^2 x}{\dd t^2}$, so Newton's second law is already a second-order differential equation:
\[
  m\,\frac{\dd^2 x}{\dd t^2} = F.
\]
Here $x(t)$ is the position (height, for vertical motion), $m$ the mass and $F$ the force. For a stone in free fall the force is constant, $F = -mg$, where $g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ is the acceleration due to gravity, and the equation collapses to $\frac{\dd^2 x}{\dd t^2} = -g$. Integrate once for velocity, again for position, collecting two constants -- which the two initial conditions (starting height and starting velocity) fix. For a mass on a spring the force is $F = -kx$ (Hooke's law: the spring pulls back toward rest with a force proportional to the stretch $x$, with spring constant $k$), giving $m\,\frac{\dd^2 x}{\dd t^2} = -kx$, whose solutions are sine and cosine waves that oscillate forever. The same law, $F = ma$, with different forces, describes falling, orbiting, oscillating, and swinging.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Three different phenomena -- a breeding population, a cooling cup, a falling stone -- led to differential equations, and two of them led to the \emph{same} equation, $\dv{y}{t} = ky$, up to a shift ($y=T-A$ for cooling) and the sign of the constant. A handful of equation shapes recur across biology, physics, chemistry and finance, so learning to solve them once pays off many times. At the level of the differential equation, a population, a bank account and a radioactive sample follow the same law.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{labbox}
Before reading on, try translating each sentence into a differential equation yourself -- do not solve, just write the equation.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A radioactive sample decays at a rate proportional to the amount remaining.
  \item A skydiver's downward speed $v$ increases due to gravity but is opposed by air resistance proportional to the speed.
  \item A savings account grows by continuous interest at rate $r$, and money is also withdrawn at a steady $w$ dollars per year.
\end{enumerate}
Answers: (1) $\dv{N}{t} = -kN$, with $N$ the amount remaining and $k>0$; (2) $m\,\dv{v}{t} = mg - bv$, with $m$ the mass and $b>0$ the air-resistance constant; (3) $\dv{B}{t} = rB - w$, with $B$ the balance. Each is a sentence about rates, written in symbols. That translation from words to equation is the main modeling skill, and it mostly comes down to identifying ``rate of change of \underline{\hspace{1.5em}}'' with a derivative.
\end{labbox}

\section{Looking ahead}
\label{ch36:sec-ahead}

What remains is technique: methods for producing the general solutions that we have so far only verified or read off pictures.

The next three chapters supply those methods. Chapter~37, \emph{First-Order Differential Equations}, develops two methods for first-order equations: separation of variables, which solves $\dv{y}{t}=ky$ and the logistic equation, and the integrating factor, which solves first-order linear equations $\dv{y}{t}+p(t)y=q(t)$. Chapter~38 treats second-order linear equations, where superposition gives a complete theory; this is where springs, electrical circuits, and resonance are studied. Chapter~39 moves on to systems of equations, where several quantities change together and each one's rate depends on the others, and the slope-field pictures of this chapter grow into the phase-plane pictures used there.

\section*{Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: equations and their unknowns]
\label{ch36:ex-mental1}
For each equation, identify what kind of mathematical object a solution must be (a number, a coordinate point, or an entire function):
(a) $x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0$; \quad
(b) $\dv{y}{t} = 3y - t$; \quad
(c) $x^2 + y^2 = 25$; \quad
(d) $\frac{\dd^2 y}{\dd t^2} + 9y = 0$.
Which of these are ordinary differential equations?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: reading slope from the law]
\label{ch36:ex-mental2}
Without solving or finding an antiderivative, determine the slope of the solution curve to $\dv{y}{t} = 2t - y$ as it passes through the point $(t, y) = (1, 3)$. Is the solution curve rising, falling, or flat at that exact instant? Where in the $(t,y)$ plane would solution curves have horizontal tangents?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch36:ex-verify1}
Verify by substitution that $y = \sin(2t)$ is a solution of the second-order equation $\frac{\dd^2 y}{\dd t^2} + 4y = 0$. Then verify that $y = \cos(2t)$ is also a solution, and that $y = 3\sin(2t) - 5\cos(2t)$ is too. What property of the equation makes this last combination work?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch36:ex-classify1}
For each equation state its order, and say whether it is linear or nonlinear (give the reason for any nonlinearity):
(a) $\dv{y}{t} = 3y - 7$; \quad
(b) $\dv{y}{t} = y\cos t$; \quad
(c) $\dv{y}{t} = \cos(y)$; \quad
(d) $\frac{\dd^2 x}{\dd t^2} + x\,\dv{x}{t} + x = 0$; \quad
(e) $t^2\frac{\dd^2 y}{\dd t^2} - t\dv{y}{t} + y = e^t$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch36:ex-ivp1}
The general solution of $\dv{y}{t} = -2y$ is $y = Ce^{-2t}$. Find the particular solution satisfying $y(0) = 7$. Then find the one satisfying $y(1) = 7$ instead, and explain why the constant $C$ comes out differently in the two cases.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch36:ex-integrate1}
Solve the initial value problem $\dv{y}{t} = 6t^2 - 4$, $y(0) = 3$, by direct integration. (This equation's right-hand side depends only on $t$, so a single antiderivative does the job.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch36:ex-slopefield1}
Consider $\dv{y}{t} = -y/t$ for $t > 0$.
(a) Along which curves in the plane is the prescribed slope equal to zero? Where is it undefined?
(b) Verify that $y = C/t$ is a solution for every constant $C$.
(c) Sketch, by hand, the slope field at a few points and confirm that the curves $y = C/t$ (hyperbolas) flow tangent to it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch36:ex-attractor1}
For Newton's law of cooling $\dv{T}{t} = -k(T - A)$ with $k>0$:
(a) What constant function $T(t)$ is a solution? (This is the \emph{equilibrium} temperature.)
(b) Using the slope-field reasoning of Section~\ref{ch36:sec-slopefield}, argue without solving that every solution approaches $A$ as $t\to\infty$.
(c) Verify that $T(t) = A + (T_0 - A)e^{-kt}$ solves the equation and satisfies $T(0) = T_0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch36:ex-order1}
Explain, in your own words, why the general solution of an $n$th-order differential equation should contain $n$ arbitrary constants. Then state how many initial conditions are needed to determine a unique solution of (a) $\dv{y}{t} = y^2$, and (b) $m\frac{\dd^2 x}{\dd t^2} = -kx$, and say physically what those conditions represent in case (b).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch36:ex-nonunique1}
Consider $\dv{y}{t} = 3y^{2/3}$ with $y(0) = 0$.
(a) Verify that $y(t) = 0$ is a solution.
(b) Verify that $y(t) = t^3$ is also a solution.
(c) Compute $\pdv{f}{y}$ for $f(t,y) = 3y^{2/3}$ and explain, by reference to Theorem~\ref{ch36:thm-exist}, why the existence of two solutions does not contradict the uniqueness theorem.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch36:ex-model1}
Translate each sentence into a differential equation (do not solve):
(a) A tank holds $V$ liters of water; salt water flows in carrying salt at a constant $r$ grams per minute, and well-mixed water flows out carrying salt at a rate proportional to the current amount $S$ of salt.
(b) The velocity $v$ of a parachutist changes under gravity minus an air-resistance force proportional to $v^2$.
(c) A rumor spreads through a town of $N$ people at a rate proportional to the product of those who know it, $y$, and those who do not, $N - y$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch36:ex-logistic1}
The logistic equation is $\dv{P}{t} = kP\!\left(1 - \dfrac{P}{M}\right)$ with $k, M > 0$.
(a) Find the two constant (equilibrium) solutions by setting the right-hand side to zero.
(b) For $0 < P < M$, is $\dv{P}{t}$ positive or negative? What does this say about a population that starts below carrying capacity?
(c) Without solving, sketch the qualitative shape of a solution that starts at $P(0)$ slightly above zero, and explain why it has the famous ``S'' shape.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch36:ex-superposition1}
The equation $\dv{y}{t} = y^2$ is nonlinear. Show that $y_1 = -1/t$ is a solution (for $t>0$), but that $2y_1 = -2/t$ is \emph{not} a solution. Why does this not violate any principle? Contrast with what would happen for a linear homogeneous equation, and connect your answer to the discussion of superposition in Section~\ref{ch36:sec-classify}.
\end{exercise}
