\chapter{The Integral: Area and Accumulation}

For ten chapters we have used one operation: \emph{taking things
apart}. The derivative breaks a curve into its local slopes -- the slope here,
the slope there, the rate of change at one moment. We zoomed in until the
curve looked like a line and read off how steep that line was. That is calculus
going from a whole thing to its local behavior.

This chapter goes the other way. We are going to \emph{add things up}.
Given a rate that changes from moment to moment, we will reconstruct the total
that accumulated. Given a curve, we will find the area trapped underneath it.
The next chapter shows that these two activities, taking apart and adding up,
undo each other, the way subtraction undoes addition. Here we meet the integral
on its own terms, with no derivatives involved.

\begin{focusbox}
Two questions that look unrelated turn out to be the \emph{same} question:
(1) What is the area under a curve? (2) If I know the \emph{rate} at which
something accumulates at every instant, how much accumulates in total? This
chapter builds one object that answers both: the \textbf{definite integral},
the total accumulated across an interval, found by cutting the interval into
thin slices, adding up one rectangle per slice, and letting the slices get
thinner and thinner.
\end{focusbox}

The definite integral is written $\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$, and each part of
that symbol (the elongated S, the $a$ and $b$, the $\dd x$) records a step of
the slice-and-add construction. We will compute one integral directly from the
definition, with no shortcuts, so you can see what the definition asks for.

\chapterline

\section{Two ancient problems, one idea}
\label{ch20:two-problems}

\subsection{The area problem}

How much area is inside a circle? For a rectangle (length times width) or a
triangle (half base times height) the answer is immediate, because those shapes are
built from straight lines and area for straight-line shapes is elementary. But a
circle is bounded by a curve, and there is no finite recipe of multiplications
that gives its area. The number $\pi$ records the fact that
the area of a curved region is a new kind of quantity.

The Greeks found a way through, and we will follow it. Around
$250$ BCE, Archimedes computed the area of regions bounded by curves -- a
segment of a parabola, the circle -- by trapping the unknown area between shapes
he \emph{could} measure. Draw a polygon inside the circle with its corners on
the circle (an \emph{inscribed} polygon) and another outside it with its sides
touching the circle (a \emph{circumscribed} polygon). The true area is squeezed
between the two. Now add more sides. The inscribed polygon swells outward, the
circumscribed one shrinks inward, and the gap between them closes
(Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-archimedes}). The area of the circle is the single
number both sequences of polygon areas converge to.

\begin{historybox}
People tried to calculate areas under curves for over two thousand years, held back by puzzles about the infinite. Around 250~BCE, in his treatise \emph{Quadrature of the Parabola} (``quadrature'' was the classical term for finding the area of a curved region by matching it to a square), Archimedes calculated the exact area of a parabolic segment (the region between a parabola and a straight line cutting across it). Calling the area of the first inscribed triangle $T$, he packed the remaining gaps with an endless sequence of smaller triangles (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-parabola-triangles}) whose areas formed a geometric series -- a sum where each term is a fixed fraction (here $\tfrac{1}{4}$) of the previous one:
\[
  \text{Area} = T + \frac{1}{4}T + \frac{1}{16}T + \cdots = \frac{4}{3}T.
\]
Because ancient Greek mathematics rejected actual infinities, Archimedes avoided limits by using the \emph{method of exhaustion}: a double proof by contradiction showing the area could be neither greater nor less than $\tfrac{4}{3}T$, so that the polygons ``exhaust'' every possible gap between themselves and the curve.

Nearly two thousand years later, in \emph{Geometria indivisibilibus continuorum} (1635), the Italian mathematician Bonaventura Cavalieri pictured a flat shape as a stack of infinitely many parallel lines of zero thickness, which he called \emph{indivisibles} (like the pages bound in a book, or coins piled in a cylinder). Cavalieri showed that if two regions have cross-sections of equal length at every height, their areas are equal (\emph{Cavalieri's Principle}). Indivisibles caused a fierce controversy. The Jesuit mathematician Paul Guldin, and later the English philosopher Thomas Hobbes, attacked such methods as absurd: how could lines of zero width add up to a finite area, or infinitely many zeros add up to anything at all?

In October 1675, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz introduced the integral sign $\int$. Instead of zero-width lines, he summed infinitely many thin rectangles of height $y$ and \emph{infinitesimal} width $\dd x$ -- a width thought of as smaller than any positive number yet not zero. He chose the elongated letter ``S'' (for the Latin \emph{summa}, sum) and wrote $\int y\,\dd x$, so the notation itself says that an integral is a sum of thin rectangular strips.

The modern foundation came in the nineteenth century from Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Bernhard Riemann. In 1854, Riemann defined the integral as the limit of finite sums of rectangle areas as the rectangles get thinner -- the definition this chapter builds -- with no infinitesimals or sums of zeros needed. Riemann's integral has its own limits: it fails for wildly discontinuous functions, such as the function that equals $1$ at rational numbers and $0$ at irrational numbers. In 1902, Henri Lebesgue handled such functions with a new integral that groups inputs by their output values instead of by their position on the axis. Integration is still being extended today: physicists integrate over all possible paths a particle could take (the Feynman path integrals of quantum physics), and in many important cases mathematicians do not yet have a rigorous definition of these integrals.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{The accumulation problem}

Now a problem that seems to have nothing to do with area. A car's speedometer
reads $v(t)$ at every instant $t$. The speed is changing -- the driver
accelerates, brakes, coasts. \emph{How far did the car travel} between time
$t=a$ and time $t=b$?

If the speed were \emph{constant} at $60$ miles per hour for $2$ hours, distance
would be easy: $60 \times 2 = 120$ miles. Rate times time. But the speed is not
constant; it changes continuously, and ``rate times time'' has no obvious
meaning when the rate is a moving target.

The method is Archimedes' idea applied to motion.
Chop the trip into short time intervals. Over a
single hundredth of a second the speed barely changes, so on that sliver the
motion is \emph{almost} constant-speed, and distance is \emph{almost}
``speed at that moment times the length of the sliver.'' Add up the slivers. The
finer you chop, the better the approximation, and the true distance is the
number these sums converge to.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Both problems have the same skeleton. There is a quantity you cannot get
directly because something is \emph{curved} or \emph{varying}. So you cut the
domain into many small pieces, on each piece pretend the varying thing is
constant (which is nearly true when the piece is small), compute the easy
straight-line answer on each piece, add them all up, and then let the pieces
shrink toward zero width. The integral is the limit of that sum.
\end{intuitionbox}

Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-speed} draws the distance computation. On the time axis, lay out the
intervals. Above each one, draw a rectangle whose height is the speed during
that interval. The \emph{area} of one rectangle is (height)$\times$(width)
$=$ (speed)$\times$(time) $=$ distance covered in that interval. So the total
distance is the total \emph{area} of all the rectangles -- which is an
approximation to the area under the speed curve $v(t)$. The accumulation problem
\emph{is} the area problem. Distance traveled is the area under the speed graph.

\section{Riemann sums: making the idea precise}
\label{ch20:riemann}

Now we make the construction exact. We have a function $f$ on a
closed interval $[a,b]$ (the interval including both endpoints). For now,
picture $f$ as continuous and positive, so ``area under the graph'' is
unambiguous; we will drop those assumptions later.

\subsection{Partitioning the interval}

Cut $[a,b]$ into $n$ subintervals. Choose points
\[
  a = x_0 < x_1 < x_2 < \cdots < x_{n-1} < x_n = b.
\]
This list of cut points is called a \textbf{partition} $P$ of $[a,b]$, like
knife cuts dividing a loaf of bread. The $k$-th subinterval
is $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$, and its width is
\[
  \Delta x_k = x_k - x_{k-1}.
\]
The widths need not be equal, though most of the time we will make them equal:
if we use $n$ subintervals of the \emph{same} width, then each has width
\[
  \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}, \qquad x_k = a + k\,\Delta x.
\]
This even spacing is called a \textbf{regular} (or uniform) partition, and it is
what you should picture by default.

\subsection{Sampling a height on each piece}

On each subinterval $[x_{k-1}, x_k]$ we are going to replace the curve by a
single horizontal line, which makes a rectangle. But at what height? The curve
takes many values across the subinterval; we must pick one. Choose any point
$x_k^\ast \in [x_{k-1}, x_k]$, called
a \textbf{sample point}, and use the height $f(x_k^\ast)$ as the height of the
rectangle. Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-partition} labels all these pieces. The
rectangle over the $k$-th subinterval has
\[
  \text{area} = f(x_k^\ast)\,\Delta x_k.
\]
Add them up over all $n$ subintervals.

\begin{definition}[Riemann sum]
\label{ch20:def-riemann-sum}
Given a function $f$ on $[a,b]$, a partition $P$ with subintervals of width
$\Delta x_k$, and a choice of sample points $x_k^\ast \in [x_{k-1},x_k]$, the
corresponding \textbf{Riemann sum} is
\[
  S(P, \{x_k^\ast\}) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k^\ast)\,\Delta x_k.
\]
\end{definition}

The name $S(P, \{x_k^\ast\})$ records that the sum depends on two choices: the
partition $P$ and the list of sample points $\{x_k^\ast\}$. The sum is a single
number, a finite total of rectangle areas (where $f$ is negative, the
``area'' $f(x_k^\ast)\,\Delta x_k$ is negative; Section~\ref{ch20:signed-area}
deals with this). It is named for Bernhard Riemann, who in $1854$ gave the first
general definition of the integral built this way. Each Riemann sum is
an \emph{approximation} to the area we are after. To get the true area we will
take a limit. First we look at the three most common ways to choose the
sample points, because their pictures show what is going on.

\subsection{Left, right, and midpoint sums}
\label{ch20:lrm}

With a regular partition, three natural choices of $x_k^\ast$ stand out.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Left sum} $L_n$: take $x_k^\ast = x_{k-1}$, the left endpoint of
each subinterval. The rectangle's height is the value of $f$ at the left edge.
\item \textbf{Right sum} $R_n$: take $x_k^\ast = x_k$, the right endpoint.
\item \textbf{Midpoint sum} $M_n$: take $x_k^\ast = \tfrac{1}{2}(x_{k-1}+x_k)$,
the center of each subinterval.
\end{itemize}

Look at Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-lrm}. On a curve that is \emph{increasing}, the
left endpoint is the lowest point of $f$ on each subinterval, so every left
rectangle sits below the curve and $L_n$ \emph{undershoots} the true area.
Symmetrically, $R_n$ \emph{overshoots}. The true area is sandwiched:
$L_n \le \text{area} \le R_n$. (On a decreasing curve the roles swap.) That
sandwich is Archimedes' inscribed-and-circumscribed idea reborn: the left sum is
an inscribed staircase, the right sum a circumscribed one, and the area lives
between them.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} What happens if the curve is \emph{decreasing} instead of increasing? Picture it before reading on: every left rectangle now sticks up above the falling curve, and every right rectangle sits below it. The inequalities flip to $R_n \le \text{area} \le L_n$, and the true area is still trapped between the two staircases.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{labbox}
A small example, done entirely by hand. Take
$f(x) = x^2$ on $[0,1]$ with $n = 4$ equal subintervals, so $\Delta x =
\tfrac14$ and the partition points are $0, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac34, 1$.

\textbf{Left sum.} Heights at $0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34$:
\[
  L_4 = \tfrac14\!\left(0^2 + \tfrac1{16} + \tfrac14 + \tfrac9{16}\right)
      = \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac{14}{16} = \tfrac{14}{64} = 0.21875.
\]
\textbf{Right sum.} Heights at $\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34,1$:
\[
  R_4 = \tfrac14\!\left(\tfrac1{16} + \tfrac14 + \tfrac9{16} + 1\right)
      = \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac{30}{16} = \tfrac{30}{64} = 0.46875.
\]
\textbf{Midpoint sum.} Heights at $\tfrac18,\tfrac38,\tfrac58,\tfrac78$:
\[
  M_4 = \tfrac14\!\left(\tfrac1{64} + \tfrac9{64} + \tfrac{25}{64}
        + \tfrac{49}{64}\right) = \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac{84}{64} = 0.328125.
\]
The true area, as we will prove below, is exactly $\tfrac13 \approx 0.3333$.
So $L_4 < \tfrac13 < R_4$ as promised, and $M_4$ is by far the closest,
off by less than $0.006$. Four rectangles already do well.
\end{labbox}

\begin{trapbox}
A Riemann sum is \textbf{not} the integral. It is one finite approximation,
pinned to a particular partition and a particular set of sample points. Change
the sample points and you get a different number. The integral is what all these
numbers \emph{converge to} as the rectangles shrink. Students who write
``$\int_a^b f = \sum f(x_k)\Delta x$'' as if they were equal have skipped the
step that matters: the limit.
\end{trapbox}

\section{The definite integral as a limit}
\label{ch20:def-integral}

Now we take the limit. As we add more and more subintervals and let their widths
shrink toward zero, the staircase of rectangles hugs the curve more and more
tightly, and all the different Riemann sums -- left, right, midpoint, any sample
points at all -- get squeezed toward one common value
(Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-limit-progression}). That value is the integral.

We measure how fine a partition is by its \textbf{mesh} (also called its
\textbf{norm}), written $\norm{P}$: the width of its widest subinterval,
\[
  \norm{P} = \max_{1 \le k \le n} \Delta x_k,
\]
where $\max_{1 \le k \le n}$ means ``the largest of the values for
$k=1,2,\dots,n$''. If even the widest subinterval is thin, then every
subinterval is. So driving $\norm{P} \to 0$ forces \emph{every} subinterval to
shrink, not merely the average one. With that we can state the definition.

\begin{definition}[The definite integral]
\label{ch20:def-definite-integral}
A function $f$ is \textbf{integrable} on $[a,b]$ if there is a single number $I$
with the following property: the Riemann sums approach $I$ no matter how the
sample points are chosen, as the mesh goes to zero. Precisely (in the same
$\eps$--$\delta$ style as the definition of a limit in Chapter~6), for every
$\eps > 0$ there is a $\delta > 0$ such that for \emph{any} partition $P$ with
$\norm{P} < \delta$ and \emph{any} choice of sample points,
\[
  \abs[\Big]{\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k^\ast)\,\Delta x_k \;-\; I} < \eps.
\]
When this number $I$ exists we call it the \textbf{definite integral} of $f$
from $a$ to $b$ and write
\[
  \int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x \;=\; \lim_{\norm{P}\to 0}
      \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k^\ast)\,\Delta x_k \;=\; I.
\]
\end{definition}

That ``for any choice of sample points'' is what gives the definition its
strength. It demands that left sums, right sums, midpoint sums, and every
other choice all approach the \emph{same} limit. For a badly
behaved $f$ they might not, and then $f$ is not integrable. The basic fact
of the subject is that most familiar functions qualify:

\begin{theorem}[Integrability of continuous functions]
\label{ch20:thm-cont-integrable}
If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then $f$ is integrable on $[a,b]$. More
generally, $f$ is integrable if it is bounded (all its values on $[a,b]$ lie
between two fixed numbers) and has at most finitely many discontinuities.
\end{theorem}

Why continuity is enough: on a narrow subinterval, a continuous
function cannot vary much. On a closed interval $[a,b]$ there is a stronger
version of this, called \emph{uniform continuity}: for any allowed amount of
variation, one single slice width works everywhere on $[a,b]$, so that on
\emph{every} slice that narrow the function varies by less than that amount.
(We use this fact without proof here.) So once the slices are narrow enough, the gap
between the tallest and shortest possible rectangle on each piece is tiny
everywhere at once, which forces the left, right, and all other sums together
(the whybox below makes this precise).

\subsection{Decoding the notation}

Leibniz designed the symbol $\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$ in $1675$ to be read
literally.

\begin{happeningbox}
Each piece of the integral notation records part of the Riemann sum:
\begin{itemize}
\item In the expression $f(x)\,\dd x$, the factor $\dd x$ recalls the finite subinterval width $\Delta x_k$ as we pass to the limit $\Delta x_k \to 0$.
\item $f(x)$ is the height of the curve across that slice.
\item The product $f(x)\,\dd x$ represents the area of one approximating strip: height times width.
\item The sign $\int$ is the seventeenth-century long-$s$ initial of the Latin \emph{summa} (``sum''), chosen by Leibniz as the continuous counterpart to finite summation (for which Euler later introduced the Greek $\Sigma$ in $1755$).
\item The $\int_a^b$ says: accumulate these contributions as $x$ sweeps from $a$ to $b$.
\end{itemize}
Read it aloud the way Leibniz meant it: \emph{``sum, from $a$ to $b$, of height
times infinitesimal width.''} The symbol is an instruction to add up infinitely
many infinitely thin pieces, and Riemann sums followed by a limit are the
rigorous way to carry out that instruction.
\end{happeningbox}

A word on the $x$. It is a \textbf{dummy variable}: a placeholder whose name does
not affect the result, like the index $k$ in $\sum_{k=1}^n k$. The value of
$\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$ is a fixed number that does not depend on what letter you
used, so
\[
  \int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x = \int_a^b f(t)\,\dd t = \int_a^b f(u)\,\dd u.
\]

\begin{whybox}
Why does letting the rectangle width go to zero give the \emph{exact} area
rather than a better guess? Because of a squeeze. On any subinterval, the
true area under the curve lies between the shortest possible rectangle (height
$=$ the minimum of $f$ on that piece) and the tallest (height $=$ the maximum).
Adding the shortest rectangles gives the \textbf{lower sum} $\underline{S}_n$;
adding the tallest gives the \textbf{upper sum} $\overline{S}_n$
(Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-upper-lower}). The true area lies between them, and so
does every Riemann sum. On each piece the gap between the two rectangles is
(maximum of $f$ $-$ minimum of $f$ on that piece)$\times$(width of that piece).
As the mesh shrinks, continuity forces every such max-minus-min difference
toward zero, so the total gap $\overline{S}_n - \underline{S}_n$ shrinks to
zero. The true area is pinned between two quantities that are coming together,
so it must equal their shared limit. This is Archimedes' method of
exhaustion made rigorous.
\end{whybox}

\section{Computing an integral from the definition}
\label{ch20:from-def}

Before we have any shortcuts, we compute one integral the hard way, straight
from Definition~\ref{ch20:def-definite-integral}, to see that the limit
exists and can be computed. We will compute
\[
  \int_0^1 x^2 \, \dd x
\]
and find it equals $\tfrac13$, the number we approximated in the lab box above.

\begin{example}[$\int_0^1 x^2\,\dd x$ by brute force]
\label{ch20:ex-x2}
Use a regular partition into $n$ equal pieces, so $\Delta x = \tfrac1n$ and
$x_k = \tfrac{k}{n}$. Choose right endpoints as sample points, $x_k^\ast =
\tfrac{k}{n}$. The Riemann sum is
\[
  R_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^2 \cdot \frac{1}{n}
      = \frac{1}{n^3} \sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2.
\]
Now we need the sum of the first $n$ squares. It has a \emph{closed form}, an
exact formula that takes the same small number of steps to evaluate however
large $n$ is:
\[
  \sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}.
\]
(You can check it for small $n$: for $n=3$, $1+4+9=14=\tfrac{3\cdot4\cdot7}{6}$.
It is proved for all $n$ by \emph{induction}: check it for $n=1$, then show that
if it holds for some $n$ it also holds for $n+1$, since adding $(n+1)^2$ to
the right side gives the formula with $n+1$ in place of $n$.)
Substitute it in:
\begin{align*}
  R_n &= \frac{1}{n^3} \cdot \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} \\
      &= \frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}
      && \text{(cancel one factor of $n$)} \\
      &= \frac{2n^2 + 3n + 1}{6n^2}
      && \text{(multiply out the top)} \\
      &= \frac{1}{6}\left(2 + \frac{3}{n} + \frac{1}{n^2}\right)
      && \text{(divide top and bottom by $n^2$)}.
\end{align*}
Now let $n \to \infty$ (which drives the mesh $\tfrac1n \to 0$). The
$\tfrac{3}{n}$ and $\tfrac{1}{n^2}$ terms go to zero, leaving
\[
  \int_0^1 x^2 \,\dd x = \lim_{n\to\infty} R_n
      = \frac{1}{6}\cdot 2 = \frac{1}{3}.
\]
\end{example}

So the answer is $\tfrac13$, found with only a finite sum of squares and a
limit. The computation follows a pattern: a finite sum that grows with $n$, a
closed form for it, then a limit that removes every term except one.

\begin{labbox}
Repeat the computation with \emph{left} endpoints, $x_k^\ast = \tfrac{k-1}{n}$.
Using
\[
  \sum_{k=1}^n (k-1)^2 = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} j^2 = \frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{6}
  = \frac{2n^3 - 3n^2 + n}{6},
\]
you will find
\[
  L_n = \frac{1}{6}\left(2 - \frac{3}{n} + \frac{1}{n^2}\right).
\]
This value is \emph{below} $\tfrac13$ for every $n \ge 1$ (the left sum
undershoots a rising curve, as Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-lrm} showed), yet it still
has the same limit $\tfrac13$. Left and right sums disagree for every finite $n$
and agree in the limit, as the definition of integrability requires.
\end{labbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Every finite sum $R_n$ was slightly too big because the rectangles poked above the parabola, yet as $n\to\infty$ the excess $\tfrac{3}{6n} + \tfrac{1}{6n^2}$ went to zero, leaving the exact area $\tfrac13$. Finite approximations plus a limit gave an exact curved area using only algebra.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Signed area: when the curve dips below the axis}
\label{ch20:signed-area}

So far $f$ was positive and ``area under the curve'' meant exactly that. But the
definition of the integral never required $f \ge 0$. If $f(x_k^\ast)$ is
\emph{negative}, the term $f(x_k^\ast)\,\Delta x_k$ is negative -- it
\emph{subtracts} from the sum. So what does the integral compute when the curve
goes below the $x$-axis?

\begin{focusbox}
The definite integral computes \textbf{signed area}: area above the $x$-axis
counts as positive, area below counts as negative. The integral is the area
above minus the area below (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-signed}).
\end{focusbox}

This sign convention follows directly from the definition,
and it matches physical applications. Recall the car. If $v(t)$ is the car's
\emph{velocity} (a signed quantity -- positive going forward, negative in
reverse), then $\int_a^b v(t)\,\dd t$ is the \emph{displacement}: net change in
position. Time spent in reverse should subtract from how far you have gotten, and
the negative values of the \emph{integrand} (the function being integrated, here
$v$) make it do that. If instead you want total
distance traveled -- odometer mileage, which never decreases -- you integrate the
\emph{speed} $\abs{v(t)}$, which is never negative. Displacement and distance
differ only in this sign convention, and both are useful.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If a car drives east at $50\text{ mph}$ for an hour, then reverses west at $30\text{ mph}$ for an hour, what is its net displacement? The odometer integrates speed $\abs{v(t)}$: $50(1) + 30(1) = 80\text{ miles}$. Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-displacement} shows both as areas. The definite integral of $v$ tracks where you ended up; the integral of $\abs{v}$ tracks how far the tires rolled.
\end{recoverybox}

``Find the area between the curve and the axis'' and ``evaluate the integral''
are different requests when the curve crosses the axis. The integral gives signed

cancels itself: $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin x\,\dd x = 0$). For \emph{geometric} area --
total paint -- you must split at the crossing points and integrate $\abs{f}$, or
equivalently add up the absolute values of the pieces. Read the question
carefully.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Properties of the integral}
\label{ch20:properties}

Working from the definition every time would be slow. Instead, the
integral inherits a handful of properties from the Riemann sum;
each one is a fact about finite sums that survives the limit. Let $f$ and $g$ be
integrable on the relevant intervals and $c$ a constant. When the variable does
not matter we shorten $\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$ to $\int_a^b f$.

\begin{focusbox}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\arabic*)}]
\item \textbf{Linearity (sum).}\quad
  $\displaystyle \int_a^b \big(f(x)+g(x)\big)\,\dd x
   = \int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x + \int_a^b g(x)\,\dd x.$
\item \textbf{Linearity (constant multiple).}\quad
  $\displaystyle \int_a^b c\,f(x)\,\dd x = c\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x.$
\item \textbf{Additivity over intervals.}\quad For $a < c < b$,
  $\displaystyle \int_a^b f = \int_a^c f + \int_c^b f.$
\item \textbf{Orientation (reversing the limits).}\quad
  $\displaystyle \int_b^a f = -\int_a^b f,$ \quad and \quad
  $\displaystyle \int_a^a f = 0.$
\item \textbf{Monotonicity.}\quad If $f(x) \le g(x)$ on $[a,b]$, then
  $\displaystyle \int_a^b f \le \int_a^b g.$
\item \textbf{The constant.}\quad
  $\displaystyle \int_a^b c \,\dd x = c\,(b-a).$
\end{enumerate}
\end{focusbox}

Each of these follows from a statement about rectangles.

\textbf{Why linearity holds.} A Riemann sum for $f+g$ is
\[
  \sum_{k=1}^{n} \big(f(x_k^\ast)+g(x_k^\ast)\big)\Delta x_k
  = \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k^\ast)\Delta x_k + \sum_{k=1}^{n} g(x_k^\ast)\Delta x_k,
\]
because finite sums split across addition. The right side is
a Riemann sum for $f$ plus a Riemann sum for $g$, on the \emph{same} partition
and \emph{same} sample points. Take the limit of both sides; the limit of a sum
is the sum of the limits. Pulling a constant $c$ out works the same way:
$\sum c\,f(x_k^\ast)\Delta x_k = c\sum f(x_k^\ast)\Delta x_k$. Linearity is
the distributive law surviving a limit.

\textbf{Why additivity holds.} If the splitting point $c$ is one of your
partition points, then the rectangles over $[a,b]$ are literally the rectangles
over $[a,c]$ followed by the rectangles over $[c,b]$. The sum over the whole is
the sum over the left part plus the sum over the right part. Take the limit.
Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-additivity} illustrates the geometry: dividing the interval
at $c$ cuts the total region into two adjacent slices that fit together edge to
edge. This is the property that lets us handle a function defined piecewise, or
split at a sign change to compute geometric area.

\textbf{Why orientation flips the sign.} We built the integral with $a$ on the
left and $b$ on the right, so $\Delta x_k > 0$. If we agree to sweep the other
way, every $\Delta x_k$ becomes negative, flipping the sign of the whole sum.
Defining $\int_b^a f = -\int_a^b f$ makes the additivity rule
$\int_a^c + \int_c^b = \int_a^b$ hold for \emph{any} arrangement of $a,b,c$ --
even when $c$ is outside $[a,b]$. The convention is chosen to keep the algebra
consistent without splitting into cases, and the next chapter relies on it.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Monotonicity says: if one curve sits above another
everywhere, the area under it is at least as large
(Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-monotonicity}).
In rectangle terms, $f \le g$ makes every rectangle for $f$ no taller than the
corresponding rectangle for $g$, so the sums obey the same inequality, and the
inequality passes to the limit. Most estimates and bounds for integrals come
from this one fact.
\end{intuitionbox}

\end{axis}

\label{ch20:fig-monotonicity}

\begin{example}[Properties in action]
\label{ch20:ex-properties}
Suppose someone tells you $\int_0^1 x^2\,\dd x = \tfrac13$ (we proved it) and
$\int_0^1 x\,\dd x = \tfrac12$ (the area of a triangle of base and height $1$).
Then with no further integration:
\begin{align*}
  \int_0^1 \big(6x^2 - 4x + 5\big)\,\dd x
  &= 6\int_0^1 x^2\,\dd x - 4\int_0^1 x\,\dd x + \int_0^1 5\,\dd x
     && \text{(linearity)} \\
  &= 6\cdot\tfrac13 - 4\cdot\tfrac12 + 5\cdot 1
     && \text{(known values, constant rule)} \\
  &= 2 - 2 + 5 = 5.
\end{align*}
Linearity and the constant rule split the integrand into pieces whose
integrals we already know.
\end{example}

\section{The average value of a function}
\label{ch20:average}

The average of finitely many numbers is their sum divided by how
many there are. But what is the average \emph{height} of a curve over an interval
-- the average of \emph{infinitely many} values $f(x)$ as $x$ ranges over
$[a,b]$? You cannot divide ``the sum of infinitely many values'' by ``infinity.''
The integral gives a way around this.

Sample the curve at $n$ evenly spaced points and average those:
\[
  \text{average} \approx \frac{f(x_1)+f(x_2)+\cdots+f(x_n)}{n}.
\]
With $\Delta x = \tfrac{b-a}{n}$ we have $\tfrac1n =
\tfrac{\Delta x}{b-a}$, so
\[
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k)
  = \frac{1}{b-a}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k)\,\Delta x.
\]
The sum on the right is a Riemann sum for $f$. Let $n \to \infty$ and it becomes
an integral, giving us the definition.

\begin{definition}[Average value]
\label{ch20:def-average}
The \textbf{average value} of an integrable function $f$ over $[a,b]$ is
\[
  \bar f = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Rearrange it as $\bar f \cdot (b-a) = \int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$. The left side is the
area of a \emph{rectangle} of width $(b-a)$ and height $\bar f$. So the average
value is the height of the rectangle that has the \emph{same area} as the region
under the curve. If the region under the graph were water and you let it settle
flat over $[a,b]$, $\bar f$ would be the water line (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-average}).
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{example}[Average value of a parabola]
\label{ch20:ex-avg}
The average value of $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,1]$ is
\[
  \bar f = \frac{1}{1-0}\int_0^1 x^2\,\dd x = \frac{1}{1}\cdot\frac13 = \frac13.
\]
Sanity check: $f$ runs from $0$ up to $1$ on this interval, but it stays low for
most of it (it only climbs steeply near $x=1$), so the average should be well
below $\tfrac12$. The large values near the right end pull it above the
midpoint value $f(\tfrac12)=\tfrac14$, and it lands at $\tfrac13$.
\end{example}

\begin{connectionbox}
The average value is the bridge to the \emph{Mean Value Theorem for integrals},
which you will meet next chapter: if $f$ is continuous, it \emph{attains}
its average value at some point $c\in[a,b]$, that is, $f(c)=\bar f$. Geometrically,
the curve must cross its own leveling line somewhere. This is the integral's
version of the Mean Value Theorem you proved for derivatives, and the next
chapter uses it to prove the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Average values of functions appear in many fields. Household alternating
current (AC) has a voltage $V(t)$ that swings up and down many times a second.
Its quoted voltage is the \emph{root-mean-square} (RMS) value: the square root of
the average value of $V(t)^2$ over one cycle. That is why a ``$120$-volt'' outlet
has a peak voltage near $170$ volts. In probability, the long-run average outcome
of a random quantity (its \emph{expected value}) is also an integral, an average
in which each outcome is weighted by how likely it is. Whenever a continuously
varying quantity must be summarized by one representative number, the average
value -- an integral divided by a length -- is the standard way to do it.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Accumulation: the integral as a running total}
\label{ch20:accumulation}

The last idea of the chapter leads into the next one. We have treated $\int_a^b f$ as
a fixed number -- one area. Now let the top endpoint $b$ (called the \emph{upper
limit} of integration) move. Define a new function
\[
  A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,\dd t,
\]
the accumulated area from the fixed start $a$ out to a variable endpoint $x$.
(Note the dummy variable is now $t$, freeing $x$ to be the endpoint.) As $x$
slides right, $A(x)$ grows by sweeping in more area; this $A$ is the
\textbf{accumulation function}. It works like an odometer: for each endpoint $x$, it reports the total accumulated from the starting point $a$ up to $x$.

\begin{happeningbox}
Think of $f$ as a \emph{rate} and $A$ as the \emph{running total} that rate
builds up. If $f(t)$ is the rate at which water flows into a tank (liters per
second), then $A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,\dd t$ is the total liters that have flowed in
by time $x$. If $f(t)$ is a country's birth rate (people per year), $A$ is the
cumulative number of births. If $f$ is velocity, $A$ is displacement. The
integrand is always a \emph{rate of accumulation}; the integral is always the
\emph{amount accumulated}. This picture -- rate in, total out -- is the
central theme of integral calculus, and it is the reverse of the
derivative's picture (total in, rate out).
\end{happeningbox}

Now ask: \emph{how fast does the running total $A(x)$ grow?} When the endpoint
moves forward from $x$ to $x+\Delta x$, the area gains a thin sliver of width
$\Delta x$ whose height is close to $f(x)$ (Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-sliver}). So
\[
  A(x+\Delta x) - A(x) \;\approx\; f(x)\,\Delta x,
  \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad
  \frac{A(x+\Delta x) - A(x)}{\Delta x} \;\approx\; f(x).
\]
The left side is the difference quotient whose limit as $\Delta x \to 0$ is the
derivative $\dv{A}{x}$, and the approximation gets better as the sliver gets
thinner. This suggests that
\[
  \dv{A}{x} = f(x).
\]

The \emph{rate of accumulation of the total is the integrand itself}. The
derivative of the area function is the curve. You can see it in
Figure~\ref{ch20:fig-accumulation}: the running-total curve rises steeply
where the rate is large and flattens where the rate is small, so its
slope matches the height of $f$ point for point. So differentiation
undoes integration. That relationship, stated and proved exactly, is the
Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the subject of the next chapter. We
built the integral from sums and limits alone, and the derivative reappears as
the rate of change of accumulated area.

\begin{walkbox}
Take this on your walk. You have long known that distance is speed
times time -- when the speed is constant. The integral is the precise meaning of
``speed times time'' when the speed is \emph{not} constant: chop time fine, on
each sliver the speed is nearly constant so distance is nearly speed-times-sliver,
add up the slivers, take the limit. Now turn it around: if you only had a record
of total distance versus time, how would you recover the speed at an instant? You
would differentiate. So the speedometer and the odometer are derivative and
integral of one another. The Fundamental Theorem states this exactly, for
\emph{every} pair of rate and total.
\end{walkbox}

\section{Looking back, and looking ahead}
\label{ch20:summary}

We built the definite integral from the ground up. We took two ancient,
apparently separate problems -- the area under a curve and the total accumulated
from a varying rate -- and discovered they are the same problem. We solved it
Archimedes' way: cut the domain into thin pieces, approximate the varying thing
by a constant on each piece, sum the easy answers, and take a limit as the pieces
vanish. That limit, when it exists, is the Riemann integral
$\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$, and we proved by hand that $\int_0^1 x^2\,\dd x =
\tfrac13$ using only a sum of squares.

We saw that the integral computes \emph{signed} area, that it obeys
linearity, additivity, orientation, and monotonicity rules -- each one a fact
about finite sums that survives the limit -- and that dividing it by the interval
length gives the average value of a function. Finally, by letting the upper limit
roam, we built the accumulation function and glimpsed the equation
$\dv{A}{x}=f(x)$ that will, next chapter, unify the two halves of calculus into a
single theorem.

\begin{aestheticbox}
The derivative tore a curve down to its instantaneous slopes. The integral
rebuilds a total from its instantaneous rates. One takes apart, the other puts
together. Because each undoes the
other, one subject, calculus, can describe both how things vary and how
much accumulates. You have now met both halves separately; the next chapter
joins them.
\end{aestheticbox}

\subsection{The Scope of Accumulation and the Bridge Ahead}
\label{ch20:what-unlocked}

\begin{connectionbox}
The definite integral is the one tool behind every total built from a varying rate:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Totals from varying rates}: distance traveled under a varying velocity ($s = \int v(t)\,\dd t$), the work done by a force $F(x)$ that varies with position $x$ ($W = \int F(x)\,\dd x$; work is force times distance when the force is constant), and the mass of a rod whose density (mass per unit length) varies along it are all integrals.
  \item \emph{Net change}: because the integral keeps the sign of $f(x)$, inflows and outflows cancel, and velocity integrates to displacement.
  \item \emph{Areas of curved regions}: a region bounded by curves has a definite area, the limit of its Riemann sums.
\end{itemize}

But Riemann sums are better as a \emph{definition} than as a daily \emph{calculation tool}. To compute $\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$ directly from the definition,
\[
  \int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x = \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k^\ast)\,\Delta x,
\]
one needs closed-form formulas for the finite sums $\sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k^\ast)$. We know such formulas for low powers ($\sum k$, $\sum k^2$, $\sum k^3$), but for almost any other function, such as $\sin x$, $e^x$, $\sqrt{1-x^2}$ or $\ln x$, there is no simple formula for the sum.

The practical shortcut arrives in Chapter~21 (\emph{The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus}). Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz recognized that accumulation and differentiation undo each other:
\[
  \frac{\dd}{\dd x} \int_a^x f(t)\,\dd t = f(x).
\]
Instead of constructing rectangles and taking infinite limits of sums, computing $\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$ requires only finding an \emph{antiderivative} $F$ with $F'(x) = f(x)$, and evaluating $F(b) - F(a)$.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}
\label{ch20:exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental checks: signs, symmetry, and constants]
Answer each of the following in your head in ten seconds, using geometry and symmetry rather than algebraic calculation:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
\item What is $\int_{-3}^3 x^5\,\dd x$, and why?
\item What is $\int_2^7 4\,\dd x$? (Picture the rectangle.)
\item If $\int_0^5 f(x)\,\dd x = 12$ and $\int_0^3 f(x)\,\dd x = 8$, what is $\int_3^5 f(x)\,\dd x$?
\item Without computing, explain why $\int_0^{\pi} \sin x\,\dd x > 0$ while $\int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\,\dd x = 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Hand-computed Riemann sums]
For $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ on $[0,4]$ with $n=4$ equal subintervals
($\Delta x = 1$), compute the left sum $L_4$, the right sum $R_4$, and the
midpoint sum $M_4$. The exact value of $\int_0^4 \sqrt{x}\,\dd x$ is
$\tfrac{16}{3}\approx 5.333$. Which of your three sums is closest, and does
$L_4 < \tfrac{16}{3} < R_4$ hold? Explain in one sentence why, using the fact
that $\sqrt{x}$ is increasing.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[An integral from the definition]
Following Example~\ref{ch20:ex-x2}, compute $\int_0^2 x\,\dd x$ directly from the
definition using a regular partition and right endpoints. You will need
$\sum_{k=1}^n k = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$. Confirm that your answer equals the area of
the triangle you can see in the graph.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Sum of cubes]
Using $\sum_{k=1}^n k^3 = \left(\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2$, evaluate
$\int_0^1 x^3\,\dd x$ from the definition with right endpoints. Show every step
of the limit.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Using the properties]
Given $\int_1^4 f = 7$, $\int_1^4 g = -2$, and $\int_4^6 f = 5$, evaluate each of
the following or state that it cannot be determined:
(a) $\int_1^4 (3f - 2g)$;\quad
(b) $\int_1^6 f$;\quad
(c) $\int_6^4 f$;\quad
(d) $\int_1^4 f\cdot g$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Signed versus geometric area]
Consider $f(x) = x^2 - 1$ on $[0,2]$. (a) Sketch the graph and shade the regions
above and below the $x$-axis. (b) The signed area is $\int_0^2 (x^2-1)\,\dd x$;
using linearity, the known integral of $1$, and $\int_0^2 x^2\,\dd x = \tfrac{8}{3}$ (and $\int_0^1 x^2\,\dd x = \tfrac{1}{3}$), evaluate
it. (c) Now find the \emph{geometric} (total, unsigned) area by splitting at the
crossing point $x=1$ and adding absolute values. Explain why the two answers
differ.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Average value]
Find the average value of $f(x) = x^3$ on $[0,2]$ (you may use
$\int_0^2 x^3\,\dd x = 4$). Then find a point $c \in [0,2]$ where $f(c)$ equals
that average value, confirming the Mean Value Theorem for integrals in this case.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Accumulation reasoning]
Water flows into a tank at a rate $r(t) = 3 + 2t$ liters per minute, for $t$ from
$0$ to $5$ minutes. (a) Express the total volume that flows in as a definite
integral. (b) Without yet knowing antiderivatives, estimate it with a midpoint
sum using $5$ subintervals of width $1$. (c) The integrand is a straight line, so
the exact total is the area of a trapezoid -- compute it and compare to your
estimate.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A conceptual trap]
A student claims: ``Since $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin x\,\dd x = 0$, the function $\sin x$
encloses no area over $[0,2\pi]$.'' Explain precisely what is right and what is
wrong with this statement, and write the correct integral expression for the
total geometric area enclosed between $\sin x$ and the $x$-axis on $[0,2\pi]$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: monotonicity]
Prove the monotonicity property: if $f$ and $g$ are integrable on $[a,b]$ and
$f(x) \le g(x)$ for all $x \in [a,b]$, then $\int_a^b f \le \int_a^b g$.
\emph{Hint:} compare the two functions' Riemann sums on a common partition with
common sample points, term by term, then pass to the limit. Note where you use
$\Delta x_k > 0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: a bound from monotonicity]
Suppose $m \le f(x) \le M$ for all $x \in [a,b]$. Prove that
\[
  m\,(b-a) \;\le\; \int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x \;\le\; M\,(b-a).
\]
\emph{Hint:} apply the previous exercise twice, comparing $f$ to the constant
functions $m$ and $M$ and using $\int_a^b c\,\dd x = c(b-a)$. Then interpret the
result as a statement about the average value $\bar f$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Order of magnitude, no computation]
Without evaluating any integral, rank these three numbers from smallest to
largest and justify each comparison using monotonicity or a sketch:
$\int_0^1 x^2\,\dd x$,\quad $\int_0^1 x^3\,\dd x$,\quad $\int_0^1 \sqrt{x}\,\dd x$.
\emph{Hint:} on $(0,1)$, how do $x^3$, $x^2$, and $\sqrt{x}$ compare in size?
\end{exercise}
