\chapter{The Mean Value Theorem and What It Means}

In the last chapter, the tangent line gave us a local linear approximation
$f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$ near a single point. But what happens across an
entire interval? If a derivative stays positive between two points, does that
guarantee the curve keeps climbing? If two functions share the same derivative
everywhere, must their values stay locked at a fixed vertical separation?

To answer questions that span an interval, we need a bridge from the derivative back
to the original function: the \emph{Mean Value Theorem} (MVT). Here ``mean'' carries
its classical mathematical meaning: \emph{average}. The theorem says that if a
function has no breaks and no corners on an interval, then at some point inside the
interval its instantaneous rate of change equals its average rate of change over the
whole interval.

\section{The Picture Behind the Theorem}

Take a smooth curve $y=f(x)$ (one with no breaks and no sharp corners) over an
interval from $x=a$ to $x=b$, and draw the straight line connecting its two endpoints
$(a,f(a))$ and $(b,f(b))$. That line is the \emph{secant} line (from the Latin
\emph{secare}, ``to cut''). Its slope is the average rate of change of $f$ across the
interval:
\[
  \text{average slope} = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.
\]
Now imagine sliding a ruler parallel to that secant until it just touches the
curve. At that contact point, the slope of the tangent line equals the slope of the
secant line. Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-mvt} shows this.

\begin{intuitionbox}
If you drove $120$ miles in $2$ hours, your average speed was $60$ mph. At some
instant during the drive, your speedometer must have read exactly $60$ mph. If your
speed had stayed below $60$ the whole time, your average would be below $60$; if it
had stayed above, your average would be above. So your speed was at or below $60$ at
some moments and at or above $60$ at others, and a speed cannot get from one to the
other without passing through $60$. The Mean Value Theorem is the mathematical
version of this observation.
\end{intuitionbox}

The picture makes two assumptions. The curve has to be unbroken: if it
jumped, the ruler could miss it entirely. And it has to be smooth, with a
tangent line at every point inside the interval, or there could be a sharp corner
where the slope you want is never achieved. These two requirements will
become the hypotheses (the conditions that must hold) of the theorem, and we will
see why each one is needed.

\section{Rolle's theorem: the flat special case}

We first prove the special case where the two endpoints sit at the same height,
and then \emph{tilt} the picture to get the general statement.

\begin{intuitionbox}
If a smooth curve starts and ends at the same height, then somewhere in between it
has a horizontal tangent. Throw a ball; it leaves your hand and returns to the same
height; at the top of its arc, for one instant, it is moving neither up nor down.
That instant is what Rolle's theorem describes.
\end{intuitionbox}

Recall the interval notation: the \emph{closed}
interval $[a,b]$ includes its endpoints $a$ and $b$, and the \emph{open} interval
$(a,b)$ leaves them out. ``Differentiable on $(a,b)$'' means $f'(x)$ exists at every
$x$ strictly between $a$ and $b$; nothing is asked at the endpoints themselves.
Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-rolle} shows what the theorem says.

\begin{theorem}[Rolle, 1691]\label{ch15:thm-rolle}
Let $f$ be continuous on the closed interval $[a,b]$ and differentiable on the open
interval $(a,b)$, and suppose $f(a)=f(b)$. Then there exists at least one point
$c\in(a,b)$ with
\[
  f'(c)=0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Why it has to be true}

A continuous
function on a closed interval cannot shoot off to infinity or approach an
unattainable ceiling without reaching it; there are no missing points in the real
number line for the highest or lowest value to hide in. This is the \emph{Extreme
Value Theorem} from Chapter~8: every continuous function on a closed interval
$[a,b]$ attains an \emph{absolute maximum} (a largest value over the whole interval)
and an \emph{absolute minimum} (a smallest value).

In Rolle's theorem, the two endpoints sit at the same
height: $f(a)=f(b)$. If the function climbs above that height anywhere, its highest
point must lie strictly inside the interval. If it
dips below, its lowest point must lie inside. And if it does neither, the function
is flat, in which case every interior point has slope zero.

At an interior peak or trough, a smooth function must have derivative zero, because
at a peak the curve has stopped rising and has not yet begun to fall. Its
slope can be neither positive nor negative, so it is zero.

\begin{whybox}
The step that makes Rolle's theorem work is called
\emph{Fermat's theorem on interior extrema}. An \emph{extremum} (plural
\emph{extrema}) is a maximum or minimum. A \emph{local maximum} at $c$ means
$f(c)\ge f(x)$ for every $x$ close enough to $c$: a hilltop, even if somewhere far
away the graph goes higher. A local minimum is a valley bottom in the same sense. The
theorem: \textit{if $f$ has a local maximum or minimum at an interior
point $c$ and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c)=0$.}

The proof. Suppose $c$ is an interior peak (a local maximum).
For a small step forward $h>0$, the value cannot exceed the peak: $f(c+h)\le f(c)$.
The difference quotient is nonpositive, and so is its limit:
\[
  \frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h} \le 0
  \quad\text{so}\quad
  \lim_{h\to 0^+} \frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h} \le 0.
\]
For a small step backward $h<0$, $f(c+h)\le f(c)$ still holds, but now we divide by a
\emph{negative} denominator $h$, which flips the direction of the inequality:
\[
  \frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h} \ge 0
  \quad\text{so}\quad
  \lim_{h\to 0^-} \frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h} \ge 0.
\]
Because $f$ is differentiable at $c$, both one-sided limits must agree with the
single number $f'(c)$. That number is both $\le 0$ and $\ge 0$, so it is $0$.
Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-fermat} shows the two sides of the peak.
\end{whybox}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Rolle's theorem]
Since $f$ is continuous on the closed, bounded interval $[a,b]$, the Extreme Value
Theorem guarantees that $f$ attains an absolute maximum value $M$ and an absolute
minimum value $m$ on $[a,b]$.

\emph{Case 1: $M=m$.} Then the maximum and minimum values coincide, so $f$ is
constant on $[a,b]$. Its derivative is zero everywhere: $f'(x)=0$ for all $x\in(a,b)$,
and any interior point serves as $c$.

\emph{Case 2: $M>m$.} Because the two endpoints share the same height $f(a)=f(b)$,
the maximum $M$ and the minimum $m$ cannot both occur only at
the endpoints. At least one of them must be attained at some interior point
$c\in(a,b)$. At that interior point $c$, $f$ has a local extremum and is
differentiable, so by Fermat's theorem on interior extrema, $f'(c)=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Each hypothesis is needed.} Drop any single condition and Rolle's
theorem fails (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-rolle-fail}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Continuity on $[a,b]$.} Let $f(x)=x$ on $[0,1)$ and redefine $f(1)=0$.
Endpoints match ($f(0)=f(1)=0$), but the derivative is $1$ everywhere it exists, so
there is no horizontal tangent anywhere. The jump at the right end let the curve
get back down without turning around.
\item \emph{Differentiability on $(a,b)$.} Let $f(x)=\abs{x}$ on $[-1,1]$. Endpoints
match ($f(-1)=f(1)=1$), and $f$ is continuous everywhere. But the slope is $-1$ for
$x<0$ and $+1$ for $x>0$. The turning point at $x=0$ is a sharp corner, where the
derivative does not exist.
\item \emph{Equal endpoints.} Without $f(a)=f(b)$, a straight ramp $f(x)=x$ has
derivative $1$ everywhere and never levels off. This tilted case is what
the general Mean Value Theorem handles next.
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: can a sharp point cheat Rolle?}
Consider $f(x)=1-x^{2/3}$ on the interval $[-1,1]$.
Check the endpoints: $f(-1)=1-(-1)^{2/3}=0$ and $f(1)=1-1^{2/3}=0$.
The endpoints match, and the curve climbs to a peak $f(0)=1$. Does $f'(c)=0$
anywhere on $(-1,1)$?
Differentiating gives $f'(x)=-\frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}$. For $x\ne 0$, this is never zero;
as $x$ approaches $0$ the slope grows without bound (a \emph{cusp}, a sharp point where
the curve comes in vertically, as in Chapter~10). The function is unbroken,
yet never has a horizontal tangent because it is not differentiable at $x=0$
(Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-cusp}).
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Tilting Rolle into the Mean Value Theorem}

Rolle's theorem handles the level case where the secant line is flat. To
reach the general Mean Value Theorem, we
\emph{subtract off the secant line} to turn a tilted problem into a level one.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Imagine drawing the general MVT curve on a piece of paper, and then tilting your head
until the straight line between the endpoints appears horizontal. In that tilted
view, the curve starts and ends at the same height: it has become a Rolle
problem. Rolle guarantees a horizontal tangent in your tilted view, which is
a tangent parallel to the secant in the original view. Algebraically, tilting the
view means subtracting the secant's formula from $f(x)$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[Mean Value Theorem, Lagrange form]\label{ch15:thm-mvt}
Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$. Then there exists at
least one point $c\in(a,b)$ such that
\[
  f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.
\]
Equivalently, $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)\,(b-a)$ for some $c$ strictly between $a$ and $b$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The secant line through the endpoints $(a,f(a))$ and $(b,f(b))$ passes through
$(a,f(a))$ with slope $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$, so its equation (point plus slope
times run) is
\[
  L(x)=f(a)+\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\,(x-a).
\]
Because $L$ is a straight line, its derivative is its constant slope:
$L'(x)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ everywhere. Now define the \emph{gap function}
\[
  g(x)=f(x)-L(x),
\]
which measures the vertical signed distance from the secant line up to the curve
(positive where the curve is above the line, negative where it is below).
It has the properties Rolle's theorem needs:
\begin{itemize}
\item $g$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, because it is the
difference of the continuous and differentiable functions $f$ and $L$.
\item At the endpoints, the curve meets the secant line:
$g(a)=f(a)-L(a)=0$, and $g(b)=f(b)-L(b)=0$.
\end{itemize}
Therefore, $g$ satisfies every condition of Rolle's theorem on $[a,b]$. Rolle
guarantees that there is at least one point $c\in(a,b)$ where the gap has a
horizontal tangent: $g'(c)=0$. Differentiating $g(x)=f(x)-L(x)$ gives
\[
  g'(x)=f'(x)-L'(x)=f'(x)-\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.
\]
Setting $g'(c)=0$ gives
\[
  f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a},
\]
which completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{happeningbox}
The gap $g(x)=f(x)-L(x)$ starts at $0$, moves away from $0$ as the curve leaves the
secant, and comes back to $0$ at the other end. So it reaches a largest or smallest
value somewhere inside, and there $g'=0$. At that point the curve is running
\emph{parallel} to the secant: the curve and the line are heading in the same
direction, which is why the gap stops growing or shrinking for an instant.
Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-gap} draws the gap.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{historybox}
The Mean Value Theorem took several centuries to reach its modern form.

An early precursor appeared in 12th-century India, where the astronomer-mathematician
\textbf{Bh\=askara~II}, working on planetary motion, stated in effect that where a
varying quantity reaches its maximum its rate of change is zero, an early form of
the idea behind Rolle's theorem. Five
hundred years later, \textbf{Michel Rolle} (1652--1719), a French
mathematician, published his theorem in 1691 in \emph{M\'ethode pour r\'esoudre les
\'egalitez}. Rolle was a sharp critic of the new calculus of Isaac Newton and
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, denouncing their ``infinitesimals'' (quantities treated as
nonzero when dividing to find a slope, then discarded as zero at the end of a
calculation) as logical absurdities. Rolle proved his theorem only for
\emph{polynomials} (expressions with whole-number powers of $x$): between any two real
roots (inputs where a polynomial equals zero), there lies a root of its
derivative, which he computed by an algebraic rule. He never applied it to
non-algebraic curves. So the critic of calculus became famous for one of its basic
theorems.

In 1797, \textbf{Joseph-Louis Lagrange} (1736--1813) tried to give calculus a
foundation free of infinitesimals. In \emph{Th\'eorie des
fonctions analytiques}, Lagrange put the tilted Mean Value Theorem at the
center of his treatment, as the main tool for connecting
derivatives back to functions. He also tried to define all derivatives using
infinite sums of powers of $(x-a)$ (Taylor series, previewed in
Chapter~14) without limits, a program that could not work because some
smooth functions are not equal to their Taylor series.

The modern formulation arrived in 1823 with \textbf{Augustin-Louis Cauchy}
(1789--1857) at the \'Ecole Polytechnique in Paris. Cauchy introduced precise limit
definitions, proved the theorem (assuming, as was common then, that $f'$ is
continuous), and developed the two-function version that bears his name, the Cauchy
Mean Value Theorem. The proof given in this chapter, through Rolle's theorem and the
Extreme Value Theorem, became standard later in the 19th century. It depends on the
\emph{completeness of the real line} (the fact that the real number line has no
missing points or gaps), and \textbf{Karl Weierstrass} and others made that
foundation rigorous.

\textbf{What remains open and active today?} In one dimension, the MVT guarantees an
exact equality: $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$. But does this exact equality hold for
\emph{vector-valued functions} (curves in two or three dimensions, where outputs are
arrows or coordinates rather than single numbers)? No. Consider a particle
tracing a \emph{helix} (the shape of a spring or spiral staircase)
$\vec{r}(t)=(\cos t, \sin t, t)$ over $[0,2\pi]$. The net
change is purely vertical: $\vec{r}(2\pi)-\vec{r}(0)=(0,0,2\pi)$. The MVT would
need a time $c$ with $\vec{r}'(c)\cdot 2\pi=(0,0,2\pi)$, that is,
$\vec{r}'(c)=(0,0,1)$. But the velocity vector is
$\vec{r}'(t)=(-\sin t, \cos t, 1)$, and $\sin t$ and $\cos t$ are never both zero, so
the velocity is never straight up. Exact equality fails in higher dimensions. What
survives is the \emph{Mean Value Inequality}, for any vector-valued function
$\vec{f}$ of one variable:
\[
  \norm{\vec{f}(b)-\vec{f}(a)}\le \Bigl(\sup_{t\in(a,b)} \norm{\vec{f}'(t)}\Bigr)\,\abs{b-a},
\]
where $\norm{\cdot}$ is the length of a vector and $\sup$ (the supremum, from
Chapter~8) is the smallest upper bound of the speeds $\norm{\vec{f}'(t)}$: the net
distance covered is at most the top speed times the elapsed time. Extending mean
value theorems to functions with sharp corners, which appear constantly in
optimization and machine learning, and to curved spaces without ordinary coordinates
is still an active area of research.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Reading the theorem carefully}

The MVT is an \emph{existence} theorem. It promises that such a point $c$ exists; it
does not tell you where, and there may be several. (It proves that your car hit
$60$ mph at some moment, but not at which mile marker; for most functions, finding $c$
means solving an equation with no solution formula.) For most purposes this is
enough, because we will use it to say ``$f'$ at some unknown point equals
this average,'' and then use what we know about the size or sign of $f'$ to learn
about the average.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The two forms of the MVT.} Different problems call for different forms.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Slope form:} $\displaystyle f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. Read it as
``the average rate is hit exactly.''
\item \emph{Increment form:} $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$. Read it as ``the total change
equals some interior rate times the change in input.'' This is the form used most
often: it converts a statement about $f'$ into a statement about $f(b)-f(a)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}\label{ch15:ex-find-c}
Let $f(x)=x^3$ on $[0,2]$. The average slope is
$\frac{f(2)-f(0)}{2-0}=\frac{8-0}{2}=4$. The MVT promises a $c\in(0,2)$ with
$f'(c)=3c^2=4$, i.e.\ $c^2=\frac43$; keeping the positive root inside $(0,2)$ gives
$c=\frac{2}{\sqrt3}\approx 1.155$, which lies in $(0,2)$. We
\emph{found} $c$ here only because the function is simple; in applications we
almost never solve for $c$, and use only the fact that some such $c$ exists.
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Take $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ on $[1,4]$. Compute the average slope:
\[
  \frac{\sqrt4-\sqrt1}{4-1}=\frac{2-1}{3}=\frac13.
\]
Now set $f'(c)$ equal to it and solve:
\[
  \frac{1}{2\sqrt c}=\frac13
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2\sqrt c=3
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sqrt c=\frac32
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=\frac94=2.25.
\]
Check that $2.25$ sits in $(1,4)$. This is the point where the curve's slope equals
its average climb. Try the same with $f(x)=1/x$ on $[1,2]$ and confirm $c=\sqrt2$.
\end{labbox}

\section{Consequences of the Mean Value Theorem}

Everything in this section follows from the increment form
$f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$. The strategy is always the same: \emph{whatever you know
about $f'$ on the interval, you know about the unknown factor $f'(c)$, and the
equation passes that knowledge on to $f(b)-f(a)$.}

\subsection{Zero derivative means constant}

You expect that if a function never changes (its rate is always $0$) then it stays
put. But why, \emph{exactly}? ``The derivative is zero'' is a statement about
slopes; ``the function is constant'' is a statement about values. The MVT
connects the two.

\begin{theorem}[Vanishing derivative]\label{ch15:thm-const}
If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $f'(x)=0$ for all
$x\in(a,b)$, then $f$ is constant on $[a,b]$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Pick any point $x\in(a,b]$. Apply the MVT to $f$ on the subinterval $[a,x]$: there
is a $c$ between $a$ and $x$ with
\[
  f(x)-f(a)=f'(c)\,(x-a).
\]
But $f'(c)=0$ by hypothesis, so $f(x)-f(a)=0$, i.e.\ $f(x)=f(a)$. Since $x\in(a,b]$
was arbitrary, $f(x)=f(a)$ for every $x\in[a,b]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
Here the \emph{interval matters}. On a domain made of separate pieces with a gap
between them, the conclusion is false. The function
\[
  f(x)=\begin{cases}1,& x>0,\\ -1,& x<0,\end{cases}
\]
has $f'(x)=0$ everywhere it is defined, yet it is not constant. There is no
contradiction: the MVT needs a single interval connecting any two points, and there
is no such interval joining a negative $x$ to a positive one inside the domain. The
derivative tells you nothing about how $f$ behaves \emph{across a gap}.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: why does a gap break the bridge?}
Suppose $f(x)=2$ for $x\in(0,1)$ and $f(x)=7$ for $x\in(2,3)$. Differentiating
gives $f'(x)=0$ everywhere the function is defined. Yet $f(2.5)-f(0.5)=7-2=5\ne 0$.
Why didn't the Mean Value Theorem force $f(2.5)=f(0.5)$?
Because the theorem requires an unbroken interval containing both points. To apply
$f(2.5)-f(0.5)=f'(c)(2.5-0.5)$, the function must be defined and differentiable
at every point between $0.5$ and $2.5$. The gap on $[1,2]$ violates that requirement
(Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-gap-domain}).
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Two antiderivatives differ by a constant}

An \emph{antiderivative} of a function $f$ is a function $F$ whose derivative is
$f$, that is, $F'=f$. For example, $x^2$ is an antiderivative of $2x$, and so are
$x^2+5$ and $x^2-\pi$. When you meet integrals later in the book, the
\emph{indefinite integral} $\int f(x)\,dx$ will be written as $F(x)+C$, meaning
``one antiderivative plus any constant $C$''. The result below is why that
``$+\,C$'' captures every antiderivative: once you have found \emph{one}, you have
found them all. Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-family} shows what this looks like.

\begin{corollary}[Antiderivatives up to a constant]\label{ch15:cor-plusC}
If $F'(x)=G'(x)$ for all $x$ in an interval $I$, then $F(x)=G(x)+C$ on $I$ for some
constant $C$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $h=F-G$. Then $h'(x)=F'(x)-G'(x)=0$ for all $x\in I$. By the previous theorem,
$h$ is constant on $I$: $h(x)=C$. Hence $F(x)=G(x)+C$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The increasing/decreasing test}

Next we determine where functions increase and decrease.
We \emph{define} these words by values. A function $f$ is \emph{strictly increasing}
(or just \emph{increasing}) on an interval $I$ if $x_1<x_2$ forces $f(x_1)<f(x_2)$.
It is \emph{nondecreasing} if $x_1<x_2$ forces $f(x_1)\le f(x_2)$, so flat stretches are
allowed. \emph{Strictly decreasing} and \emph{nonincreasing} are defined the same way
with the inequalities reversed, and a function that is one of these on $I$ is called
\emph{monotonic} there. These definitions are about values, while the sign of the
derivative is about slopes; the MVT connects the two. Below, the \emph{interior} of $I$ means $I$
without its endpoints.

\begin{theorem}[Monotonicity test]\label{ch15:thm-mono}
Let $f$ be continuous on an interval $I$ and differentiable in its interior.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $f'(x)>0$ throughout the interior, then $f$ is strictly increasing on $I$.
\item If $f'(x)<0$ throughout, then $f$ is strictly decreasing on $I$.
\item If $f'(x)\ge 0$ throughout, then $f$ is nondecreasing; if $f'(x)\le 0$,
nonincreasing.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Take any two points $x_1<x_2$ in $I$. By the MVT on $[x_1,x_2]$ there is a $c$
between them with
\[
  f(x_2)-f(x_1)=f'(c)\,(x_2-x_1).
\]
The factor $(x_2-x_1)$ is positive. So the sign of $f(x_2)-f(x_1)$ is exactly the
sign of $f'(c)$. If $f'>0$ everywhere, then $f'(c)>0$, so $f(x_2)-f(x_1)>0$, i.e.
$f(x_2)>f(x_1)$: increasing. The other three cases are read off the same equation by
changing the sign or allowing equality.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why does the \emph{sign} of the derivative control the \emph{direction} of
the function?} Because of that single MVT equation. Without it, ``$f'>0$'' and
``$f$ increasing'' are two unrelated sentences: one about tangent lines, one
about a table of values. The MVT says the total change $f(x_2)-f(x_1)$ is built out
of an actual interior slope times a positive width. You cannot build a net
\emph{decrease} out of slopes that are all positive. The MVT turns local information
(the slope at each point) into global information (the rise across a whole
interval).
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{The converse is false.} The converse of ``$f'>0$ implies increasing'' would
be ``increasing implies $f'>0$'', and it fails: ``strictly increasing'' does
\emph{not} require $f'>0$ at every point. Consider $f(x)=x^3$. It is strictly
increasing on all of $\R$, yet $f'(0)=0$. The theorem's arrow points one way:
$f'>0\Rightarrow$ increasing, but increasing only gives $f'\ge 0$. A single flat
instant is allowed, as long as the function does not pause over a whole
subinterval. Many students ``prove'' a function is increasing by checking $f'\ge 0$
and then wrongly assert it must be \emph{strictly} increasing; that needs the extra
observation that $f'$ is not zero on any subinterval.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Error bounds: the MVT as a ruler}

In applied work, the MVT is most often used to \emph{bound} how much a function can
change. If you know a car's speedometer never
exceeds $70$ mph, you know that in two hours the car travelled
at most $140$ miles. That is the MVT expressed as an inequality.

\begin{corollary}[Change is bounded by the steepest slope]\label{ch15:cor-bound}
Suppose $f$ is differentiable on an interval and $\abs{f'(x)}\le M$ for all $x$
in that interval. Then for any two points $a,b$ in the interval,
\[
  \abs{f(b)-f(a)}\le M\,\abs{b-a}.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By the Mean Value Theorem, there exists some interior point $c$ strictly between $a$
and $b$ such that $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$. Taking absolute values on both sides gives
\[
  \abs{f(b)-f(a)} = \abs{f'(c)}\,\abs{b-a} \le M\,\abs{b-a},
\]
because the slope $\abs{f'(c)}$ cannot exceed the bound $M$ anywhere on the interval.
\end{proof}

A function with $\abs{f(b)-f(a)}\le M\abs{b-a}$ for all $a$ and $b$ is called
\emph{Lipschitz continuous} with constant $M$ (after the German mathematician Rudolf
Lipschitz): it never changes by more than $M$ units of output per unit of input. The
corollary says a derivative bounded by $M$ makes $f$ Lipschitz with constant $M$.
Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-cone} shows the geometry:
the curve stays within a cone opening from $(a,f(a))$ with slopes $\pm M$.
This inequality is used constantly in error analysis and numerical computation, and in
the theory that shows a differential equation with a given starting value has exactly
one solution.

\begin{example}[Bounding a value of sine]\label{ch15:ex-sin-bound}
How big can $\sin(0.1)$ be, without a calculator? Apply the bound to $f(x)=\sin x$ on
the interval $[0,0.1]$. We have $f'(x)=\cos x$, and $|\cos x|\le 1$ everywhere on the
real line, so we can take $M=1$. Then
\[
  \abs{\sin(0.1)-\sin(0)}\le 1\cdot\abs{0.1-0}=0.1.
\]
Because $\sin(0)=0$, this gives $\abs{\sin(0.1)}\le 0.1$. The same
reasoning on the interval between $0$ and $x$ proves $\abs{\sin x}\le \abs{x}$ for
every real $x$, using only the fact that $\abs{\cos c}\le 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A sharper estimate near a known value]\label{ch15:ex-sqrt-bound}
Estimate $\sqrt{4.1}$ and find guaranteed upper and lower bounds for it.
With $f(x)=\sqrt x$ on the interval $[4,4.1]$, the Mean Value Theorem gives
\[
  \sqrt{4.1}-\sqrt 4=f'(c)\,(0.1)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt c}\,(0.1)
  \quad\text{for some }c\in(4,4.1).
\]
The function $\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}$ decreases as $x$ grows, and $c$ lies strictly between
$4$ and $4.1$, so $\frac{1}{2\sqrt c}$ is squeezed between the values at the two ends:
\[
  \frac{1}{2\sqrt{4.1}} < \frac{1}{2\sqrt c} < \frac{1}{2\sqrt 4} = 0.25.
\]
The right-hand inequality gives the upper bound
$\sqrt{4.1} < 2 + 0.1\cdot 0.25 = 2.025$. Better still, we can get the lower bound
\emph{without} knowing $\sqrt{4.1}$ in advance by feeding $\sqrt{4.1}<2.025$ back into
the left-hand denominator:
\[
  \frac{1}{2\sqrt c} > \frac{1}{2\sqrt{4.1}} > \frac{1}{2(2.025)} = \frac{1}{4.05} > 0.2469,
\]
since $4.05\times 0.2469 = 0.999945 < 1$. Multiplying by $0.1$ and adding $2$ gives,
by hand,
\[
  2.02469 < \sqrt{4.1} < 2.025,
\]
an interval of width $0.00031$ that contains the true value.
\end{example}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Where slope bounds are used.}
In numerical analysis (the study of computing approximate answers), many guarantees of
the form ``this approximation is within $\eps$ of the true value'' are MVT estimates:
the error is written as a derivative at an unknown intermediate point, and then
bounded by the largest size that derivative can have on the interval.

In machine learning, training a model means adjusting its parameters to make an error
measure, the \emph{loss function}, small. \emph{Gradient descent} does this by
repeatedly taking a small step downhill, against the slope (the \emph{gradient} is the
many-variable version of the derivative). The \emph{step size} is the number that
multiplies the slope to set how far each step moves. If the slope itself changes at a rate of at
most $M$, then any step size smaller than $2/M$ is guaranteed to lower the loss rather
than overshoot the bottom. The proof rests on mean value estimates like
Corollary~\ref{ch15:cor-bound}.

In physics and engineering, if a motor can produce an acceleration of at most $M$,
then the velocity can change by at most $M\,\Delta t$ over a time window of length
$\Delta t$. Again the MVT turns a bound on a rate into a bound on the total change.
\end{applicationbox}

\subsection{Proving inequalities without a single graph}

The MVT turns many inequalities that look obvious from a graph into short
proofs. The recipe: build a function that is the difference of the two sides, show
its derivative has a fixed sign, and let monotonicity finish the job.

\begin{example}[$e^x \ge 1+x$ for all $x$]\label{ch15:ex-exp-ineq}
Let $g(x)=e^x-(1+x)$. Then $g(0)=0$ and $g'(x)=e^x-1$. Now $g'(x)>0$ for $x>0$ and
$g'(x)<0$ for $x<0$. By the monotonicity test, $g$ decreases up to $0$ and
increases after it, so $g$ takes its smallest value overall (its \emph{global
minimum}) at $x=0$, where $g(0)=0$. Hence
$g(x)\ge 0$ everywhere, that is, $e^x\ge 1+x$. So the tangent line $y=1+x$ lies below the
exponential curve (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-exp-ineq}), and we proved it from the sign of a
derivative alone.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[$\ln(1+x)<x$ for $x>0$]\label{ch15:ex-log-ineq}
Let $g(x)=x-\ln(1+x)$ on $[0,\infty)$. Then $g(0)=0$ and
$g'(x)=1-\frac{1}{1+x}=\frac{x}{1+x}>0$ for $x>0$. So $g$ is strictly increasing on
$[0,\infty)$, giving $g(x)>g(0)=0$, that is, $\ln(1+x)<x$.
\end{example}

\begin{walkbox}
A question to think about on a walk. The monotonicity test
turned ``slope is positive'' into ``function is increasing'' using the MVT. But the
MVT itself rested on Rolle, which rested on the Extreme Value Theorem, which rested
on the \emph{completeness} of the real numbers: the fact that there are no gaps in
the number line. When you say ``a positive derivative makes a function climb,''
you are, four layers down, relying on the completeness of $\R$
(Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-chain}).
Ask yourself why these theorems fail if you work only on the rational numbers $\Q$.
(Hint: on $\Q$, a continuous function on a closed interval can climb toward a peak
located at an irrational input like $\sqrt{2}$ and never attain a maximum.)
\end{walkbox}

\section{The Cauchy Mean Value Theorem}

The MVT has a two-function version that compares the changes in \emph{two}
functions over the same interval. You will need it in Chapter~18 for l'H\^opital's
rule, a method for computing limits of quotients. Its proof uses the same
helper-function idea as the proof of the MVT.

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem]\label{ch15:thm-cauchy}
Let $f$ and $g$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$. Then there
is a point $c\in(a,b)$ with
\[
  \bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)\,g'(c)=\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)\,f'(c).
\]
If $g'$ is never zero on $(a,b)$ (then $g(b)\ne g(a)$, since otherwise Rolle's
theorem would give a zero of $g'$), this rearranges to
\[
  \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}=\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of $\bigl(g(t),f(t)\bigr)$ as a point moving in the plane as a time-like
variable $t$ runs from $a$ to $b$: its horizontal coordinate is $g(t)$ and its vertical
coordinate is $f(t)$. A curve traced out this way, with both coordinates given as
functions of $t$, is called a \emph{parametrized} curve. At time $t$ the point moves
$g'(t)$ sideways and $f'(t)$ upward per unit of time, so its direction of travel has
slope $f'(t)/g'(t)$. Cauchy's theorem says that at some time $c$ this direction
matches the slope $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}$ of the straight chord from start to
end (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-cauchy}). It is the MVT for a parametrized curve.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{proof}
We use the same method that turned Rolle into the MVT: build a helper function with
equal values at the two endpoints, and apply Rolle to it. Define
\[
  h(x)=\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)\,g(x)-\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)\,f(x).
\]
This $h$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$. Compute the
endpoint values:
\begin{align*}
  h(a)&=\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)g(a)-\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)f(a)\\
      &=f(b)g(a)-f(a)g(a)-g(b)f(a)+g(a)f(a)\\
      &=f(b)g(a)-g(b)f(a),\\[4pt]
  h(b)&=\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)g(b)-\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)f(b)\\
      &=f(b)g(b)-f(a)g(b)-g(b)f(b)+g(a)f(b)\\
      &=f(b)g(a)-g(b)f(a).
\end{align*}
The cross terms cancel and $h(a)=h(b)$. So Rolle applies: there is $c\in(a,b)$ with
$h'(c)=0$. But
\[
  h'(x)=\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)g'(x)-\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)f'(x),
\]
and $h'(c)=0$ is the claimed identity.
\end{proof}

\begin{connectionbox}
The ordinary MVT is the special case $g(x)=x$: then $g'(c)=1$ and
$g(b)-g(a)=b-a$, and Cauchy's identity becomes
$f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$. So Cauchy's theorem contains the ordinary MVT, and
both come from Rolle. All three theorems use one idea, ``a function with equal values
at both ends has a flat spot inside'', applied to three different helper
functions. In l'H\^opital's rule (Chapter~18), the
quotient $\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}$ is what lets a ratio of functions be
replaced by a ratio of their derivatives.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{A glance ahead: the MVT seeds Taylor's theorem}

The increment form can be rewritten as $f(b)=f(a)+f'(c)(b-a)$: the value at $b$
equals the value at $a$ plus an exact correction that uses the first derivative at
one unknown point. Taylor's theorem (Chapter~31) is the same idea carried to higher
derivatives. Its next case says
\[
  f(b)=f(a)+f'(a)(b-a)+\tfrac12 f''(c)(b-a)^2
\]
for some $c$ between $a$ and $b$. Compare it with the linear approximation of
Chapter~14: the first two terms are the tangent line, and the last term is the
exact error, written with the second derivative $f''$ at an unknown point. Its proof
again builds a helper function that is zero at both ends and applies Rolle. The error
bounds for polynomial approximations later in the book come from this idea.

\begin{aestheticbox}
From one geometric fact, that the average slope over an interval is attained at
some interior point, we got: zero derivative means constant, the $+C$ in
antiderivatives, the monotonicity test, error bounds, proofs of inequalities,
Cauchy's version used for l'H\^opital's rule, and the error term in Taylor's theorem.
Whenever calculus turns information about a derivative into a fact about the
function's values, the Mean Value Theorem is usually the step that does it.
\end{aestheticbox}

\subsection{Where the Mean Value Theorem leads}
\label{ch15:what-unlocked}

\begin{connectionbox}
As we saw when reading Theorem~\ref{ch15:thm-mvt} carefully, we never need the exact
value of $c$, only
the fact that $c$ lies in the interval. If every slope in the interval is positive, then
$f'(c)$ is positive, which forces $f(b)>f(a)$. If every slope is at most $M$ in size,
then so is $f'(c)$, which keeps the graph inside the cone of
Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-cone}. In this way the theorem turns facts about rates at each
point into facts about change over a whole interval.

The next chapters use this step repeatedly:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In the next chapter, \emph{Maxima, Minima, and the Shape of a Graph}, the
  monotonicity test and a similar test for which way a curve bends become our main
  tools. From the signs of $f'$ and $f''$ we will locate peaks, valleys, and the
  points where the bending changes direction, and sketch whole graphs.
  \item Later, in Chapter~18 (\emph{L'H\^opital's Rule and Indeterminate Forms}), we
  will use Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem to evaluate limits of quotients where the top
  and bottom both approach $0$ (or both grow without bound), by comparing how fast the
  two functions change.
\end{itemize}
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental warm-up: quick checks]
Answer each question in a few words or a single sentence:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If a driver starts from rest ($0$ mph) and finishes at rest ($0$ mph) after a
$10$-mile trip, does Rolle's theorem guarantee an instant during the trip where their
acceleration was zero? Explain.
\item If $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x\in(0, 4)$, could $f(1)$ ever equal $f(3)$?
\item If $f'(x) = 0$ on the two-piece domain $(0, 1) \cup (2, 3)$, must $f(0.5)$
equal $f(2.5)$?
\item Can you apply the Mean Value Theorem to $f(x) = 1/x$ on $[-1, 2]$? Which
hypothesis fails?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Finding $c$]
For each function and interval, verify the MVT hypotheses and find every $c$ the
theorem promises.
(a) $f(x)=x^2-3x$ on $[1,4]$.\quad
(b) $f(x)=x+\frac1x$ on $[1,3]$.\quad
(c) $f(x)=\sin x$ on $[0,\pi]$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Rolle by hand]
Let $f(x)=x^3-3x$. Find two values $a<b$ with $f(a)=f(b)$, then locate the interior
point(s) where $f'=0$ guaranteed by Rolle's theorem. Sketch the graph and mark the
horizontal tangents.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Why a hypothesis is needed]
Give an explicit function on $[-1,1]$ that is continuous, has $f(-1)=f(1)$, but has
\emph{no} point where $f'=0$. Explain precisely which hypothesis of Rolle's theorem
your example violates, and where.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A speed argument]
A car travels $90$ miles in exactly $90$ minutes. Use the MVT to prove that at some
instant its instantaneous speed was exactly $60$ mph. Then prove the weaker claim
that at some instant its speed was \emph{at least} $60$ mph, using only the average,
without the MVT. Which statement is stronger, and why does the MVT earn the
stronger one?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Bounding with the derivative]
Suppose $f$ is differentiable on $\R$ with $f(1)=5$ and $\abs{f'(x)}\le 2$ for all
$x$. Find the smallest interval that must contain $f(4)$. (Use
Corollary~\ref{ch15:cor-bound}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Inequalities from monotonicity]
Prove each inequality by forming a difference $g$, signing $g'$, and using the
monotonicity test.
(a) $\tan x > x$ for $0<x<\frac\pi2$.\quad
(b) $\cos x > 1-\frac{x^2}{2}$ for $x\ne 0$ \emph{(hint: first show $\sin x < x$ for
$x>0$ by the same method; for $x<0$, use that both sides are even functions, unchanged
when $x$ is replaced by $-x$)}.\quad
(c) $\frac{x}{1+x}<\ln(1+x)$ for $x>0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Uniqueness of a solution]
Show that the equation $x^5+x-1=0$ has exactly one real solution. (Existence: use
the Intermediate Value Theorem on $[0,1]$. Uniqueness: if there were two solutions,
Rolle's theorem would force $f'=0$ somewhere between them; show that is
impossible.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Constant on an interval]
Suppose $f$ is differentiable on $\R$ with $f'(x)=3$ for all $x$. Prove, using the
MVT and not antidifferentiation rules, that $f(x)=3x+f(0)$ for every $x$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A fixed-point bound]
Let $g$ be differentiable on $\R$ with $\abs{g'(x)}\le \tfrac12$ for all $x$. Show
that for any two numbers $p,q$, $\abs{g(p)-g(q)}\le \tfrac12\abs{p-q}$. (Such a $g$
is called a \emph{contraction}, because it at least halves the distance between any
two inputs. This inequality is the main step in showing that iterating $g$, that is,
computing $x, g(x), g(g(x)),\dots$, converges to a single \emph{fixed point}, an input
with $g(x)=x$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: a derivative that is always positive]
Prove carefully, citing the MVT, that if $f'(x)>0$ for all $x$ in an open interval
$I$, then $f$ is one-to-one on $I$ (different inputs give different outputs). Where
exactly do you use that $I$ is a single interval with no gaps?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Cauchy MVT in action]
Let $f(x)=x^2$ and $g(x)=x^3$ on $[1,2]$. Find the point $c$ guaranteed by Cauchy's
Mean Value Theorem, that is, solve
\[
  \frac{f(2)-f(1)}{g(2)-g(1)}=\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}
\]
for $c\in(1,2)$. Confirm your $c$ lies in the interval.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual: the role of the EVT]
Explain in a short paragraph why Rolle's theorem needs the Extreme Value
Theorem, and why the Extreme Value Theorem in turn needs the completeness of $\R$.
Give an example of a formula that defines a continuous function on the rational
numbers in $[0,2]$ but does \emph{not} attain a maximum there, illustrating what
breaks without completeness.
\end{exercise}
