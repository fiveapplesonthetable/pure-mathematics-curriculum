\chapter{The Mean Value Theorem and What It Means}

In the last chapter, the tangent line gave us a local linear approximation
$f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$ near a single point. But what happens across an
entire interval? If a derivative stays positive between two points, does that
guarantee the curve keeps climbing? If two functions share the same derivative
everywhere, must their values stay locked at a fixed vertical separation?

To answer questions that span an interval, we need a bridge from the derivative back
to the original function: the \emph{Mean Value Theorem} (MVT). Here ``mean'' carries
its classical mathematical meaning: \emph{average}. The theorem guarantees that
somewhere on any smooth journey, your instantaneous rate of change matches your
average rate of change over the entire trip.

\section{The Picture Behind the Theorem}

Take a smooth curve and draw the straight line connecting its two endpoints. That
straight line cutting across the two endpoints is the \emph{secant} line (from the
Latin \emph{secare}, ``to cut''). Its slope measures the overall average rate of change
across the entire interval:
\[
  \text{average slope} = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.
\]
Now imagine sliding a straightedge parallel to that secant until it just touches the
curve. At that contact point, the instantaneous slope of the tangent line matches the
average slope of the secant line.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The Mean Value Theorem says that somewhere along a smooth arc, the instantaneous rate
of change equals the average rate of change. If you drove $120$ miles in $2$ hours,
your average speed was $60$ mph. At some instant during the journey, your speedometer
must have pointed to exactly $60$ mph. You could speed up to $75$, you could slow down
to $45$, but you cannot transition between speeds without crossing the average. That
everyday physical observation is the geometric core of the theorem.
\end{intuitionbox}

Notice what the picture quietly assumes. The curve has to be unbroken --- if it
jumped, the ruler could miss it entirely. And it has to be smooth, with a
well-defined tangent everywhere inside, or there could be a sharp corner where the
slope you want is never achieved. Hold onto those two requirements; they will
become the hypotheses of the theorem, and we will see exactly why each one is
needed.

\section{Rolle's theorem: the flat special case}

The cleanest way into the MVT is to first prove a special case so plain it almost
proves itself, and then \emph{tilt} the whole picture to get the general statement.
The special case is the one where the two endpoints sit at the same height.

\begin{intuitionbox}
If a smooth curve starts and ends at the same height, then somewhere in between it
has a horizontal tangent. Throw a ball; it leaves your hand and returns to the same
height; at the top of its arc, for one instant, it is moving neither up nor down.
That instant is Rolle's theorem.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[Rolle, 1691]\label{ch15:thm-rolle}
Let $f$ be continuous on the closed interval $[a,b]$ and differentiable on the open
interval $(a,b)$, and suppose $f(a)=f(b)$. Then there exists at least one point
$c\in(a,b)$ with
\[
  f'(c)=0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Why it has to be true}

Here is the idea before the proof. A continuous
function on a closed interval cannot shoot off to infinity or approach an
unattainable ceiling without reaching it; there are no missing points in the real
number line for the highest or lowest value to hide in. (This is the \emph{Extreme
Value Theorem}, which guarantees that every continuous function on a closed interval
$[a,b]$ attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum.)

Now, in Rolle's theorem, the two endpoints are tied together at the exact same
height: $f(a)=f(b)$. If the function climbs above that height anywhere, it must hit
a highest peak strictly inside the interval before returning to the baseline. If it
dips below, it must hit a lowest trough inside. And if it does neither, the function
is completely flat, in which case every interior point has slope zero.

At an interior peak or trough, a smooth function must have derivative zero. Why?
Because at a peak the curve has stopped rising and has not yet begun to fall. Its
slope can be neither positive nor negative. The only number that is neither positive
nor negative is zero.

\begin{whybox}
The hidden engine of Rolle's theorem is a fundamental observation sometimes called
\emph{Fermat's theorem on interior extrema} (where an \emph{extremum} simply means a
maximum or minimum): \textit{if $f$ has a local maximum or minimum at an interior
point $c$ and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c)=0$.}

Watch the mechanism squeeze the slope to zero. Suppose $c$ is an interior peak (a local maximum).
For a small step forward $h>0$, the value cannot exceed the peak: $f(c+h)\le f(c)$.
The difference quotient is nonpositive:
\[
  \frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h} \le 0 \implies \lim_{h\to 0^+} \frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h} \le 0.
\]
For a small step backward $h<0$, $f(c+h)\le f(c)$ still holds, but now we divide by a
\emph{negative} denominator $h$, which flips the direction of the inequality:
\[
  \frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h} \ge 0 \implies \lim_{h\to 0^-} \frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h} \ge 0.
\]
Because $f$ is differentiable at $c$, both one-sided limits must agree with the
single number $f'(c)$. That number is simultaneously $\le 0$ and $\ge 0$. The only
number that satisfies both inequalities is $0$. The two sides of the peak squeeze
the derivative to zero from opposite directions.
\end{whybox}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Rolle's theorem]
Since $f$ is continuous on the closed, bounded interval $[a,b]$, the Extreme Value
Theorem guarantees that $f$ attains an absolute maximum value $M$ and an absolute
minimum value $m$ on $[a,b]$.

\emph{Case 1: $M=m$.} Then the maximum and minimum values coincide, so $f$ is
constant on $[a,b]$. Its derivative is zero everywhere: $f'(x)=0$ for all $x\in(a,b)$,
and any interior point serves as $c$.

\emph{Case 2: $M>m$.} Because the two endpoints share the same height $f(a)=f(b)$,
it is impossible for both the maximum $M$ and the minimum $m$ to occur exclusively at
the endpoints. At least one of them must be attained at some interior point
$c\in(a,b)$. At that interior point $c$, $f$ has a local extremum and is
differentiable, so by Fermat's interior-extremum fact, $f'(c)=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Necessity of the hypotheses.} Drop any single condition and Rolle's
theorem immediately breaks:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Continuity on $[a,b]$.} Let $f(x)=x$ on $[0,1)$ and redefine $f(1)=0$.
Endpoints match ($f(0)=f(1)=0$), but the derivative is $1$ everywhere it exists ---
no horizontal tangent anywhere. The sudden jump at the boundary let the curve
escape without turning around.
\item \emph{Differentiability on $(a,b)$.} Let $f(x)=\abs{x}$ on $[-1,1]$. Endpoints
match ($f(-1)=f(1)=1$), and $f$ is continuous everywhere. But the slope is $-1$ for
$x<0$ and $+1$ for $x>0$. The turning point at $x=0$ is a sharp corner, where the
derivative does not exist. The peak is a spike, not a smooth crest.
\item \emph{Equal endpoints.} Without $f(a)=f(b)$, a straight ramp $f(x)=x$ has
derivative $1$ everywhere and never levels off. It is precisely this tilted case that
the general Mean Value Theorem handles next.
\end{itemize}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: can a corner cheat Rolle?}
Consider $f(x)=1-x^{2/3}$ on the interval $[-1,1]$.
Check the endpoints: $f(-1)=1-(-1)^{2/3}=0$ and $f(1)=1-(1)^{2/3}=0$.
The endpoints match, and the curve climbs to a peak $f(0)=1$. Does $f'(c)=0$
anywhere on $(-1,1)$?
Differentiating gives $f'(x)=-\frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}$. For $x\ne 0$, this is never zero;
at $x=0$, the slope shoots to infinity (a vertical cusp). The function is unbroken,
yet never has a horizontal tangent because it lacks differentiability at $x=0$.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Tilting Rolle into the Mean Value Theorem}

Rolle's theorem handles the level case where the secant line is completely flat. To
reach the general Mean Value Theorem, we use one of the most powerful moves in all of
analysis: we \emph{subtract off the secant line} to turn a tilted problem into a
level one.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Imagine drawing the general MVT curve on a piece of paper, and then tilting your head
until the straight line between the endpoints appears horizontal. In that tilted
view, the curve starts and ends at the exact same height --- it has become a Rolle
problem! Rolle guarantees a horizontal tangent in your tilted frame, which translates
to a tangent parallel to the secant in the original frame. Algebraically, tilting the
frame means subtracting the secant formula from $f(x)$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[Mean Value Theorem, Lagrange form]\label{ch15:thm-mvt}
Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$. Then there exists at
least one point $c\in(a,b)$ such that
\[
  f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.
\]
Equivalently, $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)\,(b-a)$ for some $c$ strictly between $a$ and $b$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The secant line through the endpoints $(a,f(a))$ and $(b,f(b))$ is given by the
point-slope equation
\[
  L(x)=f(a)+\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\,(x-a).
\]
Because $L$ is a straight line, its derivative is simply its constant slope:
$L'(x)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ everywhere. Now define the \emph{gap function}
\[
  g(x)=f(x)-L(x),
\]
which measures the vertical signed distance from the curve to its secant line.
Notice how neatly $g$ inherits the necessary properties:
\begin{itemize}
\item $g$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, because it is the
difference of the continuous and differentiable functions $f$ and $L$.
\item At the endpoints, the curve meets the secant line:
$g(a)=f(a)-L(a)=0$, and $g(b)=f(b)-L(b)=0$.
\end{itemize}
Therefore, $g$ satisfies every condition of Rolle's theorem on $[a,b]$. Rolle
guarantees that there is at least one point $c\in(a,b)$ where the gap has a
horizontal tangent: $g'(c)=0$. Differentiating $g(x)=f(x)-L(x)$ gives
\[
  g'(x)=f'(x)-L'(x)=f'(x)-\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.
\]
Setting $g'(c)=0$ immediately yields
\[
  f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a},
\]
completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{happeningbox}
What is $g(x)=f(x)-L(x)$ really doing? It is the vertical gap between the curve and
the straight line joining its endpoints. The proof reveals something fundamental: the
place where this gap is widest or narrowest --- where $g'=0$ --- is the exact place
where the curve is momentarily running \emph{parallel} to the secant. As you move
along the curve away from the secant line and return to it at the other end, the gap
must reach a maximum or minimum, and at that turning point the curve and the secant
are heading in the exact same direction.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{historybox}
The story of the Mean Value Theorem reflects a century-long struggle to transform
calculus from an intuitive art into a rigorous mathematical science.

An early precursor appeared in 12th-century India, where the astronomer-mathematician
\textbf{Bh\=askara~II} observed that a planet's instantaneous daily motion must equal
its average motion over an interval containing its fastest and slowest points. Five
hundred years later, \textbf{Michel Rolle} (1652--1719), a conservative French
mathematician, published his theorem in 1691 in \emph{M\'ethode pour r\'esoudre les
\'egalitez}. Rolle was an aggressive critic of the new calculus of Isaac Newton and
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, denouncing their ``infinitesimals''---quantities treated as
nonzero when dividing to find a slope, then discarded as zero at the end of a
calculation---as logical absurdities. Rolle proved his theorem purely for
\emph{polynomials} (expressions with whole-number powers of $x$): between any two real
roots (inputs where a polynomial equals zero), there lies a root of its formal
algebraic derivative. He never envisioned his theorem applying to continuous,
non-algebraic curves. By historical irony, the critic who sought to dismantle calculus
became famous for one of its foundational stones.

In 1797, \textbf{Joseph-Louis Lagrange} (1736--1813) recognized that calculus needed an
unshakable foundation free of vanishing infinitesimals. In \emph{Th\'eorie des
fonctions analytiques}, Lagrange placed the tilted Mean Value Theorem at the very
center of differential analysis, calling it the primary tool for connecting
derivatives back to functions. Lagrange attempted to define all derivatives using
infinite series without limits, a program that was ultimately doomed because not every
smooth function equals its Taylor series.

The modern formulation arrived in 1823 with \textbf{Augustin-Louis Cauchy}
(1789--1857) at the \'Ecole Polytechnique in Paris. Cauchy introduced precise limit
definitions and recognized that proving Rolle's theorem requires the Extreme Value
Theorem, which in turn depends on the \emph{completeness of the real line} (the fact
that the real number line has no missing points or gaps). Cauchy also developed a
general two-function extension, the Cauchy Mean Value Theorem. Later in the 19th
century, \textbf{Karl Weierstrass} completed the rigorous framework that made every
step of these proofs airtight.

\textbf{What remains open and active today?} In one dimension, the MVT guarantees an
exact equality: $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$. But does this exact equality hold for
\emph{vector-valued functions} (curves in two or three dimensions, where outputs are
arrows or coordinates rather than single numbers)? No! Consider a particle
tracing a helical spiral $\vec{r}(t)=(\cos t, \sin t, t)$ over $[0,2\pi]$. The net
change is purely vertical: $\vec{r}(2\pi)-\vec{r}(0)=(0,0,2\pi)$. Yet the velocity
vector is $\vec{r}'(t)=(-\sin t, \cos t, 1)$, which is never parallel to the
$z$-axis for any $t$. The exact equality fails! In multi-variable analysis, one must
settle for the \emph{Mean Value Inequality}:
$\norm{\vec{f}(b)-\vec{f}(a)}\le \sup \norm{\vec{f}'(t)}\,\abs{b-a}$. In modern
optimization, nonsmooth analysis, and machine learning, extending mean value theorems
to functions with sharp corners (subgradient calculus) and to curved geometric spaces
without linear coordinates remains an active frontier of mathematical research.
\end{historybox}

\subsection{Reading the theorem carefully}

The MVT is an \emph{existence} theorem. It promises that such a point $c$ exists; it
does not tell you where, and there may be several. For most purposes this is
exactly enough, because we will use it to say ``$f'$ at some unknown point equals
this average,'' and then bound or sign that unknown derivative.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The two faces of the MVT.} Keep both forms in mind; you will reach for
different ones in different situations.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Slope form:} $\displaystyle f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. Read it as
``the average rate is hit exactly.''
\item \emph{Increment form:} $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$. Read it as ``the total change
equals some interior rate times the elapsed input.'' This is the primary operational form:
it converts a statement about $f'$ into a statement about $f(b)-f(a)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{focusbox}

\begin{example}\label{ch15:ex-find-c}
Let $f(x)=x^3$ on $[0,2]$. The average slope is
$\frac{f(2)-f(0)}{2-0}=\frac{8-0}{2}=4$. The MVT promises a $c\in(0,2)$ with
$f'(c)=3c^2=4$, i.e. $c^2=\frac43$, $c=\frac{2}{\sqrt3}\approx 1.155$. Indeed
$1.155$ lies inside $(0,2)$, as advertised. Notice we \emph{found} $c$ here only
because the function is simple; in real applications we almost never solve for $c$
--- we just use the fact that some such $c$ exists.
\end{example}

\begin{labbox}
Take $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ on $[1,4]$. Compute the average slope
$\frac{\sqrt4-\sqrt1}{4-1}=\frac{2-1}{3}=\frac13$. Now set $f'(c)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt
c}=\frac13$ and solve: $2\sqrt c=3$, $\sqrt c=\frac32$, $c=\frac94=2.25$. Check that
$2.25$ sits in $(1,4)$. You have just located the exact instant where this curve's
slope equals its average climb. Try the same with $f(x)=1/x$ on $[1,2]$ and confirm
$c=\sqrt2$.
\end{labbox}

\section{Consequences of the Mean Value Theorem}

Now the payoff. Everything in this section follows from the single increment form
$f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$. The strategy is always the same: \emph{whatever you know
about $f'$ on the interval, you know about that mystery factor $f'(c)$, and the
equation passes the knowledge on to $f(b)-f(a)$.} Many intuitively obvious facts
are direct consequences of this single theorem.

\subsection{Zero derivative means constant}

You believe that if a function never changes (its rate is always $0$) then it stays
put. But why, \emph{exactly}? ``The derivative is zero'' is a statement about
slopes; ``the function is constant'' is a statement about values. The MVT is the
only rigorous bridge between them.

\begin{theorem}[Vanishing derivative]\label{ch15:thm-const}
If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $f'(x)=0$ for all
$x\in(a,b)$, then $f$ is constant on $[a,b]$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Pick any point $x\in(a,b]$. Apply the MVT to $f$ on the subinterval $[a,x]$: there
is a $c$ between $a$ and $x$ with
\[
  f(x)-f(a)=f'(c)\,(x-a).
\]
But $f'(c)=0$ by hypothesis, so $f(x)-f(a)=0$, i.e. $f(x)=f(a)$. Since $x$ was
arbitrary, $f$ equals the constant $f(a)$ everywhere.
\end{proof}

\begin{trapbox}
This is exactly the place where the \emph{interval matters}. On a disconnected
domain the conclusion is false. The function
\[
  f(x)=\begin{cases}1,& x>0,\\ -1,& x<0,\end{cases}
\]
has $f'(x)=0$ everywhere it is defined, yet it is not constant. There is no
contradiction: the MVT needs a single interval connecting any two points, and there
is no such interval joining a negative $x$ to a positive one inside the domain. The
derivative tells you nothing about how $f$ behaves \emph{across a gap}.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: why does a gap break the bridge?}
Suppose $f(x)=2$ for $x\in(0,1)$ and $f(x)=7$ for $x\in(2,3)$. Differentiating
gives $f'(x)=0$ everywhere the function is defined. Yet $f(2.5)-f(0.5)=7-2=5\ne 0$.
Why didn't the Mean Value Theorem force $f(2.5)=f(0.5)$?
Because the theorem requires an unbroken interval containing both points. To apply
$f(2.5)-f(0.5)=f'(c)(2.5-0.5)$, the function must be defined and differentiable
at every point between $0.5$ and $2.5$. The gap on $[1,2]$ violates that requirement.
\end{recoverybox}

\subsection{Two antiderivatives differ by a constant}

This is the fact that makes the ``$+\,C$'' in every indefinite integral legitimate.
If you have found \emph{one} antiderivative of a function, you have essentially
found them all.

\begin{corollary}[Antiderivatives up to a constant]\label{ch15:cor-plusC}
If $F'(x)=G'(x)$ for all $x$ in an interval $I$, then $F(x)=G(x)+C$ on $I$ for some
constant $C$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $h=F-G$. Then $h'(x)=F'(x)-G'(x)=0$ for all $x\in I$. By the previous theorem,
$h$ is constant on $I$: $h(x)=C$. Hence $F(x)=G(x)+C$.
\end{proof}

\begin{aestheticbox}
This corollary is fundamental to integration. It explains why the Fundamental Theorem
of Calculus can speak of \emph{the} antiderivative up to an additive constant, why
$\int f$ is well posed, and why solving a differential equation $y'=f$ yields a
one-parameter family of curves.
\end{aestheticbox}

\subsection{The increasing/decreasing test}

Here is a central application: determining where functions increase and decrease.
We \emph{define} ``increasing'' by values: $f$ is increasing on $I$ if
$x_1<x_2$ forces $f(x_1)<f(x_2)$. The sign of the derivative is, again, a statement
about slopes. The MVT connects the two.

\begin{theorem}[Monotonicity test]\label{ch15:thm-mono}
Let $f$ be continuous on an interval $I$ and differentiable in its interior.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $f'(x)>0$ throughout the interior, then $f$ is strictly increasing on $I$.
\item If $f'(x)<0$ throughout, then $f$ is strictly decreasing on $I$.
\item If $f'(x)\ge 0$ throughout, then $f$ is nondecreasing; if $f'(x)\le 0$,
nonincreasing.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Take any two points $x_1<x_2$ in $I$. By the MVT on $[x_1,x_2]$ there is a $c$
between them with
\[
  f(x_2)-f(x_1)=f'(c)\,(x_2-x_1).
\]
The factor $(x_2-x_1)$ is positive. So the sign of $f(x_2)-f(x_1)$ is exactly the
sign of $f'(c)$. If $f'>0$ everywhere, then $f'(c)>0$, so $f(x_2)-f(x_1)>0$, i.e.
$f(x_2)>f(x_1)$: increasing. The other three cases are read off the same equation by
changing the sign or allowing equality.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why does the \emph{sign} of the derivative control the \emph{direction} of
the function?} Because of that single MVT equation. Without it, ``$f'>0$'' and
``$f$ increasing'' are two unrelated sentences --- one about tangent lines, one
about a table of values. The MVT says the total change $f(x_2)-f(x_1)$ is built out
of an actual interior slope times a positive width. You cannot accumulate a net
\emph{decrease} out of slopes that are all positive. The local information (slope at
points) integrates up into global information (rise across an interval), and the MVT
is the gear that meshes the two.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{A subtle but important converse warning.} ``Strictly increasing'' does
\emph{not} require $f'>0$ at every point. Consider $f(x)=x^3$. It is strictly
increasing on all of $\R$, yet $f'(0)=0$. The theorem's arrow points one way:
$f'>0\Rightarrow$ increasing, but increasing only gives $f'\ge 0$. A single flat
instant is allowed, as long as the function does not actually pause over a whole
subinterval. Many students ``prove'' a function is increasing by checking $f'\ge 0$
and then wrongly assert it must be \emph{strictly} increasing; that needs the extra
observation that $f'$ is not zero on any subinterval.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Error bounds: the MVT as a ruler}

Perhaps the most practical face of the MVT in applied mathematics is its power to
\emph{bound} how much a function can change. If you know a car's speedometer never
exceeds $70$ mph, you know with absolute certainty that in two hours the car travelled
at most $140$ miles. That is the MVT expressed as an inequality.

\begin{corollary}[Change is bounded by the steepest slope]\label{ch15:cor-bound}
Suppose $f$ is differentiable on an interval and $\abs{f'(x)}\le M$ for all $x$
in that interval. Then for any two points $a,b$ in the interval,
\[
  \abs{f(b)-f(a)}\le M\,\abs{b-a}.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By the Mean Value Theorem, there exists some interior point $c$ strictly between $a$
and $b$ such that $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$. Taking absolute values on both sides gives
\[
  \abs{f(b)-f(a)} = \abs{f'(c)}\,\abs{b-a} \le M\,\abs{b-a},
\]
because the slope $\abs{f'(c)}$ cannot exceed the bound $M$ anywhere on the interval.
\end{proof}

A function whose derivative is bounded by $M$ in this way is called
\emph{Lipschitz continuous} with constant $M$: it cannot change faster than $M$ units
of output per unit of input, ever. Geometrically, Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-cone} reveals
that the curve is trapped within a cone opening from $(a,f(a))$ with slopes $\pm M$.
This single inequality is the backbone of error analysis, numerical computation, and
the foundational theory that guarantees solutions to differential equations exist
and are unique.

\begin{example}[Bounding a value of sine]\label{ch15:ex-sin-bound}
How big can $\sin(0.1)$ be, without a calculator? Apply the bound to $f(x)=\sin x$ on
the interval $[0,0.1]$. We have $f'(x)=\cos x$, and $|\cos x|\le 1$ everywhere on the
real line, so we can take $M=1$. Then
\[
  \abs{\sin(0.1)-\sin(0)}\le 1\cdot\abs{0.1-0}=0.1.
\]
Because $\sin(0)=0$, this gives $|\sin(0.1)|\le 0.1$. In fact, applying the exact same
reasoning on $[0,x]$ proves the inequality $|\sin x|\le |x|$ for all real $x$ in a
single stroke, purely from the fact that $|\cos c|\le 1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A sharper estimate near a known value]\label{ch15:ex-sqrt-bound}
Estimate $\sqrt{4.1}$ and place rigorous upper and lower bounds on the error.
With $f(x)=\sqrt x$ on the interval $[4,4.1]$, the Mean Value Theorem gives
\[
  \sqrt{4.1}-\sqrt 4=f'(c)\,(0.1)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt c}\,(0.1)
  \quad\text{for some }c\in(4,4.1).
\]
Because $c$ lies strictly between $4$ and $4.1$, the derivative $\frac{1}{2\sqrt c}$
is strictly decreasing with respect to $c$, so it is squeezed between its endpoint values:
\[
  \frac{1}{2\sqrt{4.1}} < \frac{1}{2\sqrt c} < \frac{1}{2\sqrt 4} = 0.25.
\]
Using $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{4.1}} \approx 0.2469$, we substitute back:
\[
  \sqrt{4.1}=2+\frac{0.1}{2\sqrt c}, \qquad
  2+0.1(0.2469)\le \sqrt{4.1}\le 2+0.1(0.25),
\]
which proves $2.02469\le \sqrt{4.1}\le 2.025$. The MVT did not just supply a heuristic
guess; it \emph{trapped} the true value within a tight, provable interval of width
$0.00031$. This is the engine of rigorous numerical approximation.
\end{example}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Where this bites in practice.}
In numerical analysis, every guarantee of the form ``this approximation is accurate to
within tolerance $\epsilon$'' is ultimately an MVT estimate: the error is expressed as
a derivative evaluated at an unknown intermediate point, bounded by the worst-case
magnitude of that derivative across the interval.

In machine learning and neural network training, a loss function whose gradient has a
derivative bounded by $M$ (an $M$-Lipschitz gradient) guarantees that gradient
descent with a step size smaller than $2/M$ cannot overshoot or diverge --- precisely
Corollary~\ref{ch15:cor-bound} ensuring that a single gradient update cannot produce
an uncontrolled jump.

In physics and engineering control, if an actuator has a maximum torque or acceleration
$M$, the change in velocity over a time window $\Delta t$ is strictly bounded by
$M\,\Delta t$. The Mean Value Theorem converts an upper bound on rate into a rigorous
bound on displacement.
\end{applicationbox}

\subsection{Proving inequalities without a single graph}

The MVT turns many inequalities that look obvious from a graph into short
proofs. The recipe: build a function that is the difference of the two sides, show
its derivative has a fixed sign, and let monotonicity finish the job.

\begin{example}[$e^x \ge 1+x$ for all $x$]\label{ch15:ex-exp-ineq}
Let $g(x)=e^x-(1+x)$. Then $g(0)=0$ and $g'(x)=e^x-1$. Now $g'(x)>0$ for $x>0$ and
$g'(x)<0$ for $x<0$. By the monotonicity test, $g$ decreases up to $0$ and
increases after it, so $x=0$ is a global minimum of $g$, with value $g(0)=0$. Hence
$g(x)\ge 0$ everywhere, i.e. $e^x\ge 1+x$. The tangent line $1+x$ lies below the
exponential (Figure~\ref{ch15:fig-exp-ineq}), proved purely from the sign of a
derivative.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[$\ln(1+x)<x$ for $x>0$]\label{ch15:ex-log-ineq}
Let $g(x)=x-\ln(1+x)$ on $[0,\infty)$. Then $g(0)=0$ and
$g'(x)=1-\frac{1}{1+x}=\frac{x}{1+x}>0$ for $x>0$. So $g$ is strictly increasing on
$[0,\infty)$, giving $g(x)>g(0)=0$, that is $\ln(1+x)<x$. Same recipe, opposite
function.
\end{example}

\begin{walkbox}
Here is a question to chew on next time you are out walking. The monotonicity test
turned ``slope is positive'' into ``function is increasing'' using the MVT. But the
MVT itself rested on Rolle, which rested on the Extreme Value Theorem, which rested
on the \emph{completeness} of the real numbers --- the fact that there are no gaps in
the number line. When you say ``a positive derivative makes a function climb,''
you are, four layers down, relying on the completeness of $\R$.
Ask yourself why these theorems fail if you work only on the rational numbers $\Q$. (Hint: on $\Q$, a continuous function on a closed interval can climb toward a peak located at an irrational input like $\sqrt{2}$ and never actually attain a maximum.)
\end{walkbox}

\section{The Cauchy Mean Value Theorem}

There is a powerful generalization that you will need the moment you reach
l'H\^opital's rule, so we prove it here while the machinery is fresh. It compares the
changes in \emph{two} functions over the same interval.

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem]\label{ch15:thm-cauchy}
Let $f$ and $g$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$. Then there
is a point $c\in(a,b)$ with
\[
  \bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)\,g'(c)=\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)\,f'(c).
\]
If $g'$ is never zero on $(a,b)$ (so $g(b)\ne g(a)$), this rearranges to
\[
  \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}=\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of $\bigl(g(t),f(t)\bigr)$ as a point tracing a curve in the plane as $t$ runs
from $a$ to $b$. The ordinary MVT compared a slope to an average slope along the
$x$-axis. Cauchy's version compares the \emph{direction the curve is heading},
$f'(c)/g'(c)$, to the \emph{straight-line direction} from start to end,
$\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}$. It is the MVT for a parametrized path: somewhere the
path points the same way as the chord joining its endpoints.
\end{intuitionbox}

The secant chord connecting the endpoints has slope $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}$. At some interior
parameter value $t=c$, the instantaneous velocity vector $(g'(c), f'(c))$ points in the exact same
direction, matching the chord's slope: $\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}$.}
\label{ch15:fig-cauchy}

\begin{proof}
We use the same subtract-a-helper trick that turned Rolle into the MVT, only now the
helper is chosen to kill both endpoints at once. Define
\[
  h(x)=\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)\,g(x)-\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)\,f(x).
\]
This $h$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$. Compute the
endpoint values:
\begin{align*}
  h(a)&=\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)g(a)-\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)f(a)
      =f(b)g(a)-g(b)f(a),\\
  h(b)&=\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)g(b)-\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)f(b)
      =f(b)g(a)-g(b)f(a).
\end{align*}
The cross terms cancel and $h(a)=h(b)$. So Rolle applies: there is $c\in(a,b)$ with
$h'(c)=0$. But
\[
  h'(x)=\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)g'(x)-\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)f'(x),
\]
and $h'(c)=0$ is precisely the claimed identity.
\end{proof}

\begin{connectionbox}
Notice the ordinary MVT is the special case $g(x)=x$: then $g'(c)=1$ and
$g(b)-g(a)=b-a$, and Cauchy's identity collapses to
$f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$. So Cauchy's theorem genuinely contains the Lagrange MVT, and
both descend from Rolle. Three theorems, one idea --- ``a difference that vanishes at
both ends has a flat spot inside'' --- applied to three cleverly chosen helper
functions. When you reach l'H\^opital's rule a couple of chapters from now, that
quotient $\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}$ will be exactly what lets a ratio of functions be
replaced by a ratio of their derivatives.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{A glance ahead: the MVT seeds Taylor's theorem}

The increment form $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$ can be read as a \emph{first-order} exact
remainder: the change in $f$ is captured exactly by one derivative at one mystery
point. Taylor's theorem, which you will meet in full later, is the same idea pushed
to higher order. It says
\[
  f(b)=f(a)+f'(a)(b-a)+\tfrac12 f''(c)(b-a)^2
\]
for some interior $c$ --- a ``second-order MVT'' whose proof is, once again, build a
helper that vanishes at both ends and apply Rolle. The whole tower of polynomial
approximation, and every error bound that comes with it, grows from the seed you
planted in this chapter.

\begin{aestheticbox}
The simple geometric fact that an average slope across an interval is attained at
some interior point yields far-reaching consequences: the $+C$ in antidifferentiation,
the monotonicity test relating derivative signs to graphs, the constant function theorem,
rigorous error bounds, proofs of inequalities, Cauchy's extension behind l'H\^opital's rule,
and the remainder terms in Taylor's theorem. Every time we turn local information about a derivative into a global fact about a function, the Mean Value Theorem is doing the work.
\end{aestheticbox}

\subsection{The Scope and Horizons of the Mean Value Theorem}
\label{ch15:what-unlocked}

\begin{connectionbox}
The Mean Value Theorem is an existence guarantee, not a GPS navigator. It assures you
with absolute mathematical certainty that your car hit $60$ mph at some moment, but
it provides no mechanical formula for locating the exact mile-marker where it
occurred. For simple quadratics we can solve for $c$ directly, but for most
functions, finding $c$ means solving a transcendental equation with no algebraic
formula.

Yet that lack of a specific address is precisely what makes the theorem so powerful.
We do not need the exact coordinate of $c$; we only need to know that $c$ belongs to
the interval. If every possible slope in the interval is positive, then $f'(c)$ must
be positive, which forces $f(b)>f(a)$. If every possible slope is bounded by $M$,
then $f'(c)$ cannot exceed $M$, trapping the entire trajectory within our geometric
cone. By relinquishing the need to pinpoint $c$, the Mean Value Theorem transforms
local rate bounds into global guarantees that hold across an entire span.

This local-to-global connection underlies the chapters that follow:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In the next chapter, \emph{Maxima, Minima, and the Shape of a Graph}, the
  monotonicity and concavity tests become our primary architectural tools. By reading
  the signs of $f'$ and $f''$, we will locate peaks, valleys, and inflection points,
  turning calculus into complete geometric portraits of curves.
  \item Later, in Chapter~18 (\emph{Indeterminate Forms and L'H\^opital's Rule}), we
  will harness Cauchy's generalized Mean Value Theorem to resolve indeterminate limits
  of the form $0/0$ and $\infty/\infty$ by comparing the simultaneous growth rates of
  two functions.
\end{itemize}
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Mental warm-up: quick checks]
Answer each question in a few words or a single sentence:

\end{enumerate}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A speed argument]
A car travels $90$ miles in exactly $90$ minutes. Use the MVT to prove that at some
instant its instantaneous speed was exactly $60$ mph. Then prove the weaker claim
that at some instant its speed was \emph{at least} $60$ mph, using only the average,
without the MVT. Which statement is stronger, and why does the MVT earn the
stronger one?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Bounding with the derivative]
Suppose $f$ is differentiable on $\R$ with $f(1)=5$ and $\abs{f'(x)}\le 2$ for all
$x$. Find the smallest interval that must contain $f(4)$. (Use
Corollary~\ref{ch15:cor-bound}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Inequalities from monotonicity]
Prove each inequality by forming a difference $g$, signing $g'$, and using the
monotonicity test.
(a) $\tan x > x$ for $0<x<\frac\pi2$.\quad
(b) $\cos x > 1-\frac{x^2}{2}$ for $x\ne 0$.\quad
(c) $\frac{x}{1+x}<\ln(1+x)$ for $x>0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Uniqueness of a solution]
Show that the equation $x^5+x-1=0$ has exactly one real solution. (Existence: use
the Intermediate Value Theorem on $[0,1]$. Uniqueness: if there were two solutions,
Rolle's theorem would force $f'=0$ somewhere between them --- show that is
impossible.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Constant on an interval]
Suppose $f$ is differentiable on $\R$ with $f'(x)=3$ for all $x$. Prove, using the
MVT and not antidifferentiation rules, that $f(x)=3x+f(0)$ for every $x$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A fixed-point bound]
Let $g$ be differentiable on $\R$ with $\abs{g'(x)}\le \tfrac12$ for all $x$. Show
that for any two numbers $p,q$, $\abs{g(p)-g(q)}\le \tfrac12\abs{p-q}$. (Such a $g$
is a \emph{contraction}; this inequality is the heart of why iterating $g$ converges
to a single fixed point.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Proof: a derivative that is always positive]
Prove carefully, citing the MVT, that if $f'(x)>0$ for all $x$ in an open interval
$I$, then $f$ is one-to-one on $I$. Where exactly do you use that the interval is
connected?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Cauchy MVT in action]
Let $f(x)=x^2$ and $g(x)=x^3$ on $[1,2]$. Find the point $c$ guaranteed by Cauchy's
Mean Value Theorem, i.e. solve $\frac{f(2)-f(1)}{g(2)-g(1)}=\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}$ for
$c\in(1,2)$. Confirm your $c$ lies in the interval.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Conceptual: the role of the EVT]
Explain in a short paragraph why Rolle's theorem genuinely needs the Extreme Value
Theorem, and why the Extreme Value Theorem in turn needs the completeness of $\R$.
Give an example of a continuous function on the rationals in $[0,2]$ (with the usual
formula) that does \emph{not} attain a maximum, illustrating what breaks without
completeness.
\end{exercise}
