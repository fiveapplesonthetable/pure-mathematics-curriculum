\chapter{First-Order Differential Equations}

Chapter~36 introduced differential equations from the outside: how to read them
as local laws of change, how an initial condition selects one solution out of a
family, and how to see solutions in a slope field. This chapter builds the
methods that \emph{solve} them, starting with first-order equations,
where a single derivative ties the slope of an unknown curve to its current
position.

Differential equations are the standard way physical and biological laws are stated.
Newton did not hand us a closed-form formula for the position of a planet; he
handed us a law dictating how its velocity changes under force. A biologist
modeling an epidemic does not start with a predetermined infected-count curve;
she starts with a relationship stating that the rate of new infections is
proportional to the encounters between sick and susceptible individuals. A bank
does not define your balance in twenty years by decree; it quotes an annual
interest rate, which is a statement about $\dv{B}{t}$, the rate at which the
balance $B$ changes with time $t$. Most laws of nature are local rules of change
like these. Solving a differential equation means turning such a rule, which
speaks about one instant at a time, into a description of the behavior over a
long stretch of time.

A \textbf{first-order} differential equation is one in which only the first
derivative appears: no $y''$, no $y'''$, only $y'$ and $y$ and the independent
variable. The general shape is
\[
  \dv{y}{x} = f(x, y).
\]
Geometrically, at every point $(x,y)$ in the plane, the right-hand side $f(x,y)$
hands you a \emph{number}, a slope. So a first-order ODE is a field of
little slope marks covering the plane, and a solution is a curve that is
everywhere tangent to those marks. That picture, the \emph{slope field} of
Chapter~36, is the most useful image in this chapter.

\section{The Picture First: Slope Fields}

The equation $\dv{y}{x} = f(x,y)$ places a short segment of slope $f(x,y)$ at
each point $(x,y)$, and a solution curve is a path that runs tangent to those
segments everywhere. Keep this picture in view through every algebraic step
that follows.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Think of the slope field as a current in a river and the solution curves as the
paths leaves take when dropped in. Dropping a leaf at a point is choosing an
initial condition, and the current carries it along one path. Different
starting points give different paths, but they all obey the same current. Solving
the ODE means \emph{finding the shape of those paths}. You can often sketch their
shape by eye, long before you can write a formula, by following the slope marks.
\end{intuitionbox}

Consider the simplest interesting example, $\dv{y}{x} = y$. At a point with height
$y=2$ the slope is $2$; at height $y=-1$ the slope is $-1$; along the $x$-axis the
slope is $0$. The slope depends only on height, not on $x$, so the field looks the
same as you slide left or right. Figure~\ref{ch37:slopefield-exp} shows it.

\textbf{Pause and think: reading a slope field by eye.}
Consider the differential equation $\dv{y}{x} = -\dfrac{x}{y}$. What do the slopes
do along the coordinate axes? On the $y$-axis (where $x=0$), the slope is $0$,

$y\,\dd y = -x\,\dd x$ integrates to $x^2+y^2=C$.)
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{happeningbox}
A first-order ODE is a \emph{local} law: it talks about one point at a time,
telling you the slope there. Solving it means \emph{building a whole curve out of
that local law}. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and the existence and
uniqueness theorem of Chapter~36 guarantee that this can be done: following the
slope rule from a starting point traces out exactly one curve. Every solution
method in this chapter is a different way of building that curve.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{focusbox}
Three words you must keep straight:
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{general solution} is a whole \emph{family} of curves, carrying one
arbitrary constant $C$ (because a first-order equation involves one integration,
and integration always leaves a $+C$).
\item An \textbf{initial condition} like $y(x_0)=y_0$ picks \emph{one} curve out of
the family by naming a point it must pass through. This nails down $C$.
\item A \textbf{particular solution} is that single chosen curve.
\end{itemize}
The general solution is the river; the initial condition is where you drop the
leaf; the particular solution is the leaf's path.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Separable Equations}
\label{ch37:sec-separable}

The friendliest equations are those in which the slope $f(x,y)$ factors into a part
that depends only on $x$ times a part that depends only on $y$:
\[
  \dv{y}{x} = g(x)\,h(y).
\]
We call these \textbf{separable}, because we can herd all the $y$'s to one side and
all the $x$'s to the other. The standard procedure treats $\dv{y}{x}$ formally
as a differential quotient, multiplying both sides by $\dd x$, dividing by
$h(y)$, and integrating:
\[
  \frac{\dd y}{h(y)} = g(x)\,\dd x
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \int \frac{\dd y}{h(y)} = \int g(x)\,\dd x.
\]
This gives the right answer. But look at what we did: we cut the derivative $\dv{y}{x}$ in
half and treated $\dd y$ and $\dd x$ as separate movable objects, as if the
derivative were a genuine ratio of two numbers. However, $\dv{y}{x}$ is formally a
\emph{limit} of ratios, a single indivisible symbol. So why is this allowed?

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Separation of variables is the chain rule run backwards.} Here is the
rigorous derivation, with no division of $\dd y$ by $\dd x$.

Start from $\dv{y}{x}=g(x)\,h(y)$. Assume $h(y)\neq 0$, and divide both sides
(legitimately, these are real numbers) by $h(y)$:
\[
  \frac{1}{h(y)}\,\dv{y}{x} = g(x).
\]
Now introduce $H(y)$, an antiderivative of $1/h(y)$, so that $H'(y)=1/h(y)$. By the
\emph{chain rule}, the left-hand side is exactly the derivative of $H(y(x))$ with
respect to $x$:
\[
  \ddx{H(y(x))} = H'(y)\,\dv{y}{x} = \frac{1}{h(y)}\,\dv{y}{x}.
\]
So our equation says
\[
  \ddx{H(y(x))} = g(x).
\]
Two functions of $x$ with the same derivative differ by a constant, so integrating
both sides in $x$ gives
\[
  H(y) = \int g(x)\,\dd x + C.
\]
That is \emph{precisely} the answer the casual ``multiply by $\dd x$'' recipe
produces, because $\int \dd y / h(y)$ is $H(y)$. The fraction-juggling is a
memory aid; the chain rule is the reason it works. The $\dd y$ and $\dd x$ are not
being separated; they are bookkeeping symbols that happen to track the
substitution $u=y(x)$ correctly. This is the same reason $u$-substitution in
integration works: separation of variables is mathematically equivalent to
$u$-substitution.
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
When we divided by $h(y)$ we assumed $h(y)\neq 0$. But any value $y=c$ with
$h(c)=0$ gives the \emph{constant} solution $y\equiv c$ (the symbol $\equiv$,
``identically equal'', means $y(x)=c$ for every $x$). Its derivative is zero, and
$g(x)\,h(c)=0$ too, so it solves the equation. These constant solutions are called
\emph{equilibrium} solutions, because nothing changes along them. The separation
recipe can lose them, because dividing by $h(y)$ excludes them. Always check the
roots of $h$ separately. We will meet these again as
equilibria in the section on autonomous equations.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{A First Worked Example}

Solve $\dv{y}{x} = xy$ with $y(0)=3$.

\begin{walkbox}
Is it separable? The right side is $x \cdot y$, a function of $x$ times a function
of $y$. Yes, with $g(x)=x$ and $h(y)=y$. So we expect to find $\int \dd y/y$ on the
left and $\int x\,\dd x$ on the right.
\end{walkbox}

\textbf{Step 1. Separate.} Divide by $y$ (noting $y=0$ as a possible lost solution):
\[
  \frac{1}{y}\,\dv{y}{x} = x.
\]
\textbf{Step 2. Integrate both sides in $x$.} The left integrates to $\ln\abs{y}$
(chain rule in reverse), the right to $\tfrac12 x^2$:
\[
  \ln\abs{y} = \tfrac12 x^2 + C.
\]
\textbf{Step 3. Solve for $y$.} Exponentiate:
\[
  \abs{y} = e^{C}\,e^{x^2/2}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad y = A\,e^{x^2/2},
\]
where $A=\pm e^{C}$ is a new constant absorbing the sign and the $e^C$. (Letting
$A=0$ even recovers the lost solution $y\equiv 0$, so the single formula
$y=Ae^{x^2/2}$ captures everything.)

\textbf{Step 4. Apply the initial condition.} $y(0)=3$ means $3 = A\,e^{0}=A$, so
$A=3$. The particular solution is
\[
  \boxed{\,y = 3\,e^{x^2/2}\,}.
\]
Check: $\dv{y}{x} = 3\,e^{x^2/2}\cdot x = x\cdot y$. It satisfies the equation, and
$y(0)=3$. Figure~\ref{ch37:fig-separable-family} shows this curve among the other
members of the family $y=Ae^{x^2/2}$.

\begin{labbox}
Solve $\dv{y}{x} = \dfrac{x}{y}$ with $y(0)=2$ before reading on. Separate as
$y\,\dd y = x\,\dd x$, integrate to $\tfrac12 y^2 = \tfrac12 x^2 + C$, so
$y^2 - x^2 = $ const. The solution curves are \emph{hyperbolas}. The initial
condition $y(0)=2$ gives $4 - 0 = 4$, so $y = \sqrt{x^2+4}$ (the positive root,
because $y(0)=2>0$). The answer first came out as an
implicit relation $y^2-x^2=4$ that we then solved for $y$; many separable problems
leave you with an implicit equation, and that is a perfectly good answer.
\end{labbox}

\subsection{Why an Initial Condition Pins Down One Curve}

\begin{connectionbox}
The general solution $y=Ae^{x^2/2}$ is a one-parameter family: a stack of
curves, one for each value of $A$. They fill the plane without crossing, like
the leaves' paths in the river picture. The existence and uniqueness theorem of
Chapter~36 (which
holds whenever $f(x,y)$ and $\pdv{f}{y}$ are continuous near the starting point)
guarantees that through each point passes \emph{exactly one} solution curve. That is
why naming one point, the initial condition, selects one and only one member of the
family. No two distinct solution curves of a well-behaved first-order ODE ever
touch.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Exponential Growth and Decay}

The most widely used separable equation in science is
\[
  \dv{y}{t} = k\,y,
\]
where $k$ is a fixed number. It says \emph{the rate of change is proportional to
the amount present}. Read it
as a sentence: ``the more there is, the faster it grows'' (if $k>0$), or ``the more
there is, the faster it shrinks'' (if $k<0$). Money in a continuously compounded
account, a colony of bacteria with unlimited food, a lump of radioactive uranium,
the shrinking temperature difference between a hot object and its room: all obey this.

Separating, $\dfrac{\dd y}{y} = k\,\dd t$, so $\ln\abs{y} = kt + C$, and
\[
  \boxed{\,y(t) = y_0\,e^{kt}\,},
\]
where $y_0=y(0)$ is the starting amount. If $k>0$ the amount explodes; if $k<0$ it
decays toward zero. Figure~\ref{ch37:growth-decay} shows both.

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{Radiocarbon dating.} The radioactive decay law $N'=-\lambda N$, where
$N(t)$ is the number of undecayed atoms and $\lambda>0$ is a constant called the
\emph{decay constant}, was pieced together by Ernest Rutherford and Frederick
Soddy around 1902 from careful laboratory measurements of how ``thorium
emanation'' (a radioactive gas given off by thorium) faded over time. They
discovered that a fixed \emph{fraction} of atoms decayed per unit time,
regardless of the total amount present, which is the signature of the
proportionality law $N'\propto N$. From this came the concept of
\emph{half-life}, the time $t_{1/2}$ required for half of any given sample to
vanish. The solution is $N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$, so setting
$\tfrac12 = e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}}$ and taking logarithms gives
\[
  t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}.
\]
Half a century later, this exact differential
equation, run backward in time, allowed Willard Libby to calculate the age of
ancient biological remains from the residual carbon-14 left in their bones, an
invention that earned the 1960 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Carbon dating, worked.} Living tissue holds a steady ratio of carbon-14
(written $^{14}$C); when it dies, the $^{14}$C decays with half-life
$t_{1/2}=5730$ years, so
\[
  \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{5730}\approx 1.21\times 10^{-4}\ \text{yr}^{-1}.
\]
A bone is found with $62\%$ of the living $^{14}$C level. How old is it? Solve
$0.62 = e^{-\lambda t}$ for $t$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  t &= -\frac{\ln 0.62}{\lambda}
     = -\frac{\ln 0.62}{1.21\times 10^{-4}} \\
    &\approx \frac{0.478}{1.21\times 10^{-4}}
     \approx 3950\ \text{years}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The bone is roughly four thousand years old. Figure~\ref{ch37:fig-carbon} shows the
decay curve and where the bone sits on it.
\end{applicationbox}

\subsection{Newton's Law of Cooling}

A hot cup of coffee in a cool room cools fast at first, then slowly, leveling off at
room temperature. Newton's law of cooling captures this: the rate of cooling is
proportional to the temperature \emph{difference} between the object and its
surroundings. If $T(t)$ is the object's temperature, $T_{\!a}$ the (constant)
ambient temperature (the temperature of the surroundings), and $k$ a positive
constant that depends on the object and how well it sheds heat,
\[
  \dv{T}{t} = -k\,(T - T_{\!a}), \qquad k>0.
\]
The minus sign is the physics: if $T>T_{\!a}$ the object is hotter than the room, so
$\dv{T}{t}<0$ and it cools; if $T<T_{\!a}$ it warms. Either way it heads toward
$T_{\!a}$.

\begin{whybox}
This has the same structure as $y'=ky$ under a shift of coordinates. Let $u = T - T_{\!a}$ be the temperature \emph{difference}. Since $T_{\!a}$
is constant, $\dv{u}{t} = \dv{T}{t}$, and the equation becomes
\[
  \dv{u}{t} = -k\,u,
\]
plain exponential decay. The difference from ambient decays exponentially:
$u(t)=u_0 e^{-kt}$, where $u_0=T_0-T_{\!a}$ and $T_0=T(0)$ is the starting
temperature. Translating back,
\[
  T(t) = T_{\!a} + (T_0 - T_{\!a})\,e^{-kt}.
\]
The substitution $u=T-T_{\!a}$ \emph{slid the whole problem down} so that the
equilibrium sat at zero, where we already know the answer, then slid it back up.
Much of differential-equations technique is this move: find the change of
variable that turns your problem into one you have already solved.
\end{whybox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{The coffee, worked.} Coffee at $T_0 = 90^\circ$C sits in a $20^\circ$C room.
After $5$ minutes it has cooled to $70^\circ$C. When will it reach a drinkable
$50^\circ$C?

First find $k$. With $T_{\!a}=20$ and $T_0=90$, the difference starts at $70$ and at
$t=5$ has fallen to $70-20=50$:
\[
  50 = 70\,e^{-5k}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{-5k}=\frac{5}{7}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = \frac{1}{5}\ln\frac{7}{5}\approx 0.0673\ \text{min}^{-1}.
\]
Now solve for the time when $T=50$, that is, when the difference is $30$:
\[
  30 = 70\,e^{-kt}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{-kt}=\frac{3}{7}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = \frac{\ln(7/3)}{k}\approx \frac{0.847}{0.0673}\approx 12.6\ \text{min}.
\]
So about $12.6$ minutes after pouring. The curve in Figure~\ref{ch37:cooling}
flattens toward the room line; that flattening is the factor $e^{-kt}$ shrinking
toward $0$.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{First-Order Linear Equations and the Integrating Factor}

Not every equation separates. Consider
\[
  \dv{y}{x} + 2y = x.
\]
Try to separate it: $\dv{y}{x} = x - 2y$. The right side is $x-2y$, which does
\emph{not} factor as (function of $x$)(function of $y$), so separation fails. We
need another method.

A large class of equations we can solve systematically is the \textbf{first-order linear} equations:
\[
  \dv{y}{x} + P(x)\,y = Q(x).
\]
``Linear'' means $y$ and $y'$ appear only to the first power, never multiplied
together, never inside a sine or a square. The coefficients $P$ and $Q$ may be any
functions of $x$. Many models in physics, biology, and finance have this form.

\subsection{The Idea: Make the Left Side a Single Derivative}

The left side $\dv{y}{x} + P(x)\,y$ is \emph{almost} the
derivative of a product. Recall the product rule, for any function $\mu(x)$:
\[
  \ddx{\mu y} = \mu\,\dv{y}{x} + \mu' y.
\]
If we could multiply our whole equation by some well-chosen function
$\mu(x)$ (the Greek letter mu) so that the left side became exactly $\ddx{\mu y}$,
then the equation would collapse to
\[
  \ddx{\mu y} = \mu\,Q,
\]
and we could \emph{integrate both sides}. The function $\mu$ that
achieves this is called the \textbf{integrating factor}.

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Deriving the integrating factor.} Multiply the equation by an unknown
positive function $\mu(x)$:
\[
  \mu\,\dv{y}{x} + \mu P\,y = \mu Q.
\]
We \emph{want} the left side to equal $\ddx{\mu y} = \mu\,\dv{y}{x} + \mu' y$.
Comparing the two expressions term by term, the $\mu\,y'$ pieces already match. The
remaining pieces match precisely when
\[
  \mu' = \mu P.
\]
That is a \emph{separable} equation for $\mu$. Separate it:
$\dfrac{\dd\mu}{\mu} = P\,\dd x$, integrate to $\ln\mu = \int P\,\dd x$, and
exponentiate:
\[
  \boxed{\,\mu(x) = e^{\int P(x)\,\dd x}\,}.
\]
This formula comes from one demand: that the product rule apply. Rewritten as
$\mu'/\mu = P$, the condition says the logarithmic derivative of $\mu$ (the
derivative of $\ln\mu$) is $P$; that is what it takes to make
$\mu y'+\mu P y$ collapse into one derivative.
\end{whybox}

\begin{happeningbox}
Multiplying by $\mu$ changes the point of view. The combination
$y' + Py$ does not look like the derivative of anything tidy. But multiply by $\mu$
and it becomes the derivative of the single quantity $\mu y$. We have re-expressed
an awkward two-term expression as the rate of change of one product. Once something
is written as $\ddx{\text{stuff}}$, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus lets us undo
it by integration.
\end{happeningbox}

\subsection{The Full Recipe, Derived}

Putting it together: to solve $\dv{y}{x} + P(x)\,y = Q(x)$,
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute the integrating factor $\mu = e^{\int P\,\dd x}$.
\item Multiply the equation by $\mu$. The left side is now $\ddx{\mu y}$ by
construction.
\item Integrate both sides: $\mu y = \int \mu Q\,\dd x + C$.
\item Divide by $\mu$ to get $y = \dfrac{1}{\mu}\left(\int \mu Q\,\dd x + C\right)$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Worked Example with the Integrating Factor}

Solve $\dv{y}{x} + 2y = x$ with $y(0)=1$.

\begin{walkbox}
Here $P(x)=2$ (a constant) and $Q(x)=x$. The integrating factor will be
$e^{\int 2\,\dd x}=e^{2x}$. We expect to multiply through by $e^{2x}$ and watch the
left side fold into $\ddx{e^{2x}y}$.
\end{walkbox}

\textbf{Step 1. Integrating factor.} $\mu = e^{\int 2\,\dd x}=e^{2x}$.

\textbf{Step 2. Multiply through.}
\[
  e^{2x}\dv{y}{x} + 2e^{2x}y = x\,e^{2x}.
\]
The left side is exactly $\ddx{e^{2x}y}$; check it with the product rule:
$\ddx{e^{2x}y} = e^{2x}y' + 2e^{2x}y$.

\textbf{Step 3. Integrate both sides.}
\[
  e^{2x}y = \int x\,e^{2x}\,\dd x.
\]
The integral on the right needs integration by parts (let $u=x$, $\dd v=e^{2x}\dd
x$):
\[
  \int x\,e^{2x}\,\dd x = \frac{x}{2}e^{2x} - \frac{1}{2}\int e^{2x}\,\dd x
  = \frac{x}{2}e^{2x} - \frac{1}{4}e^{2x} + C.
\]
So
\[
  e^{2x}y = \frac{x}{2}e^{2x} - \frac{1}{4}e^{2x} + C.
\]
\textbf{Step 4. Solve for $y$.} Divide by $e^{2x}$:
\[
  y = \frac{x}{2} - \frac{1}{4} + C\,e^{-2x}.
\]
\textbf{Step 5. Initial condition.} $y(0)=1$:
\[
  1 = 0 - \tfrac14 + C \ \Rightarrow\ C = \tfrac54.
\]
The particular solution is
\[
  \boxed{\,y = \frac{x}{2} - \frac14 + \frac54\,e^{-2x}\,}.
\]
The solution has two parts. The part $\frac{x}{2}-\frac14$ follows the input
$Q(x)=x$ on the right-hand side (engineers call $Q$ the \emph{forcing} or
\emph{input}); this is the \emph{steady response}, and it is a particular
solution of the equation. The part $\frac54 e^{-2x}$ is a \emph{transient}: a
term that dies away as $x$ grows. It solves the homogeneous equation
$y'+2y=0$ (the same equation with the right side set to zero, as in Chapter~36).
This split into a lasting response to the input plus a dying transient recurs
throughout applied mathematics.
Figure~\ref{ch37:fig-linear-transient} shows it.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: transient versus steady response.}
Examine the formula $y(x) = \frac{x}{2} - \frac{1}{4} + C e^{-2x}$. By the
time $x=3$, the transient exponential factor is $e^{-6} \approx 0.0025$. How much
does the initial starting point $y(0)$ matter once $x > 3$?
Hardly at all: the solution has in effect ``forgotten'' where it started, and the
input $Q(x)=x$ now determines its course. In
electronics, heat transfer, and acoustics, the initial state produces only a
short-lived transient; the long-term behavior is set by the input.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Two common pitfalls to avoid:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Forgetting to put the equation in \emph{standard form} first. The coefficient
of $y'$ must be $1$ before you read off $P$. If you start from
$x\,y' + y = x^2$, divide by $x$ to get $y' + \tfrac1x y = x$, so $P=1/x$, not
something else.
\item Dragging the $+C$ from the integrating-factor integral $\int P\,\dd x$ into
$\mu$. You do not need it: any one antiderivative works, because an extra constant
in the exponent only multiplies $\mu$ by a constant, and that constant cancels when
you divide by $\mu$ at the end. Use the simplest $\mu$. The $+C$ that \emph{does}
matter is the one from Step 3, integrating $\mu Q$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{trapbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The integrating-factor method resembles completing the square: you
change an expression by a deliberate multiplication (or addition) until it
has a shape you know how to undo. There, you force a perfect square; here,
you force a perfect derivative. Reshaping a problem until it matches a known
operation is a move you will meet again and again in mathematics.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{historybox}
Differential equations arose alongside calculus itself, as mathematicians sought
to model physical laws that relate rates of change to the current state of a
system. The earliest
challenges in the 1690s grew directly out of physics and geometry. Mathematicians sought the
shape of a drooping hanging chain (the \emph{catenary}, from the Latin for chain),
the fastest frictionless ramp between two points
under gravity (the \emph{brachistochrone}, from the Greek for shortest time), and
cannonball trajectories through air resistance.

The pioneers had very few tools. Isaac Newton in England and Gottfried Wilhelm
Leibniz in Germany had developed the initial rules of fluxions and differentials
in the 1670s and 1680s, but there was no general theory of differential
equations, no systematic integration tables, and not even a standard concept of
a function as an abstract mapping. Instead, mathematicians worked with geometric
sketches, ingenious coordinate substitutions, and power series. Above all, they
relied on differentials $\dd x$ and $\dd y$, which they manipulated as literal,
vanishingly small pieces of curves.

Between 1691 and 1695, the Swiss brothers Jakob and Johann Bernoulli turned the
new calculus into an arena of fierce public competition. Working in Basel, the
brothers challenged each other and the mathematical world through the pages of
the journal \emph{Acta Eruditorum}. When Jakob posed what is now known as the
Bernoulli equation ($y' + P(x)y = Q(x)y^n$, where $y$ is raised to a power),
Leibniz and Johann solved it promptly. Leibniz discovered the
integrating-factor technique in 1694, realizing that multiplying by an auxiliary
exponential could force an unruly sum of terms into the derivative of a single
product. Leibniz and Johann also worked out separation of variables (Johann
gave the method its name), herding the variables to opposite sides of the
equals sign so that each side could be integrated on its own. Leonhard Euler, a student of Johann Bernoulli, later generalized
integrating factors in the 1730s and 1740s into a systematic technique.

These developments occurred alongside two major debates. First was the
priority dispute between Newton and Leibniz and their respective disciples, which
soured relations between British and Continental mathematicians for decades and
delayed Britain's adoption of the far more flexible Leibnizian notation
$\dv{y}{x}$. Second was a question of rigor: critics, notably Bishop George
Berkeley in his 1734 treatise \emph{The Analyst}, objected to treating $\dd y$
and $\dd x$ as nonzero quantities when separating and dividing them, yet as
vanishing increments without a clear logical definition. As we saw in Section~\ref{ch37:sec-separable}, it took the modern
understanding of the chain rule and the limit concept to validate the
infinitesimal bookkeeping that the Bernoullis had intuitively mastered.

First-order methods made it possible to analyze classical celestial mechanics, ballistics,
fluid flow, population biology, and the foundations of thermodynamics. Yet deep
barriers quickly emerged. In 1841 the French mathematician Joseph Liouville
proved that even simple-looking \emph{Riccati equations} (equations of the form
$y'=a(x)+b(x)\,y+c(x)\,y^2$, named after Jacopo Riccati) such as $y' = x^2 + y^2$ cannot
be solved in terms of \emph{elementary functions} (finite combinations of
polynomials, roots, exponentials, logarithms, and trigonometric functions) and
their integrals. The same holds for $y' = y^2 + x$, and for most differential
equations one writes down at random.

That boundary shifted the entire field from formula-hunting toward the modern
geometric and qualitative viewpoint pioneered by Henri Poincar\'e in the late
nineteenth century, which describes how solutions behave without needing a
formula for them.
Today, open questions about such equations belong to
\emph{dynamical systems} (the study of how systems evolve over time, viewed
geometrically). A central open problem is the second part of \emph{Hilbert's sixteenth
problem}, posed by David Hilbert in 1900: what is the maximum number and relative
configuration of \emph{limit cycles} (isolated closed loops in the plane
representing self-sustaining, periodic rhythms) that can arise for
differential equations $\dv{y}{x} = \frac{Q(x,y)}{P(x,y)}$ in which $P$ and $Q$ are
polynomials? Even when $P$ and $Q$ have degree two, Hilbert's question remains
unsolved.
\end{historybox}

\section{Autonomous Equations, Equilibria, and Stability}

A first-order equation is \textbf{autonomous} (self-governing) when the right side
does not mention the independent variable explicitly:
\[
  \dv{y}{t} = f(y).
\]
The slope depends only on \emph{where} you are ($y$), never on \emph{when} ($t$).
Newton cooling, exponential growth, and the logistic equation we are about to meet
are all autonomous. For such equations we can read off the long-term
fate of solutions \emph{without solving the equation at all}, by looking at the
sign of $f$.

\subsection{Equilibria: Where Nothing Changes}

An \textbf{equilibrium} (also called a steady state or fixed point) is a value $y^*$ where
$f(y^*)=0$. There, $\dv{y}{t}=0$: the system is frozen. If you start exactly at
$y^*$ you stay there forever, giving the constant solution $y\equiv y^*$. These are
the constant solutions from the separable section, now considered systematically.

The next question is \emph{stability}: if the system is pushed slightly away
from an equilibrium (a small push like this is called a \emph{perturbation}), does
it return toward the equilibrium (\emph{stable}) or move away (\emph{unstable})?

\begin{intuitionbox}
Picture a marble resting on a curved track of hills and valleys. A \textbf{stable}
equilibrium is the bottom of a bowl: push the marble a little in either direction
and gravity rolls it back to the bottom. An \textbf{unstable} equilibrium
is the top of a hill: balanced while undisturbed, but the
smallest nudge sends it rolling away. A \textbf{phase line}
is the one-dimensional version of this picture, and the sign of $f(y)$ tells you
which way the marble rolls.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{The Phase Line}

Because the equation is autonomous, we can draw everything on a single vertical
$y$-axis, the \emph{phase line}. Mark the equilibria (roots of $f$). Between them,
$f$ has a constant sign. Where $f(y)>0$, the solution increases (draw an upward
arrow); where $f(y)<0$, it decreases (downward arrow). An equilibrium with arrows
pointing \emph{toward} it from both sides is stable, and is also called a
\emph{sink} or \emph{attractor}; one with arrows pointing \emph{away} on both sides
is unstable, a \emph{source} or \emph{repeller}. If the arrows on both sides point
the same way, the equilibrium is \emph{semi-stable}: solutions on one side approach
it, solutions on the other side leave. Figure~\ref{ch37:fig-stability-archetypes}
shows the three cases.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The derivative test for stability.} Near an equilibrium $y^*$, replace $f$
by its tangent line (its linear approximation from Chapter~14):
$f(y)\approx f(y^*)+f'(y^*)(y-y^*) = f'(y^*)(y-y^*)$, since $f(y^*)=0$. So a small displacement
$u=y-y^*$ obeys $\dv{u}{t}\approx f'(y^*)\,u$, the exponential equation with
$k=f'(y^*)$. Therefore:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  f'(y^*) &< 0 \implies \text{stable (perturbations decay)}, \\
  f'(y^*) &> 0 \implies \text{unstable (perturbations grow)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
If $f'(y^*)=0$ the test says nothing, and you must look at the signs of $f$ on
either side (as in panel (c) of Figure~\ref{ch37:fig-stability-archetypes}).
Replacing $f$ by its tangent line to judge stability is called \emph{linear
stability analysis}; this is its one-dimensional case.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think: stability from the slope of $f$.}
Suppose an autonomous rate law has an equilibrium at $y^* = 4$. If you sketch the
graph of $f(y)$ against $y$, and the curve crosses the horizontal axis from positive
to negative as $y$ increases past $4$, is $y^*=4$ stable or unstable?
Below $4$, $f(y) > 0$, so $y$ increases toward $4$. Above $4$, $f(y) < 0$, so $y$
decreases toward $4$. The flow squeezes toward $4$ from both sides: it is a stable
sink. A curve that crosses from positive to negative is sloping downward there, so
$f'(4) < 0$, matching the derivative test.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Logistic Growth}

Exponential growth $y'=ky$ holds only while resources are unlimited. No real
population grows forever; resource limits and competition eventually constrain growth. The simplest
fix, proposed by the Belgian mathematician Pierre Fran\c{c}ois Verhulst in 1838, is
to let the per-capita growth rate (the growth rate per individual, $P'/P$)
\emph{decline} as the population $P(t)$ approaches a
ceiling $M$, the \textbf{carrying capacity}:
\[
  \dv{P}{t} = r\,P\left(1 - \frac{P}{M}\right).
\]
Here $r>0$ is the growth rate the population would have if it were tiny (often
called the \emph{intrinsic} growth rate).

\begin{intuitionbox}
Read the two factors. When $P$ is tiny, $1-P/M\approx 1$, and growth is nearly
exponential, $P'\approx rP$. As $P$ climbs toward
$M$, the factor $1-P/M$ shrinks toward $0$, throttling the growth. At $P=M$ growth
stops entirely. The population is self-limiting: its own size applies the brakes.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{The Phase Line of the Logistic}

This is autonomous with $f(P)=rP(1-P/M)$, a downward parabola in $P$ crossing zero
at $P=0$ and $P=M$. Between them $f>0$ (growth); below $0$ and above $M$, $f<0$. So:
\begin{itemize}
\item $P^*=0$ is \textbf{unstable}: $f'(0)=r>0$. A tiny population grows away from
extinction.
\item $P^*=M$ is \textbf{stable}: $f'(M)=-r<0$. Populations above or below $M$ are
drawn back to it.
\end{itemize}
Every positive starting population, no matter how small or how large, ends up at the
carrying capacity $M$. Figure~\ref{ch37:logistic-phaseline} draws this verdict as a
phase line, and Figure~\ref{ch37:logistic} shows the family of solution curves all
funneling toward $P=M$.

\subsection{Solving the Logistic Equation}

The logistic equation is separable, though evaluating the integral calls on partial
fractions. Separate variables:
\[
  \frac{\dd P}{P\,(1 - P/M)} = r\,\dd t.
\]
Split the fraction on the left into two simpler pieces by partial fractions
(Chapter~24):
\[
  \frac{1}{P(1-P/M)} = \frac{1}{P} + \frac{1/M}{1-P/M}.
\]
You can check this by recombining the right side over a common denominator. Now
integrate both sides:
\[
  \int\!\left(\frac{1}{P} + \frac{1/M}{1-P/M}\right)\dd P = \int r\,\dd t.
\]
For a population, $P>0$, so the first term integrates to $\ln P$. For the second
term, substituting $u = 1 - P/M$ gives $\dd u = -\frac{1}{M}\dd P$, introducing a
minus sign. We keep an absolute value, because $u$ is negative when the population
starts above capacity ($P>M$):
\[
  \int \frac{1/M}{1-P/M}\,\dd P = -\int \frac{\dd u}{u} = -\ln\left\lvert 1 - \frac{P}{M}\right\rvert.
\]
Combining the logarithms gives
\[
  \ln P - \ln\left\lvert 1 - \frac{P}{M}\right\rvert = \ln\left\lvert\frac{P}{1-P/M}\right\rvert = rt + C.
\]
Exponentiate both sides, and absorb the sign hidden in the absolute value into the
constant:
\[
  \frac{P}{1 - P/M} = C_0\,e^{rt}, \qquad\text{where}\quad C_0 = \pm e^C.
\]
To isolate $P(t)$, take the reciprocal of both sides:
\[
  \frac{1 - P/M}{P} = \frac{1}{P} - \frac{1}{M} = \frac{1}{C_0}\,e^{-rt}.
\]
Add $\frac{1}{M}$ and place the right-hand side over a common denominator $M$:
\[
  \frac{1}{P} = \frac{1}{M} + \frac{1}{C_0}\,e^{-rt} = \frac{1 + (M/C_0)\,e^{-rt}}{M}.
\]
Inverting gives the \textbf{logistic function}:
\[
  \boxed{\,P(t) = \frac{M}{1 + A\,e^{-rt}}\,},\qquad
  A = \frac{M}{C_0} = \frac{M - P_0}{P_0},
\]
where $P_0 = P(0)$ is the initial population at $t=0$.

As $t\to\infty$, $e^{-rt}\to 0$ and $P\to M$, confirming the phase-line verdict. (A
start above capacity gives $A<0$, and the same formula describes the red curve in
Figure~\ref{ch37:logistic}.) A solution that starts below $M/2$ has a single
inflection point, where it switches from concave-up to concave-down, located
exactly at $P=M/2$, the moment of fastest growth.

\begin{walkbox}
Why is the fastest growth at $P=M/2$? The growth \emph{rate} is
$f(P)=rP(1-P/M)$, a downward parabola in $P$. Its maximum is at the vertex, halfway
between its roots $0$ and $M$, namely $P=M/2$. So the population adds members
fastest when it is at half capacity, $P=M/2$.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{A yeast culture, worked.} A flask of yeast follows the logistic law with
carrying capacity $M=1000$ cells and intrinsic rate $r=0.7\ \text{hr}^{-1}$,
starting from $P_0=50$ cells. Then $A=(1000-50)/50 = 19$, so
\[
  P(t) = \frac{1000}{1 + 19\,e^{-0.7t}}.
\]
How long until the culture is half full ($P=500$)? Half-full happens when the
denominator is $2$, i.e.\ $19e^{-0.747t}=1$:
\[
  t = \frac{\ln 19}{0.747} \approx \frac{2.944}{0.747}\approx 3.9\ \text{hours}.
\]
That is also the moment of fastest growth. Verhulst's 1845 fit of this curve to
Belgian census data was one of the first predictive uses of a
differential-equation model in the social sciences.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
\textbf{Verhulst and population modeling.} Verhulst introduced the logistic curve
in 1838 and named it \emph{logistique} in 1845, but his work was largely forgotten
for eighty years. It was rediscovered independently in the 1920s by Raymond Pearl
and Lowell Reed, who fit it to the United States population and projected a ceiling
of approximately 197 million. Later census counts exceeded that estimate,
illustrating that the logistic model is an idealization that clarifies self-limiting
dynamics rather than providing exact demographic forecasts.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Existence, Uniqueness, and the Limits of Formulas}

We have leaned on the comforting belief that an initial condition picks out exactly
one solution. When is that guaranteed?

\begin{formalbox}
\textbf{Existence and Uniqueness (Picard--Lindel\"of, stated plainly).} Consider the
initial value problem $\dv{y}{x}=f(x,y)$, $y(x_0)=y_0$. If $f$ and its partial
derivative $\pdv{f}{y}$ are continuous on some rectangle containing $(x_0,y_0)$, then
on some interval around $x_0$ there exists \emph{one and only one} solution passing
through $(x_0,y_0)$.
\end{formalbox}

Here the partial derivative $\pdv{f}{y}$ (Chapter~32) measures how fast the prescribed
slope changes as you move straight up or down across the plane. This theorem
justifies the slope-field picture: continuous, non-crossing
solution curves filling the plane, exactly one through each point. When the
hypotheses fail, several solutions can branch out of the
same point. And even when they hold, a solution can \emph{blow up in finite time}, meaning it shoots off
to infinity as $x$ approaches some finite value. (For example, the solution of
$y'=y^2$, $y(0)=1$, is $y=1/(1-x)$, which blows up as $x\to 1$. Here $f=y^2$ is
smooth, so the theorem still holds; it only promises a solution on \emph{some}
interval around $x_0$.)

\begin{trapbox}
The equation $\dv{y}{x}=\sqrt{\,\abs{y}\,}$ with $y(0)=0$ has \emph{two} solutions
through the origin: the constant $y\equiv 0$, and the curve $y=\tfrac14 x^2$ (for
$x\ge 0$). Uniqueness fails because for $y>0$ we have $\pdv{f}{y} = \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}$,
which blows up as $y\to 0$; the hypothesis that $\pdv{f}{y}$ be continuous fails
exactly at $y=0$. In fact there are infinitely many solutions: for any $c\ge 0$, the
curve that stays at $0$ until $x=c$ and then follows $y=\tfrac14(x-c)^2$ also works
(Figure~\ref{ch37:fig-nonunique}).
If a physical particle obeyed this rate law starting from rest at the origin, it
could sit motionless for an arbitrary length of time and then start moving, with
nothing in the law to say when. Uniqueness is the mathematical condition
that keeps a physical model \emph{deterministic}: the present state fixes the
whole future.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Most first-order ODEs that arise in practice cannot be solved with a formula
at all: $\dv{y}{x}=x^2+y^2$ has no elementary solution. Yet the slope field still
exists, the phase line still tells us about equilibria, and numerical methods
such as Euler's method (take a short step along the current slope, recompute the
slope, repeat) and its more accurate descendants can trace any particular solution
to whatever accuracy we like. Geometry and numerical stepping tell you how
solutions behave even when no closed-form formula exists.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Looking Ahead: The Leap to Second Order}
\label{ch37:sec-looking-ahead}

Every differential equation in this chapter had one feature in common: it tied the
rate of change $\dv{y}{t}$ directly to the state $y$ and the time $t$. A single
integration, accompanied by one initial condition $y(t_0)=y_0$, was always enough
to pin down a unique trajectory. The geometry lived entirely on a one-dimensional line
or in a planar slope field where solution curves could never cross.

Yet many basic laws of physics are second order. When Isaac
Newton formulated his second law of motion, $F = ma$ (force equals mass times
acceleration), he did not declare that
force dictates velocity. He found that force dictates \emph{acceleration}, the
second derivative $\frac{\dd^2 y}{\dd t^2}$.

Once acceleration enters, the mathematics changes.
A moving particle does not only occupy a location; it has momentum and
inertia that resist sudden changes of direction. Because the governing equation involves
$y''$, finding the position function requires \emph{two} successive integrations,
producing two arbitrary constants instead of one. So pinning down a single
physical trajectory takes two initial measurements: where the object starts
($y(0)$), and how fast it is launched ($y'(0)$).

Second derivatives also allow a behavior that no single
first-order autonomous equation can show: \emph{oscillation}. A solution of
$y'=f(y)$ is monotonic (it only ever moves in one direction): it can drift toward
an equilibrium or away from it, but it can never overshoot, double back, and swing
back and forth like a mass on a spring, a pendulum, or a plucked violin string.

In Chapter~38, we turn to \textbf{second-order linear differential
equations}. We will see how inertia and restoring forces give rise to the
mathematics of mechanical vibrations, electrical circuits, and \emph{resonance}:
pushing a system periodically at its natural frequency (the rate at which it
vibrates when left alone) produces very large swings.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}
\textbf{Mental check: equilibrium and cooling without formulas.}
Inspect Newton's cooling equation $\dv{T}{t} = -2(T - 25)$ without integrating or solving.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item What is the equilibrium temperature $T^*$?
\item If hot tea starts at $T(0) = 85^\circ$C, what is the initial sign of $\dv{T}{t}$? Does the tea warm or cool?
\item If chilled lemonade starts at $T(0) = 5^\circ$C, what is the initial sign of $\dv{T}{t}$? Does it warm or cool?
\item What value does $T(t)$ approach as $t\to\infty$, regardless of $T(0)$?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\textbf{Mental check: classifying by eye.}
For each differential equation, state whether it is separable, first-order linear,
autonomous, or none of these (an equation may fit multiple categories):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\dv{y}{x} = x^2 y$
\item $\dv{y}{x} + x y = x^3$
\item $\dv{y}{t} = y(3 - y)$
\item $\dv{y}{x} = x + y$
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Solve the separable equation $\dv{y}{x} = 3x^2 y$ with $y(0)=5$. Identify the lost
constant solution and check whether your general formula recovers it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Solve $\dv{y}{x} = \dfrac{1+y^2}{1+x^2}$. (Hint: both sides integrate to arctangents.)
Express the answer as an implicit relation, then solve for $y$ when $y(0)=1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
A sample of a radioactive isotope decays to $80\%$ of its initial mass in $3$ days.
Find its half-life, and determine how long until only $10\%$ remains.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
A turkey at $5^\circ$C is placed in a $175^\circ$C oven. After $30$ minutes its
internal temperature is $40^\circ$C. Using Newton's law of heating, find the
constant $k$ and predict when the turkey reaches $75^\circ$C.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Solve the linear equation $\dv{y}{x} + \dfrac{1}{x}\,y = \cos x$ for $x>0$ by the
integrating-factor method. (Hint: $\mu = x$, and $\int x\cos x\,\dd x$ needs parts.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Solve $x\,\dv{y}{x} - 2y = x^3 e^{x}$. First put it in standard form, then find the
integrating factor. Show that $\mu = x^{-2}$ and finish the problem.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
For the autonomous equation $\dv{y}{t} = y^3 - y$, find all equilibria, draw the
phase line, and classify each equilibrium as stable or unstable using the sign of
$f'$ at each.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
For the autonomous equation $\dv{y}{t} = (y-1)(y-3)^2$, find the equilibria and
classify them. One of them is \emph{semi-stable} (attracting on one side, repelling
on the other) because $f$ touches zero without changing sign there. Identify which,
and explain using the phase-line arrows.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
A lake with carrying capacity $M=20{,}000$ fish currently holds $4{,}000$ fish and
grows logistically with $r=0.4\ \text{yr}^{-1}$. Write $P(t)$ explicitly, find when
the population reaches $10{,}000$, and find when it is growing fastest.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\textbf{Mixing problem.} A tank holds $100$ liters of pure water. Brine containing
$0.5$ kg of salt per liter flows in at $4$ L/min, and the well-stirred mixture
flows out at the same rate. Let $S(t)$ be the kilograms of salt in the tank. Argue
that $\dv{S}{t} = 2 - \dfrac{4}{100}S$, solve this first-order linear equation with
$S(0)=0$, and find the long-run amount of salt. (Hint: the equilibrium is where
$S'=0$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
Show by direct substitution that the logistic function
$P(t)=\dfrac{M}{1+Ae^{-rt}}$ satisfies $\dv{P}{t}=rP(1-P/M)$. (This verifies the
solution without re-deriving it, a good habit: solving is hard, checking is easy.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
The equation $\dv{y}{x} = x^2 + y^2$ has no elementary closed-form solution. Without
solving it, sketch its slope field for $-2\le x,y\le 2$ by computing the slope at a
handful of points, and describe qualitatively how a solution starting at $(0,0)$
behaves as $x$ increases. (This is the spirit of the final box: geometry without
formulas.)
\end{exercise}
