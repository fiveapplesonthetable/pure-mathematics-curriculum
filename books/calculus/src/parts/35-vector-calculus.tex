\chapter{Vector Calculus: Green, Stokes, and Divergence}

\providecommand{\oiint}{\oint\kern-0.62em\oint}

In Chapter~34 we computed double integrals $\iint_D f\,\dd A$ over flat regions
and triple integrals $\iiint_V f\,\dd V$ over solids. In every case, we chopped
the region into a fine grid of cells and summed a scalar quantity -- density,
height, or charge -- over every cell of the interior.

What happens when the quantity we add up across the interior is not an arbitrary
function, but the \emph{derivative} of some underlying field?

Back in Chapter~21, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) answered this in one
dimension: to add up a rate of change over an interval, you do not need the values
inside the interval at all, only the values at the two endpoints:
\[
  \int_a^b F'(x)\,\dd x \;=\; F(b) - F(a).
\]
All the changes of $F$ inside $[a,b]$ are summarized by two numbers at the
boundary.

That principle -- \emph{the boundary records what happens inside} -- is the central
theme of vector calculus. The three theorems of this chapter (Green's theorem, the
divergence theorem, Stokes' theorem) say the same thing in higher dimensions.
An integral over a solid blob equals an integral over the skin that wraps it. An
integral over a flat patch equals an integral around the loop that fences it. In
each case, integrating a derivative over a region equals integrating the field
itself over the region's boundary, which has one dimension fewer. The FTC is the
one-dimensional version, where the boundary of an interval consists of its two
endpoints.

To state these theorems we use \emph{vector fields}. Up to this point our
functions have assigned numbers to points. A vector field assigns a
\emph{vector} to each point: a velocity at every point of a flowing fluid, a
gravitational or electric force, or the flow of heat. Once we can
integrate vector fields along curves and across surfaces, all the theorems can be
written in the same form.

\begin{focusbox}
One idea, four faces:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FTC:} &\quad \int_a^b F' = F(b)-F(a) &&\text{(1D: endpoints)}\\
\text{Gradient theorem:} &\quad \int_C \grad f\cdot \dd\vec r = f(\text{end})-f(\text{start}) &&\text{(curve: endpoints)}\\
\text{Green / Stokes:} &\quad \iint_S (\text{curl}) = \oint_{\partial S}(\text{field}) &&\text{(patch: boundary loop)}\\
\text{Divergence:} &\quad \iiint_V (\text{div}) = \oiint_{\partial V}(\text{flux}) &&\text{(solid: boundary skin)}
\end{aligned}
\]
Read every line as: \emph{accumulate a derivative inside} $=$ \emph{read the
field on the boundary}. Notation: $\partial S$ (read ``the boundary of $S$'') is
the edge of $S$: the two endpoints of an interval, the loop around a flat patch,
the skin around a solid. A circle on an integral sign, as in $\oint$ or $\oiint$,
means the curve or surface is closed (it has no loose ends or edges). Curl and
div are two kinds of derivative of a vector field, and flux measures how much
field crosses a surface; all are defined in this chapter.
\end{focusbox}

\section{Vector fields: assigning vectors to points}
\label{ch35:fields}

A \emph{scalar field} assigns a number to each point: temperature in a room,
altitude on a map, pressure in the ocean. We have been studying these for three
chapters as functions $f(x,y)$ or $f(x,y,z)$. A \emph{vector field} assigns an
\emph{arrow} -- a vector -- to each point instead.

\begin{definition}[Vector field]
\label{ch35:def-field}
A vector field on a region of the plane is a function
\[
  \vec F(x,y) = \big(P(x,y),\, Q(x,y)\big) = P(x,y)\,\vec i + Q(x,y)\,\vec j,
\]
that assigns to each point $(x,y)$ the vector $\vec F(x,y)$. Here $\vec i=(1,0)$
and $\vec j=(0,1)$ are the unit vectors along the $x$- and $y$-axes, and the
functions $P$ and $Q$ are the \emph{components} of $\vec F$. In three dimensions,
$\vec F(x,y,z) = (P,Q,R)$, with three component functions. We draw it by planting
the arrow $\vec F(x,y)$ with its \emph{tail} at the point $(x,y)$.
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-swirl} (left) shows the field $\vec F(x,y) = (-y, x)$. At each
point $(x,y)$ we draw the arrow $(-y,x)$. This arrow always points ninety degrees
counterclockwise from the \emph{position vector} $(x,y)$, the arrow from the origin
to the point, because $(-y,x)\cdot(x,y)=0$. So the whole field swirls around the
origin like water circling a drain, or a spinning turntable.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Two motions live inside every vector field, and the rest of the chapter is about
measuring them. \textbf{Circulation}: does the field tend to \emph{spin} you
around a loop? (The swirl in Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-swirl}, left, does.) \textbf{Flux}:
does the field tend to \emph{carry stuff out through} a boundary? (The source in
Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-swirl}, right, does.) Curl will measure spin at a single
point; divergence will measure spreading at a single point. The integral theorems
turn these point-by-point measurements into totals over curves, surfaces, and
solids.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think}: Compare the arrows in Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-swirl}.
If you drop a tiny floating twig into the swirl $\vec F = (-y, x)$, it spins around
its own center as it circles the origin. But if you drop the same twig into
the outflow $\vec F = (x, y)$, it is pushed straight outward along a ray,
never turning.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{applicationbox}
Vector fields model physical quantities distributed through space. The wind on a
weather map is $\vec F(x,y)=$ (velocity of air). The gravitational pull near a
planet is a field of arrows pointing inward. The electric field around a charge,
the magnetic field around a wire, the flow of heat through a wall, the current in
the ocean -- each is a vector field, and each obeys laws written in the language
of this chapter. Maxwell's four equations of electromagnetism are statements
about the divergence and curl of electric and magnetic fields.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Line integrals: adding a field along a path}
\label{ch35:lineint}

\subsection{Work, and why we multiply force by displacement}

Drag a box across a floor. The work you do is force times distance -- but only the
\emph{part of the force that lies along the motion} counts. Push straight down on
a box and slide it sideways and you have done no work on it at all; your downward
force is perpendicular to the sideways motion. The right measure of work for a
constant force $\vec F$ over a straight displacement $\vec d$ is the dot product
\[
  W = \vec F \cdot \vec d = \abs{\vec F}\,\abs{\vec d}\cos\theta,
\]
which keeps only the component of $\vec F$ pointing along $\vec d$ and throws
away the perpendicular part. (Here $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec F$ and
$\vec d$; Chapter~33 introduced this form of the dot product.)

Now let the force vary from point to point -- a vector field $\vec F$ -- and let
the path curve. We do what we always do in calculus: chop the path into tiny
nearly-straight pieces, on each piece treat the force as roughly constant, compute
the little bit of work $\vec F\cdot\Delta\vec r$, add them all up, and take the
limit as the pieces shrink. That limit is the \emph{line integral}.

\begin{definition}[Line integral of a vector field]
\label{ch35:def-lineint}
Let $C$ be a curve traced by $\vec r(t)=(x(t),y(t))$ for $t\in[a,b]$ (so as the
parameter $t$ runs from $a$ to $b$, the point $\vec r(t)$ moves along $C$), and let
$\vec F$ be a vector field along it. The line integral of $\vec F$ along $C$ is
\[
  \int_C \vec F\cdot \dd\vec r
  \;=\; \int_a^b \vec F\big(\vec r(t)\big)\cdot \vec r\,'(t)\,\dd t.
\]
Writing $\vec F=(P,Q)$ and $\dd\vec r = (\dd x,\dd y)$, this is also written
$\displaystyle \int_C P\,\dd x + Q\,\dd y$.
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-lineint-tangent} shows one small step of this sum.

\begin{whybox}
Why does $\dd\vec r$ become $\vec r\,'(t)\,\dd t$? Because $\vec r\,'(t)$ is the
velocity vector -- it points along the curve and its length is the speed. Over a
tiny time $\dd t$ the path advances by the little displacement vector
$\dd\vec r = \vec r\,'(t)\,\dd t$. Dotting the force against this displacement
gives the work $\vec F\cdot\dd\vec r$ on that small step, and integrating in $t$
adds these up along the curve.
\end{whybox}

\begin{walkbox}
Why doesn't the speed of traversal change the answer? If you re-parametrize and
move twice as fast, then $\vec r\,'$ doubles but the time interval $\dd t$ halves,
and the product $\vec r\,'(t)\,\dd t = \dd\vec r$ is unchanged -- it is a
displacement in space, independent of the rate of traversal. (Reversing direction, however, flips every
$\dd\vec r$, so it flips the sign of the integral. Orientation matters; speed does
not.)
\end{walkbox}

\subsection{A worked line integral, step by step}

\begin{example}[Work along a parabola]
\label{ch35:ex-parab}
Compute $\displaystyle\int_C \vec F\cdot\dd\vec r$ for $\vec F(x,y)=(y,\,x^2)$
along the parabola $y=x^2$ from $(0,0)$ to $(2,4)$.

\textbf{Step 1 -- parametrize.} Let $x=t$, $y=t^2$, so $\vec r(t)=(t,t^2)$ for
$t\in[0,2]$. Then $\vec r\,'(t)=(1,2t)$.

\textbf{Step 2 -- evaluate the field on the path.}
$\vec F(\vec r(t)) = (y,\,x^2)\big|_{x=t,\,y=t^2} = (t^2,\, t^2)$.

\textbf{Step 3 -- dot.}
$\vec F(\vec r(t))\cdot\vec r\,'(t) = (t^2)(1)+(t^2)(2t) = t^2 + 2t^3$.

\textbf{Step 4 -- integrate.}
\[
  \int_0^2 (t^2 + 2t^3)\,\dd t
  = \left[\frac{t^3}{3} + \frac{t^4}{2}\right]_0^2
  = \frac{8}{3} + 8 = \frac{32}{3}.
\]
The total work is $\tfrac{32}{3}$ (Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-work}). The recipe never
changes: parametrize, plug in, dot, integrate a single ordinary integral in $t$.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
A line integral $\int_C \vec F\cdot\dd\vec r$ is \emph{not} the same as the
arc-length integral $\int_C f\,\dd s$, which adds up a number $f$ times the length
$\dd s$ of each small piece of the curve (for example, the total mass of a wire whose
density is $f$). The dot product version measures the field's component \emph{along} the curve and is
sensitive to direction; reversing the path negates it. The arc-length version uses
$\dd s = \abs{\vec r\,'(t)}\,\dd t$ (a length, always positive) and does not care
about direction. They answer different questions. This chapter is almost entirely
about the dot-product kind.
\end{trapbox}

\section{Conservative fields: when the path does not matter}
\label{ch35:conservative}

In Example~\ref{ch35:ex-parab} the answer depended on the curve we chose. For some
special fields it does \emph{not}: the work depends only on where you start and
where you stop, never on the route between. These are the \emph{conservative}
fields, and they are exactly the fields that are gradients.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental Theorem for line integrals / Gradient theorem]
\label{ch35:thm-gradient}
If $\vec F = \grad f$ for some scalar function $f$ (a \emph{potential}), then for
any curve $C$ from point $A$ to point $B$,
\[
  \int_C \grad f\cdot \dd\vec r = f(B) - f(A).
\]
The integral depends only on the endpoints. In particular, around any closed loop
($A=B$), $\displaystyle\oint_C \grad f\cdot\dd\vec r = 0$. (The circle on
$\oint$ marks an integral around a closed loop.)
\end{theorem}

\begin{whybox}
This is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus applied along a curve. Parametrize $C$ by
$\vec r(t)$, $t\in[a,b]$; the chain rule does the rest:
\begin{align*}
  \int_C \grad f\cdot\dd\vec r
  &= \int_a^b \grad f(\vec r(t))\cdot \vec r\,'(t)\,\dd t\\
  &= \int_a^b \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Big[f(\vec r(t))\Big]\,\dd t
  && \text{(chain rule)}\\
  &= f(\vec r(b)) - f(\vec r(a)) && \text{(FTC)}.
\end{align*}
The middle equality is the multivariable chain rule (Chapter~33):
$\grad f\cdot\vec r\,' = \dv{}{t}f(\vec r(t))$. So the integrand is the
derivative of $f(\vec r(t))$, and the ordinary FTC finishes it.
\end{whybox}

\begin{intuitionbox}
A potential $f$ is an \emph{altitude}. The field $\grad f$ points straight uphill,
and its work is the height you gain. Walk any trail from the trailhead to the
summit -- switchbacks, scenic detours, whatever -- and the elevation gained is the
same, because it is (summit altitude) minus (trailhead altitude). Walk a loop
back to where you started and you gain zero net altitude. A field is conservative
precisely when it has such an altitude function. Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-gradient}
draws one example.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{How to test for, and find, a potential}

In the plane, write $\vec F=(P,Q)$. If $\vec F=\grad f=(f_x,f_y)$ then
$P=f_x$ and $Q=f_y$. By Clairaut's theorem (mixed partials commute, Chapter~32),
\[
  \pdv{P}{y} = f_{xy} = f_{yx} = \pdv{Q}{x}.
\]
So a \emph{necessary} condition is $P_y = Q_x$: every conservative field passes
this test. On a region with no holes this condition is also \emph{sufficient}: if
the test is passed, a potential exists. Such a region is called \emph{simply
connected}: every closed loop in it can be shrunk to a point without leaving the
region (Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-simply-connected}).

\label{ch35:fig-simply-connected}

\begin{example}[Finding a potential]
\label{ch35:ex-potential}
Is $\vec F=(2xy + 3,\; x^2 - 1)$ conservative? Here $P=2xy+3$, $Q=x^2-1$.

\textbf{Test.} $P_y = 2x$ and $Q_x = 2x$. They agree, and the plane has no holes,
so a potential exists.

\textbf{Build it.} We need $f$ with $f_x = 2xy+3$. Integrate in $x$ (treating $y$
as constant):
\[
  f(x,y) = x^2 y + 3x + g(y),
\]
where the ``constant'' of integration is allowed to depend on $y$. Now we need
$f_y = Q$:
\begin{align*}
  f_y = x^2 + g'(y) &= x^2 - 1,\\
  \text{so}\quad g'(y) &= -1 \quad\text{and}\quad g(y) = -y + C.
\end{align*}
So $f(x,y) = x^2y + 3x - y + C$. As a check, $\grad f = (2xy+3,\; x^2-1)=\vec F$.
Now any line integral of $\vec F$ is easy: from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$ it is
$f(1,2)-f(0,0) = (2 + 3 - 2) - 0 = 3$, no matter the path.
\end{example}

\begin{trapbox}
The hole matters. The swirl
$\vec F = \big(\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\big)$ satisfies $P_y=Q_x$
\emph{everywhere it is defined}, yet $\oint \vec F\cdot\dd\vec r = 2\pi \ne 0$
around the unit circle. It is \emph{not} conservative. The catch: it is undefined
at the origin, so its domain has a hole, and the loop around that hole cannot be
shrunk to a point (Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-simply-connected}, right). The test
$P_y=Q_x$ only promises a potential on a \emph{simply connected} domain. Here the
shape of the domain, and not only the formula for the field, decides the answer.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
The word ``conservative'' comes from physics, not bookkeeping. If $\vec F$ is a
force with potential energy $U$ (so $\vec F=-\grad U$), then the work done equals
the drop in $U$, and the total mechanical energy (kinetic plus potential) stays
constant along any trajectory -- energy is \emph{conserved}. Gravity
and electrostatic forces are conservative; friction is not (it bleeds energy away as
heat, and its closed-loop integral is nonzero, which is why every real machine
eventually runs down). The nineteenth-century effort to understand which physical
forces conserve mechanical energy, led by Hermann von Helmholtz and Lord
Kelvin, is what led mathematicians to clarify when a vector field is the
gradient of an altitude function.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Green's theorem: interior curl equals boundary circulation}
\label{ch35:green}

Green's theorem relates a line integral \emph{around} a closed loop $C$ to a double
integral \emph{over} the flat region $D$ that the loop encloses.

\begin{theorem}[Green's theorem, circulation form]
\label{ch35:thm-green}
Let $D$ be a region in the plane bounded by a simple closed curve $C$ (a loop that
does not cross itself), traversed counterclockwise, so that $D$ is on your left as
you walk along $C$. If $\vec F=(P,Q)$ has continuous partial derivatives on $D$, then
\[
  \oint_C P\,\dd x + Q\,\dd y \;=\; \iint_D \left(\pdv{Q}{x} - \pdv{P}{y}\right)\dd A.
\]
\end{theorem}

The quantity $\pdv{Q}{x}-\pdv{P}{y}$ measures circulation at a single point: it is
the ``curl'' of the field, which we meet properly in the next section. Green's theorem
says: the total swirl around the boundary equals the sum of all the little swirls
inside.

\begin{happeningbox}
The reason is a telescoping cancellation (Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-green-cancel}).
Tile the region $D$ with tiny
rectangles. For each tiny rectangle, run the line integral around its own little
boundary, counterclockwise. Now add up all these little loop integrals. Look at
any internal edge shared by two adjacent tiles: one tile traverses it
in one direction, its neighbor traverses the \emph{same} edge in the opposite direction. The two
contributions are equal and opposite -- they \textbf{cancel}. The only edges that survive this
cancellation are those on the outer boundary of $D$, where there is no adjacent tile
to cancel with. So
\[
  \sum_{\text{tiles}} (\text{tiny loop integral}) = (\text{big boundary integral}).
\]
Meanwhile each tiny loop integral equals
$\big(Q_x - P_y\big)\times(\text{area of tile})$; Section~\ref{ch35:curl-div}
does this short computation. Summing those is the double
integral. The interior cancellation is the same telescoping that
made the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus work, where the inner endpoints of
adjacent subintervals cancelled and only $a$ and $b$ survived.
\end{happeningbox}

\subsection{Computing area with Green's theorem}

As a first application, choose $\vec F$ so that $Q_x - P_y = 1$. Then the double
integral is the \emph{area} of $D$, and Green's theorem computes area from a
trip around the boundary. Taking $\vec F=(-y/2,\,x/2)$ gives
$Q_x-P_y=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$, so
\[
  \text{Area}(D) = \frac{1}{2}\oint_C x\,\dd y - y\,\dd x.
\]
This is the principle behind the \emph{planimeter}, a mechanical instrument that
measures the area of any shape by tracing its outline: guide its pointer once
around the boundary and a dial reads off the enclosed area.

\begin{example}[Area of an ellipse by its boundary]
\label{ch35:ex-ellipse}
Let $C$ be the ellipse $x=a\cos t$, $y=b\sin t$, $t\in[0,2\pi]$. Then
$\dd x = -a\sin t\,\dd t$, $\dd y = b\cos t\,\dd t$, and
\begin{align*}
  x\,\dd y - y\,\dd x
  &= (a\cos t)(b\cos t)\,\dd t - (b\sin t)(-a\sin t)\,\dd t\\
  &= ab(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t)\,\dd t = ab\,\dd t.
\end{align*}
Therefore
\[
  \text{Area} = \frac12\int_0^{2\pi} ab\,\dd t = \frac12\,(ab)(2\pi) = \pi ab.
\]
This is the familiar formula $\pi a b$, found using only the boundary curve.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Verifying Green's theorem directly]
\label{ch35:ex-green-verify}
Let $\vec F=(-y,\,x)$ (the swirl) and let $D$ be the unit disk, $C$ its boundary
circle counterclockwise.

\textbf{Right side (double integral).} Here $P=-y$, $Q=x$, so
$Q_x - P_y = 1 - (-1) = 2$. Thus
\[
  \iint_D 2\,\dd A = 2\cdot(\text{area of disk}) = 2\pi.
\]

\textbf{Left side (line integral).} Parametrize $x=\cos t$, $y=\sin t$:
\begin{align*}
  \oint_C -y\,\dd x + x\,\dd y
  &= \int_0^{2\pi}\big[(-\sin t)(-\sin t) + (\cos t)(\cos t)\big]\,\dd t\\
  &= \int_0^{2\pi} 1\,\dd t = 2\pi.
\end{align*}
Both sides give $2\pi$. The constant swirl of $2$ at every point,
summed over the disk, equals the circulation you feel going once around the rim.
\end{example}

\section{Curl and divergence: spin and spread, made local}
\label{ch35:curl-div}

Two derivatives of a vector field appear inside the integral theorems:
divergence, which measures spreading at a point, and curl, which measures spin at a
point. This section defines each one and says what it measures physically.

\subsection{Divergence: how much a point is a source}

\begin{definition}[Divergence]
\label{ch35:def-div}
For $\vec F=(P,Q,R)$, the divergence is the scalar
\[
  \operatorname{div}\vec F = \grad\cdot\vec F = \pdv{P}{x} + \pdv{Q}{y} + \pdv{R}{z}.
\]
(In the plane, drop the last term.) The notation $\grad\cdot\vec F$ is a memory
aid: treat $\grad = \big(\pdv{}{x},\pdv{}{y},\pdv{}{z}\big)$ as if it were a vector
and take its dot product with $\vec F$.
\end{definition}

\begin{intuitionbox}
Divergence measures \emph{spreading}. Imagine $\vec F$ is the velocity of a fluid.
Surround a point with a tiny balloon. If more fluid flows \emph{out} of the balloon
than in, the point is a \emph{source} and $\operatorname{div}\vec F>0$. If fluid piles
\emph{in}, it is a \emph{sink} and $\operatorname{div}\vec F<0$. If as much leaves as
enters, $\operatorname{div}\vec F=0$ and the fluid is \emph{incompressible} there.
For the source field $\vec F=(x,y)$ of Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-swirl} (right),
$\operatorname{div}\vec F = 1+1 = 2 > 0$ everywhere: fluid is being created at
every point. For the
swirl $(-y,x)$, $\operatorname{div}\vec F = 0$: swirling moves fluid around but never
makes it pile up or thin out.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{Curl: how much a point is a whirlpool}

\begin{definition}[Curl]
\label{ch35:def-curl}
In three dimensions, $\operatorname{curl}\vec F = \grad\times\vec F$ is the vector
\[
  \operatorname{curl}\vec F =
  \left(\pdv{R}{y}-\pdv{Q}{z},\;\; \pdv{P}{z}-\pdv{R}{x},\;\; \pdv{Q}{x}-\pdv{P}{y}\right).
\]
In the plane, only the last component survives; the \emph{scalar curl} of
$(P,Q)$ is $\pdv{Q}{x}-\pdv{P}{y}$ -- the integrand of Green's theorem.
\end{definition}

The symbol $\times$ is the \emph{cross product}, a way of multiplying two
three-dimensional vectors that produces a third vector perpendicular to both. In
$\grad\times\vec F$ it is again only a memory aid; the component formula above is
all you need.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Curl measures \emph{spin}. Drop a tiny paddlewheel into the fluid at a point. If
the flow makes it rotate, the curl is nonzero. The curl vector points along the
axle, in the direction given by the \emph{right-hand rule}: curl the fingers of
your right hand in the direction of the spin, and your thumb points along the curl.
Its length is twice the angular speed of the spin. The
swirl $(-y,x)$ has scalar curl $1-(-1)=2$ everywhere -- a paddlewheel anywhere in
it spins counterclockwise. The source $(x,y)$ has curl $0-0=0$: gas rushing
straight outward never spins a paddlewheel; it carries it straight out.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{trapbox}
A field can \emph{circle the origin} and still have zero curl everywhere it is
defined: ``curls around the origin'' (a property of big loops) and ``has nonzero
curl'' (a property of single points) are different. The
hole field $\big(\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2},\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\big)$ has scalar curl $0$ at
every point of its domain, yet its loop integral around the origin is $2\pi$.
Curl is a local paddlewheel test; circulation around a big loop is a global sum.
Green's theorem links them only when the field is smooth \emph{everywhere inside}
the loop -- and this field is not (it blows up at the origin).
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Where the formulas come from: one tiny rectangle}

Why is divergence $\pdv{P}{x} + \pdv{Q}{y}$? Why is curl $\pdv{Q}{x} - \pdv{P}{y}$,
with a minus sign? Neither formula is arbitrary. Both come from doing the
bookkeeping around one tiny rectangle $[x, x+\Delta x] \times [y, y+\Delta y]$ of
area $\Delta A=\Delta x\,\Delta y$ (Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-div-curl-box}).

\textbf{Flux out through the four walls.} Think of $\vec F$ as the velocity of a
fluid. Only the horizontal component $P$ carries fluid across a vertical wall, and
the amount per unit time is that component times the wall's length. So fluid leaves
through the right wall at rate about $P(x+\Delta x,y)\,\Delta y$ and enters through
the left wall at rate about $P(x,y)\,\Delta y$. The net horizontal outflow is
$[P(x+\Delta x,y)-P(x,y)]\,\Delta y\approx P_x\,\Delta x\,\Delta y$, by the
definition of the partial derivative. The top and bottom walls give, in the same
way, $[Q(x,y+\Delta y)-Q(x,y)]\,\Delta x\approx Q_y\,\Delta x\,\Delta y$. The total
outward flux is about $(P_x+Q_y)\,\Delta A$; per unit area, that is
$\operatorname{div}\vec F=P_x+Q_y$.

\textbf{Circulation around the four edges.} Walk counterclockwise. On each edge the
contribution is the component of $\vec F$ along the direction of walking times the
edge's length:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  &\text{east along the bottom:} && +P(x,y)\,\Delta x, &\qquad
  &\text{north up the right side:} && +Q(x+\Delta x,y)\,\Delta y,\\
  &\text{\emph{west} along the top:} && -P(x,y+\Delta y)\,\Delta x, &\qquad
  &\text{\emph{south} down the left side:} && -Q(x,y)\,\Delta y.
\end{aligned}
\]
Pair opposite sides:
\[
  \big[Q(x+\Delta x,y)-Q(x,y)\big]\Delta y-\big[P(x,y+\Delta y)-P(x,y)\big]\Delta x
  \;\approx\;(Q_x-P_y)\,\Delta x\,\Delta y .
\]
The minus sign in $Q_x-P_y$ comes from the fact that a counterclockwise loop walks
east along the bottom but west along the top.

\begin{aestheticbox}
Green's theorem in \emph{circulation form}
($\oint P\,\dd x+Q\,\dd y = \iint (Q_x-P_y)$) integrates the \emph{curl}. There is
a twin, the \emph{flux form}, that integrates the \emph{divergence}:
\[
  \oint_C \vec F\cdot \vec n\,\dd s = \iint_D \left(\pdv{P}{x}+\pdv{Q}{y}\right)\dd A
  = \iint_D \operatorname{div}\vec F\,\dd A,
\]
where $\vec n$ is the \emph{outward unit normal}: at each point of $C$, the vector
of length $1$ perpendicular (``normal'') to the curve and pointing out of $D$. Here
$\dd s$ is the length of a small piece of $C$, so $\vec F\cdot\vec n\,\dd s$ is the
amount of field crossing that piece. The same Green's theorem, read two ways,
is the seed of \emph{both} theorems to come: extending its circulation form into
3D gives Stokes' theorem; extending its flux form into 3D gives the divergence
theorem.
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Flux and the divergence theorem: the boundary skin}
\label{ch35:divthm}

\subsection{Flux across a surface}

In 3D the analog of ``how much field crosses the boundary loop'' becomes ``how much
field crosses the boundary \emph{surface}.'' That quantity is the \emph{flux}.

\begin{definition}[Flux integral]
\label{ch35:def-flux}
Let $S$ be a surface with a chosen unit normal $\vec n$: at each point, a vector
of length $1$ perpendicular to the surface. (Every surface has two such choices,
one on each side; picking one is called \emph{orienting} the surface.) The flux of
$\vec F$ through $S$ is
\[
  \iint_S \vec F\cdot\vec n\,\dd S.
\]
Only the component of $\vec F$ \emph{piercing} the surface (along $\vec n$)
contributes; field sliding \emph{along} the surface carries nothing through.
\end{definition}

Here $\dd S$ is the area of a tiny patch of the surface. In particular, if
$\vec F\cdot\vec n$ has the same value $c$ everywhere on $S$, the flux is
$c\cdot\text{Area}(S)$.

\begin{intuitionbox}
Hold a hoop in a river. The flux is the volume of water passing through the hoop
per second. Hold it face-on to the current and the flux is large; turn it edge-on
and the flux drops to zero, because now the water slides past the rim instead of
through the hoop. That ``how much is going through'' -- counting only the piercing
component $\vec F\cdot\vec n$ -- is flux. Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-flux-hoop} shows the
three cases.
\end{intuitionbox}

\subsection{The divergence theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Divergence theorem / Gauss's theorem]
\label{ch35:thm-div}
Let $V$ be a solid region in space bounded by a closed surface $S=\partial V$ (a
surface with no edges that encloses the solid, like a sphere around a ball), with
outward unit normal $\vec n$. If $\vec F$ has continuous partial derivatives on $V$, then
\[
  \iiint_V \operatorname{div}\vec F\,\dd V \;=\; \oiint_{S}\vec F\cdot\vec n\,\dd S.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{happeningbox}
The proof is the same cancellation story, one dimension up. Chop the solid $V$ into
tiny boxes. Compute the net flux out of each tiny box -- its outward push minus its
inward pull on all six faces. Now sum over all the boxes. Every \emph{internal}
face is shared by two adjacent boxes: what flows \emph{out} of one box through that
face flows \emph{into} its neighbor, so the two flux contributions are equal and
opposite and \textbf{cancel}. Only the faces on the \emph{outer skin} of $V$
survive, with no neighbor to cancel them -- and their total is the flux through
$S=\partial V$. Meanwhile the net flux out of one tiny box equals
$\operatorname{div}\vec F\times(\text{box volume})$, because divergence is
outflow per unit volume (the three-dimensional version of the rectangle computation
in Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-div-curl-box}). Summing gives
$\iiint_V \operatorname{div}\vec F\,\dd V$. Interior fluxes cancel; the boundary
records the net source inside (Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-div}).
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
The divergence theorem is a conservation law written in geometric form: if nothing
piles up inside a solid, everything produced by the sources inside must leave
through its surface. This one identity underlies Gauss's law of electrostatics (the
electric flux out of a closed surface measures the charge inside), the continuity
equation of fluid flow (mass is neither created nor destroyed), and the energy
balance behind the heat equation (heat flowing out of a region equals heat lost
inside it).
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{example}[Divergence theorem on a ball]
\label{ch35:ex-div}
Let $\vec F=(x,y,z)$ and let $V$ be the ball of radius $a$, with $S$ its sphere.

\textbf{Left side.} $\operatorname{div}\vec F = 1+1+1 = 3$, so
\[
  \iiint_V 3\,\dd V = 3\cdot \text{Vol}(V) = 3\cdot\frac{4}{3}\pi a^3 = 4\pi a^3.
\]

\textbf{Right side.} On the sphere of radius $a$ the outward normal is
$\vec n = \frac{1}{a}(x,y,z)$, so
$\vec F\cdot\vec n = \frac{1}{a}(x^2+y^2+z^2) = \frac{a^2}{a} = a$, a constant.
Then
\[
  \oiint_S \vec F\cdot\vec n\,\dd S = a\cdot \text{Area}(S) = a\cdot 4\pi a^2 = 4\pi a^3.
\]
The two agree. The constant divergence $3$ throughout the ball is balanced exactly
by the outward flux through the sphere.
\end{example}

\section{Stokes' theorem: the boundary loop of a surface}
\label{ch35:stokes}

Green's theorem lives in the flat plane. Stokes' theorem lifts it into 3D, where
the surface $S$ may be any curved membrane in space, like a soap film, with the line
integral run around its rim $\partial S$.

\begin{theorem}[Stokes' theorem]
\label{ch35:thm-stokes}
Let $S$ be an oriented surface in space (one with a chosen unit normal $\vec n$)
bounded by a simple closed curve $C=\partial S$. Walk around $C$ in the direction
that matches $\vec n$ by the right-hand rule: with your right thumb along $\vec n$,
your fingers curl in the direction of travel
(Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-stokes-orient}). If $\vec F$ has continuous partial
derivatives, then
\[
  \oint_C \vec F\cdot\dd\vec r \;=\; \iint_S (\operatorname{curl}\vec F)\cdot\vec n\,\dd S.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
The total circulation you feel marching once around the rim of a membrane equals
the sum of all the little paddlewheel spins (the curl) spread across the membrane.
It follows that \emph{any} surface with the same rim gives the same answer
(Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-stokes}): a flat disk, a tall dome, a curved sheet -- if they
share a boundary loop, the flux of the curl through each is the same, since each
equals the same rim integral. (A second reason: one can check that
$\operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl}\vec F)=0$ for every field, so the curl has
no sources, and by the divergence theorem its net flux out of the closed surface
formed by two caps is zero.) Green's theorem is Stokes' theorem for the special
case where $S$ is a flat region lying in the plane.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{happeningbox}
The same cancellation mechanism applies on a curved surface. Tile the membrane $S$ with tiny patches. Around each
patch run the local circulation; by Green's theorem in miniature, that circulation
equals $(\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot\vec n)\times(\text{patch area})$.
Sum over all patches. The shared edges between neighboring patches are traversed in
opposite directions and \textbf{cancel}; only the outer rim $C=\partial S$ survives.
Result: rim circulation $=$ total flux of the curl.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{example}[Stokes on a hemisphere]
\label{ch35:ex-stokes}
Let $\vec F=(-y,x,0)$ (the swirl, lifted into 3D) and let $S$ be the upper unit
hemisphere $x^2+y^2+z^2=1$, $z\ge0$, oriented upward. Its boundary $C$ is the unit
circle in the $xy$-plane.

\textbf{Surface side.}
$\operatorname{curl}\vec F = (0,0,\,Q_x-P_y)=(0,0,1-(-1))=(0,0,2)$.
The simplest membrane with the same rim is the flat disk $z=0$, where
$\vec n=(0,0,1)$, so $(\operatorname{curl}\vec F)\cdot\vec n = 2$ and
\[
  \iint_S (\operatorname{curl}\vec F)\cdot\vec n\,\dd S = 2\cdot(\text{area of unit disk}) = 2\pi.
\]
(We are allowed to swap the hemisphere for the flat disk because Stokes
says only the shared rim matters.)

\textbf{Rim side.} Parametrize $C$ by $x=\cos t$, $y=\sin t$, $z=0$:
\begin{align*}
  \oint_C \vec F\cdot\dd\vec r
  &= \int_0^{2\pi}\big[(-\sin t)(-\sin t)+(\cos t)(\cos t)\big]\,\dd t\\
  &= \int_0^{2\pi} 1\,\dd t = 2\pi.
\end{align*}
Both give $2\pi$. The curl pointing up through the cap matches the swirl you
feel walking the equator.
\end{example}

\begin{historybox}
Vector calculus began with a miller in Nottingham. In 1828, George Green, a
self-taught miller who had only about one year of formal schooling as a child,
published a private essay on electricity and magnetism. Green was trying to solve a concrete puzzle: if electrical charges or
heat sources are buried invisibly inside a solid body, how can you deduce what they
are doing using only measurements taken by meters placed on the outside skin?

At the time, mathematicians worked in coordinate components. They wrote
out separate formulas for the $x$, $y$, and $z$ directions. Modern vector
notation did not exist: neither dot products nor cross products, nor the symbols
$\grad$, $\operatorname{div}$, and $\operatorname{curl}$, had been invented yet. Green
realized that the sum of small changes inside an enclosure must cancel along
every interior boundary, leaving only a reckoning at the outer edge.

Because Green was an unknown outsider, his self-funded booklet was sold to only 51
subscribers, mostly local friends, and was completely ignored by the scientific
world. In 1845, a young physicist named William
Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) found a surviving copy, saw its
importance, and circulated it across Europe. Five years later, in 1850, Thomson wrote a
letter to his friend George Gabriel Stokes containing the three-dimensional version
of the boundary principle on a curved surface. Stokes never published a proof; instead,
he set it as Question~8 on the 1854 Cambridge Smith's Prize examination, an advanced
mathematics competition. Sitting that very exam was an undergraduate named James Clerk
Maxwell.

Maxwell later made curl and divergence central to his
equations of electromagnetism, which showed that light is an
electromagnetic wave. A debate followed over how to write these
ideas. Peter Guthrie Tait championed four-dimensional numbers called quaternions,
debating with Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside,
who argued for the three-dimensional vectors used today.
In the early twentieth century, Henri Poincar\'e and \'Elie Cartan unified these results into
the generalized Stokes theorem $\int_{\partial M}\omega = \int_M \dd\omega$,
where $\omega$ is a \emph{differential form} (an expression such as $P\,\dd x + Q\,\dd y$
that is built to be integrated over curves, surfaces, or solids)
and $\partial M$ is the boundary of a smooth region $M$.

Basic questions about fluid flow are still open. The Navier--Stokes equations
describe flowing water and air. Whether their solutions in three dimensions always
stay smooth for all time, or whether the curl of the velocity field (its local
whirlpool spin) can concentrate and become infinite after a finite time, is
unknown; it is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a
one-million-dollar prize.
\end{historybox}

\section{One theorem, four faces}
\label{ch35:unification}

These theorems share a common structure. In each statement, an integral of a
\emph{derivative} over a region equals an integral of the original \emph{field}
over the region's \emph{boundary}:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Theorem} & \textbf{Inside (a derivative)} & \textbf{Boundary (the field)} \\
\midrule
FTC & $\displaystyle\int_a^b F'(x)\,\dd x$ & $F(b)-F(a)$ \\
Gradient thm & $\displaystyle\int_C \grad f\cdot\dd\vec r$ & $f(B)-f(A)$ \\
Green & $\displaystyle\iint_D (Q_x-P_y)\,\dd A$ & $\displaystyle\oint_{\partial D}\vec F\cdot\dd\vec r$ \\
Stokes & $\displaystyle\iint_S \operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot\vec n\,\dd S$ & $\displaystyle\oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\dd\vec r$ \\
Divergence & $\displaystyle\iiint_V \operatorname{div}\vec F\,\dd V$ & $\displaystyle\oiint_{\partial V}\vec F\cdot\vec n\,\dd S$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{aestheticbox}
Taking the boundary $\partial$ lowers dimension by one: the boundary of a solid is
a surface, of a surface a curve, of a curve two endpoints. In each row, the
derivative taken inside ($F'$, $\grad$, curl, div) is the one whose integral the
boundary can account for. Every row of the table, from $\int F' = F(b)-F(a)$
onward, is a special case of a single formula, the generalized Stokes theorem
$\int_M \dd\omega = \int_{\partial M}\omega$ from the history box: integrating the
derivative $\dd\omega$ over a region $M$ equals integrating $\omega$ over the
boundary $\partial M$.
\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Physics uses these theorems constantly. Two of Maxwell's equations say
$\operatorname{div}\vec E = \rho/\eps_0$ (charges are sources of the
electric field) and $\operatorname{curl}\vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \cdots$ (currents
swirl the magnetic field). Here $\vec E$ is the electric field, $\vec B$ the
magnetic field, $\rho$ the charge per unit volume, $\vec J$ the electric current
per unit area, $\eps_0$ and $\mu_0$ are physical constants, and the omitted term
accounts for changing electric fields. Feed these into the divergence and Stokes
theorems and out come Gauss's law (the electric flux out of a closed surface is
proportional to the charge inside) and Amp\`ere's law (the circulation of $\vec B$
around a loop is proportional to the current through it). These boundary forms are
the ones you can measure with a wire and a meter. The law at each point and the law
over a whole region are the same statement seen from inside and from the boundary.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{labbox}
Verify the unification on a concrete example. Take $\vec F=(-y,x)$.
(1) Compute its scalar curl. (2) Compute its divergence. (3) Use Green's theorem to
find its circulation around the boundary of the square $[0,1]\times[0,1]$, then
check it by integrating around the four edges directly. (4) Now compute its
\emph{flux} out of that same square using the flux form of Green's theorem -- and
explain, before you compute, why you expect $0$. (The swirl spins but does not
spread.)
\end{labbox}

\section{The Bridge to Complex Analysis: Analytic Functions, Cauchy--Riemann, and Contour Integrals}
\label{ch35:bridge-complex-analysis}

Green's theorem has a further use in two dimensions, when we treat the plane $\R^2$ as the complex plane $\C$ of Chapter~30, writing the point $(x,y)$ as the complex number $z = x + iy$.

Green's theorem then becomes a starting point for \emph{complex analysis}, the calculus of functions of a complex variable. In this subject a function with one complex derivative on an open region automatically has derivatives of every order, and difficult definite integrals can be evaluated by counting how often a path winds around the function's \emph{singularities}. A singularity is a point where the function fails to be differentiable, typically because a denominator vanishes there; it is \emph{isolated} if there are no other singularities close to it.

\subsection{Complex Differentiability and the Amplitwist}

A complex function $f: \C \to \C$ maps an input point $z = x + iy$ to an output $w = u(x,y) + iv(x,y)$, where $u, v: \R^2 \to \R$ are its real and imaginary parts. 

As in single-variable calculus, the complex derivative of $f$ at a point $z_0$ is defined as the limit of a difference quotient:
\begin{equation}
f'(z_0) = \lim_{\Delta z \to 0} \frac{f(z_0 + \Delta z) - f(z_0)}{\Delta z}.
\label{ch35:eq-complex-derivative}
\end{equation}
In single-variable real calculus, the increment $\Delta x$ can approach zero from only two directions: left or right. In the complex plane, however, $\Delta z = \Delta x + i\Delta y$ can approach zero from any direction, or along any curve. For the complex derivative $f'(z_0)$ to exist, the quotient must approach the same complex number whichever way $\Delta z$ approaches zero.

Compare two perpendicular directions of approach (Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-cr-approach}):

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[>={Stealth[length=5pt]}]
  \draw[->, gray!60] (-2.2,0) -- (2.4,0) node[right, font=\small, text=black] {$x$ (real part)};
  \draw[->, gray!60] (0,-1.6) -- (0,2.0) node[above, font=\small, text=black] {$y$ (imaginary part)};
  \fill[black] (0,0) circle (2pt) node[below right, font=\small] {$z_0$};
  % horizontal approach
  \foreach \xx in {1.8,1.2,0.7,0.35}{ \fill[curveblue] (\xx,0) circle (1.5pt); }
  \draw[->, curveblue, very thick] (1.95,0.25) -- (0.35,0.25);
  \node[curveblue, font=\small, anchor=south] at (1.2,0.35) {$\Delta z = \Delta x$};
  % vertical approach
  \foreach \yy in {1.6,1.05,0.6,0.3}{ \fill[tangentred] (0,\yy) circle (1.5pt); }
  \draw[->, tangentred, very thick] (-0.25,1.7) -- (-0.25,0.3);
  \node[tangentred, font=\small, anchor=east] at (-0.35,1.05) {$\Delta z = i\,\Delta y$};
  % some other direction
  \draw[->, gray, thick, dashed] (-1.6,-1.2) -- (-0.25,-0.19);

\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{The point $z_0+\Delta z$ can approach $z_0$ horizontally (blue), vertically
(red), or from any other direction. A complex derivative exists only if the
difference quotient has the same limit along every route. Comparing the blue and red
routes gives the Cauchy--Riemann equations.}
\label{ch35:fig-cr-approach}
\end{figure}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Horizontal approach} ($\Delta z = \Delta x \in \R$, so $\Delta y = 0$):
\begin{align*}
f'(z) &= \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{u(x+\Delta x, y) - u(x,y)}{\Delta x} + i \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{v(x+\Delta x, y) - v(x,y)}{\Delta x}\\
&= u_x + i v_x.
\end{align*}
\item \textbf{Vertical approach} ($\Delta z = i\Delta y$ with $\Delta y \in \R$, so $\Delta x = 0$):
\begin{align*}
f'(z) &= \lim_{\Delta y \to 0} \frac{u(x, y+\Delta y) - u(x,y)}{i\Delta y} + i \lim_{\Delta y \to 0} \frac{v(x, y+\Delta y) - v(x,y)}{i\Delta y}\\
&= \frac{u_y + i v_y}{i}.
\end{align*}
Multiplying numerator and denominator by $-i$ (recalling that $1/i = -i$):
\[
\frac{u_y + i v_y}{i} = -i(u_y + i v_y) = v_y - i u_y.
\]
\end{enumerate}

For $f'(z)$ to exist, these two directional limits must be identical:
\[
u_x + i v_x = v_y - i u_y.
\]
Equating real and imaginary parts gives the \textbf{Cauchy--Riemann equations}:
\begin{equation}
u_x = v_y \qquad \text{and} \qquad u_y = -v_x.
\label{ch35:eq-cauchy-riemann}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Geometric View: Pure Rotation and Scaling}

What do the Cauchy--Riemann equations mean geometrically?

View $f$ as a mapping from $\R^2$ to $\R^2$, sending $(x,y)$ to $(u(x,y), v(x,y))$. When $u$ and $v$ are differentiable in the sense of Chapter~33 (for instance, when their first partials are continuous), a small step $(\dd x,\dd y)$ near a point is sent to approximately the step $(\dd u,\dd v)$ with $\dd u = u_x\,\dd x + u_y\,\dd y$ and $\dd v = v_x\,\dd x + v_y\,\dd y$. The four partial derivatives are collected in the $2 \times 2$ \emph{Jacobian matrix} (whose determinant is the Jacobian of Chapter~34):
\[
J = \begin{pmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Imposing the Cauchy--Riemann equations $v_y = u_x$ and $u_y = -v_x$, the Jacobian specializes to:
\[
J = \begin{pmatrix} u_x & -v_x \\ v_x & u_x \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Let $r = \sqrt{u_x^2 + v_x^2} = \abs{f'(z)}$, and let $\theta = \operatorname{Arg}(u_x + i v_x) = \operatorname{Arg}(f'(z))$, where $\operatorname{Arg}(w)$ is the angle from the positive real axis to the point $w$, measured counterclockwise. Then $u_x = r\cos\theta$ and $v_x = r\sin\theta$, and the matrix factors as
\[
J = r \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The matrix with $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ entries rotates every vector counterclockwise by the angle $\theta$. So near each point, $f$ acts as a rotation by $\theta$ combined with a scaling by the factor $r$, the same in every direction. A general $2\times 2$ matrix can also stretch one direction more than another or shear a square into a slanted parallelogram; the Cauchy--Riemann equations rule that out.

Tristan Needham, in his book \emph{Visual Complex Analysis}, calls this an \textbf{amplitwist}: the complex derivative \emph{amplifies} lengths by $r = \abs{f'(z)}$ and \emph{twists} directions by the angle $\theta = \operatorname{Arg}(f'(z))$ (Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-amplitwist}). A function that is complex-differentiable at every point of an open region is called \emph{holomorphic}. Because all directions are scaled equally and rotated by the same angle, the angle between any two curves is preserved; maps with this property are called \emph{conformal} (angle-preserving). So every holomorphic map is conformal wherever $f'(z)\neq 0$: tiny squares map to tiny squares, rotated and scaled.

\subsection{Cauchy's Integral Theorem in Three Lines via Green's Theorem}

A \emph{contour integral} $\oint_C f(z)\,\dd z$ is a line integral in the complex plane: walk along the curve $C$, multiply the value $f(z)$ by the small complex step $\dd z = \dd x + i\,\dd y$, and add up. Take $C = \partial D$ to be a closed curve bounding a simply connected region $D \subset \C$.

Split the integrand and the step into real and imaginary parts:
\[
f(z) = u + iv, \qquad \dd z = \dd x + i\,\dd y.
\]
Multiplying these expressions (using $i^2=-1$):
\[
f(z)\,\dd z = (u + iv)(\dd x + i\,\dd y) = (u\,\dd x - v\,\dd y) + i\,(v\,\dd x + u\,\dd y).
\]
Integrating around the closed boundary loop $C$ splits the complex contour integral into two ordinary real line integrals:
\begin{equation}
\oint_C f(z)\,\dd z = \oint_C (u\,\dd x - v\,\dd y) + i \oint_C (v\,\dd x + u\,\dd y).
\label{ch35:eq-contour-split}
\end{equation}

Now apply Green's theorem, $\oint_C (P\,\dd x + Q\,\dd y) = \iint_D (Q_x - P_y)\,\dd A$, to both integrals:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Real part} ($P = u$, $Q = -v$):
\[
\pdv{Q}{x} - \pdv{P}{y} = \pdv{(-v)}{x} - \pdv{u}{y} = -v_x - u_y.
\]
By the second Cauchy--Riemann equation, $u_y = -v_x$. Substituting this gives:
\[
-v_x - (-v_x) = 0.
\]
\item \textbf{Imaginary part} ($P = v$, $Q = u$):
\[
\pdv{Q}{x} - \pdv{P}{y} = \pdv{u}{x} - \pdv{v}{y} = u_x - v_y.
\]
By the first Cauchy--Riemann equation, $u_x = v_y$. Substituting this gives:
\[
v_y - v_y = 0.
\]
\end{enumerate}

Both integrands on the right of Green's theorem are zero everywhere in $D$:
\begin{equation}
\oint_C f(z)\,\dd z = \iint_D 0\,\dd A + i \iint_D 0\,\dd A = 0.
\label{ch35:eq-cauchy-theorem}
\end{equation}

This is \textbf{Cauchy's Integral Theorem}:
\begin{theorem}[Cauchy's Integral Theorem]
If $f(z)$ is complex-differentiable (holomorphic) on and inside a simple closed contour $C$, then:
\[
\oint_C f(z)\,\dd z = 0.
\]
\end{theorem}
Cauchy's integral theorem is Green's theorem applied to complex functions: the Cauchy--Riemann equations make the scalar curl of the vector fields $(u, -v)$ and $(v, u)$ zero everywhere. (Green's theorem needs continuous partial derivatives of $u$ and $v$; Goursat later showed that the theorem holds without that extra assumption.)

\subsection{The Single Punctured Exception: \texorpdfstring{$\oint_{\abs{z}=R} \frac{1}{z}\,\dd z = 2\pi i$}{The Contour Integral of 1/z}}

What happens when $f(z)$ fails to be differentiable at an isolated singularity inside $C$?

The standard test case is $f(z) = 1/z$, which is holomorphic everywhere except at the origin $z = 0$, where it is undefined. As in Chapter~30, an isolated singularity where $|f(z)| \to \infty$ is called a \emph{pole}.

Let $C$ be the circle $\abs{z} = R$ traversed counterclockwise. Parametrize $C$ in polar form, using Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ (Chapter~30):
\[
z(\theta) = R e^{i\theta}, \qquad \dd z = i R e^{i\theta}\,\dd\theta, \qquad \theta \in [0, 2\pi].
\]
Substitute this parametrization into the contour integral:
\begin{equation}
\oint_{\abs{z}=R} \frac{1}{z}\,\dd z = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{1}{R e^{i\theta}} \left(i R e^{i\theta}\,\dd\theta\right) = \int_0^{2\pi} i\,\dd\theta = 2\pi i.
\label{ch35:eq-1-over-z}
\end{equation}

The radius $R$ cancels. Whether the circular path has radius $R = 10^5$ or $R = 10^{-5}$, the integral is $2\pi i$.

Now take any smooth loop $C$ around the origin and a small circle $C_\eps$ of radius $\eps$ inside it. In the region between them, $1/z$ is holomorphic, so the two Green's-theorem integrands from the previous subsection are zero there. Cut a narrow corridor joining the two curves; its two sides are traversed in opposite directions and cancel (Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-cauchy-contour}). Green's theorem on the cut region then shows that $C$ can be shrunk onto $C_\eps$ without changing the integral:

\[
\oint_C \frac{1}{z}\,\dd z = \oint_{C_\eps} \frac{1}{z}\,\dd z = 2\pi i.
\]
More generally, $\oint_C \frac{1}{z}\,\dd z$ counts turns: it equals $2\pi i$ times the number of times the path $C$ winds counterclockwise around the origin (the \emph{winding number} of $C$).

In contrast, for every other integer power $z^n$ with $n \neq -1$, the number $n+1$ is a nonzero integer, so $e^{i(n+1)\theta}$ has an antiderivative that returns to its starting value after a full turn:
\begin{align*}
\oint_{\abs{z}=R} z^n\,\dd z &= \int_0^{2\pi} R^n e^{i n \theta} \left(i R e^{i\theta}\,\dd\theta\right)
= i R^{n+1} \int_0^{2\pi} e^{i(n+1)\theta}\,\dd\theta\\
&= i R^{n+1}\left[\frac{e^{i(n+1)\theta}}{i(n+1)}\right]_0^{2\pi} = 0.
\end{align*}
Only for $n=-1$ does the division by $n+1$ break down, which is why $z^{-1}$ is the unique survivor.

\subsection{Cauchy's Residue Theorem and Signal Processing}

A \emph{Laurent series} is a power series that also allows negative integer
powers; the negative powers describe how the function blows up near $z_0$. A
function that is holomorphic on a \emph{punctured disk} around an isolated
singularity $z_0$ (a disk with its center $z_0$ removed) can be written there as a
Laurent series:
\[
f(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty a_n (z - z_0)^n = \dots + \frac{a_{-2}}{(z-z_0)^2} + \frac{a_{-1}}{z-z_0} + a_0 + a_1(z-z_0) + \dots
\]
The singularity is a pole when only finitely many negative powers appear. To integrate $f(z)$ around a small loop enclosing $z_0$,
deform the loop onto a circle $\abs{z-z_0}=R$ and integrate the series term by
term (allowed because the series converges uniformly on the circle, in the sense of Chapter~30). By the
calculation above, every power with $n\neq -1$ contributes zero. Only the term
$a_{-1}(z - z_0)^{-1}$ survives, yielding $2\pi i a_{-1}$. The coefficient $a_{-1}$ is called the \textbf{residue} of
$f$ at $z_0$, written $\operatorname{Res}(f, z_0)$ -- it is the only term that
contributes to the loop integral.

If $C$ encloses several isolated poles $z_1, z_2, \dots, z_m$, shrink $C$ onto a
small circle around each pole, joined by corridors as before
(Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-residues}). Each small circle contributes $2\pi i$ times its
residue, which gives \textbf{Cauchy's Residue Theorem}:
\begin{equation}
\oint_C f(z)\,\dd z = 2\pi i \sum_{k=1}^{m} \operatorname{Res}(f, z_k).
\label{ch35:eq-residue-theorem}
\end{equation}

In engineering and physics, this residue calculation is a standard tool for solving linear differential equations (equations relating a function to its derivatives, the subject of Chapters~36--39):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Continuous-time systems and the Laplace transform}: When modeling an electrical circuit or a vibrating spring, physical laws give differential equations relating a function $f(t)$ to its time derivatives. The \emph{Laplace transform} replaces $f(t)$ by a new function $F(s)$ of a complex variable $s$, chosen so that taking a derivative in $t$ becomes multiplying by $s$. The differential equation becomes an algebraic equation, which is solved for $F(s)$. To get back the answer $f(t)$ as a function of time, one computes a contour integral of $F(s)$ in the complex plane (called the Bromwich integral). Cauchy's Residue Theorem evaluates this integral: the poles of $F(s)$ give the system's natural frequencies and exponential decay rates, and the residues give how much each contributes.
\item \textbf{Discrete-time systems and digital filters}: In digital signal processing (DSP), signals are sequences of sampled numbers $x[0], x[1], x[2], \dots$ rather than continuous curves. The \emph{$Z$-transform} $X(z) = \sum_{n\ge 0} x[n]\,z^{-n}$ plays the role of the Laplace transform. A contour integral around the origin recovers each sample $x[n]$ from $X(z)$, and the residue theorem evaluates it; the same method gives a digital filter's output from its transfer function (the ratio of the transformed output to the transformed input).
\end{itemize}
In both cases the contour integrals are evaluated by the method above, which rests on Green's theorem.

\section{Looking forward: from static vector fields to differential equations}
\label{ch35:bridge-ode}

So far we have treated a vector field as a fixed pattern of arrows painted across
space, and asked questions about totals over regions:
\begin{itemize}
\item How much does the field circulate around a boundary fence $C$?
  ($\oint_C \vec F \cdot \dd\vec r$)
\item How much fluid leaks out across the enclosing skin of a solid?
  ($\oiint_S \vec F \cdot \vec n\,\dd S$)
\end{itemize}
A different question is where a single particle goes. Instead of computing
flux across an entire surface, place a particle in the flow at
a starting point $\vec r_0 = (x_0, y_0)$ and track its motion over time.

At every location $(x,y)$, the vector field plants a velocity arrow:
\[
  \vec F(x,y) = \big(P(x,y),\, Q(x,y)\big).
\]
If the vector field represents a fluid current, the particle moves in the direction
the arrow at its current position points, with speed equal to the arrow's length.
So its velocity $\dv{\vec r}{t}$ at every moment is determined by where it is:
\begin{equation}
  \dv{\vec r}{t} = \vec F\big(\vec r(t)\big)
  \quad\iff\quad
  \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle \dv{x}{t} = P(x, y),\\[6pt]
    \displaystyle \dv{y}{t} = Q(x, y).
  \end{cases}
  \label{ch35:eq-field-ode}
\end{equation}
This is our first encounter with an \textbf{ordinary differential equation} (ODE):
a rule that gives a quantity's rate of change in terms of its current value.
(``Ordinary'' means there is a single input variable, here the time $t$.)

The two panels of Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-field-to-ode} show the two points of view:
\begin{itemize}
\item In \textbf{vector calculus}, the vector field is fixed. We ask what
happens when we integrate across it or around closed boundary loops.
\item In \textbf{differential equations}, the vector field is a \emph{rule of motion}.
It acts as a compass and speedometer at every point in the plane. A starting point
$\vec r(0) = \vec r_0$ sends a particle along a path $\vec r(t)$ called a
\emph{streamline} or \emph{solution trajectory}.
\end{itemize}

The properties of the field studied in this chapter shape these trajectories.
If the field is a gradient pointing downhill ($\vec F = -\grad U$), every trajectory
moves downhill along lines of steepest descent, typically settling toward a local
minimum of the potential $U$. If the field has zero divergence, a cloud of starting
points carried along by the flow keeps the same total area for all $t$; this is
Liouville's theorem, used in statistical mechanics (the physics of systems with very
many particles).

Chapter~36 starts the study of differential equations with the simplest case: a
single quantity $y(t)$ whose rate of change $\dv{y}{t}$ depends on its own value.

If a solid watermelon contains no fluid sources or sinks ($\operatorname{div}\vec F = 0$ everywhere inside), can more fluid flow out through its rind than flows in? What does the divergence theorem say about the net outward flux $\oiint_S \vec F\cdot\vec n\,\dd S$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mental check: constant wind]

A steady wind blows horizontally across the plains with constant velocity $\vec F(x,y) = (4, 7)$ meters per second. What is its scalar curl? What is its divergence? If you walk once around a circular running track in this wind, what is the net work done on you by the air?

\label{ch35:exr-green-area}
Use the area formula $\text{Area}=\tfrac12\oint_C x\,\dd y - y\,\dd x$ to find the
area enclosed by the astroid $x=\cos^3 t$, $y=\sin^3 t$, $t\in[0,2\pi]$. (You should
get $\tfrac{3}{8}\pi$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch35:exr-divcurl}
For $\vec F=(x^2 y,\; -xy^2,\; z)$ compute $\operatorname{div}\vec F$ and
$\operatorname{curl}\vec F$ as functions of $(x,y,z)$. Is there any point where the
field is locally incompressible ($\operatorname{div}\vec F=0$)? Explain.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch35:exr-greenverify}
Verify Green's theorem for $\vec F=(xy,\,x^2)$ over the triangle with vertices
$(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(1,1)$, by computing both the boundary line integral (three
edges) and the double integral of $Q_x-P_y$ over the triangle.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch35:exr-divthm}
Use the divergence theorem to compute the flux of $\vec F=(x^3,\,y^3,\,z^3)$ out of
the unit sphere. (Hint: $\operatorname{div}\vec F = 3(x^2+y^2+z^2)$; convert the
resulting triple integral to spherical coordinates.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch35:exr-stokes}
Let $\vec F=(z,\,x,\,y)$ and let $S$ be the part of the paraboloid $z=1-x^2-y^2$
above the $xy$-plane, oriented upward, with boundary the unit circle. Use Stokes'
theorem to compute $\oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\dd\vec r$ by integrating
$\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot\vec n$ over the flat unit disk instead (legal because
only the rim matters).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch35:exr-hole}
Consider $\vec F=\big(\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2},\,\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\big)$. (a) Verify that
its scalar curl is $0$ wherever it is defined. (b) Compute its circulation around
the unit circle and get $2\pi$. (c) Explain why parts (a) and (b) do not contradict
Green's theorem. (What goes wrong at the origin?)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ch35:exr-unify}
In your own words, state the single sentence that contains the FTC, the gradient
theorem, Green's, Stokes', and the divergence theorem at once. Identify, for each of
the five, what plays the role of ``the inside,'' ``the derivative taken inside,''
and ``the boundary.''
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[From field to trajectory]
\label{ch35:exr-ode-bridge}
For the swirl field $\vec F(x,y) = (-y, x)$, write down the corresponding system of
differential equations for a moving particle: $\dv{x}{t} = P(x,y)$ and $\dv{y}{t} = Q(x,y)$.
Show by direct differentiation that $\vec r(t) = \big(R\cos(t-t_0),\, R\sin(t-t_0)\big)$
solves this system for any choice of radius $R$ and initial phase $t_0$. How does this
family of solution curves relate to the concentric circles of Figure~\ref{ch35:fig-swirl}?
\end{exercise}
