\chapter{The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus}

On one side of calculus sits the \emph{derivative}: an operation for measuring
instantaneous rates of change, slopes of tangent lines, and how fast a quantity
changes at a single point. On the other side sits the \emph{integral}: an
operation for accumulating quantities over an interval, measuring
signed area, total displacement, and swept-out volume. Slope and area, rate and
total, look like separate questions. One concerns behavior at a single instant;
the other concerns behavior across a whole interval. One is a tangent line; the other is a region
of the plane.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (often shortened to FTC) proves that these two
operations are inverses of each other: differentiation and integration undo one
another, in the same way that addition undoes subtraction or squaring undoes
square-rooting (for positive numbers).

This changes how integrals are computed. Before the theorem, finding an area meant
adding up rectangles and evaluating the limit of those sums directly, with a new
summation trick for each curve.
After the theorem, finding an area means: find a function whose derivative is
the integrand (the function being integrated), then subtract two of its values.
This chapter proves the theorem and explains \emph{why} it holds.

\section{What we are bridging}

\subsection{The integral, briefly recalled}

Given a continuous function $f$ on an interval $[a,b]$, the \emph{definite integral}
\[
  \int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x
\]
is the limit of Riemann sums. Using $n$ equal subintervals of width $\Delta x = (b-a)/n$,
you build a thin rectangle on each piece whose height is a sample value $f(x_k^*)$ there,
add up the rectangle areas, and let $n\To\infty$ so the widths shrink to zero:
\[
  \int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x \;=\; \lim_{n\To\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k^*)\,\Delta x .
\]
When $f\ge 0$ this is the area under the graph. When $f$ dips below the axis it
is signed area -- area above the axis counts positive, area below counts
negative. For us the important point is that this is a \emph{global} object: to
know it you must, in principle, account for the value of $f$ at \emph{every}
point of $[a,b]$.

\subsection{The derivative, briefly recalled}

Given a function $F$, its \emph{derivative} at a point $x$ is the local rate of
change,
\[
  F'(x) \;=\; \lim_{h\To 0}\frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h},
\]
the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $F$ at $x$. This is a
\emph{local} object: it depends only on the values of $F$ at points as close to
$x$ as we like.

\begin{intuitionbox}
The integral depends on values across an entire interval. The derivative depends
only on values near a single point. How can an accumulated area and a slope
determine each other?

The answer, used throughout this chapter, is to \textbf{watch the area
grow}. Instead of computing the area once, let the right-hand edge
slide, and ask how fast the accumulated area is increasing at each moment. That
\emph{rate} of accumulation is a derivative, and it equals the height of the
curve.
\end{intuitionbox}

\section{The area-so-far function}

The key step, already previewed at the end of Chapter~20, is to treat the area
as a \emph{function} instead of a \emph{number}. Fix the left endpoint $a$ and let
the right endpoint vary. Define the \emph{area-so-far} function (the standard
name is the \emph{accumulation function}):
\[
  A(x) \;=\; \int_a^x f(t)\,\dd t .
\]

\begin{focusbox}
Read $A(x)$ as ``the signed area under $f$ from the starting line $a$ out to the
moving wall at $x$.'' As you push the wall $x$ to the right, you sweep out more
area, so $A$ grows. As you pull the wall back left, $A$ shrinks. $A(x)$ is a
running total.

The upper limit of integration is now $x$, and the variable inside the integral
is written $t$ so it does not collide with that limit. Here $t$ is a dummy variable
(Chapter~20): it is integrated over and disappears, leaving $A$ as a function of
the upper limit $x$ alone.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{labbox}
Take a simple example: $f(t)=t$ on $[0,x]$, with $x>0$. The region under
$f$ from $0$ to $x$ is a right triangle with both legs of length $x$, so its
area is $\tfrac12 x^2$. Hence
\[
  A(x) = \int_0^x t\,\dd t = \tfrac12 x^2 .
\]
Now do the central calculation of this chapter: differentiate the area.
\[
  A'(x) = \ddx{\tfrac12 x^2} = x = f(x).
\]
The derivative of the area-so-far is the original height. This is not special to
triangles: it is the Fundamental Theorem in its simplest case, and the next
section shows why it holds for every continuous $f$.
\end{labbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} If $f(t) > 0$ everywhere on $[a,b]$, can the
accumulation function $A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,\dd t$ ever decrease as $x$ increases?
Picture the sliding wall: whenever you advance $x$ to the right, the newly swept
strip sits above the horizontal axis, adding strictly positive area. So $A(x)$
must be strictly increasing. This matches what the next section proves:
$A'(x) = f(x) > 0$. The sign of $f$ is the sign of the slope of the running area.
\end{recoverybox}

\section{Part 1: differentiating the area gives back the curve}

\subsection{The infinitesimal-strip picture}

First, a geometric argument for \emph{why} $A'(x)=f(x)$ should be true; the
careful proof comes after it.

Push the right wall from $x$ to $x+h$, where $h$ is a tiny positive number. The
area grows by a sliver: the strip sitting between $x$ and $x+h$. Call that extra
area $A(x+h)-A(x)$. What does the sliver look like? It is a thin vertical strip
of width $h$, sitting under the curve $f$.

If $h$ is small, the curve barely changes across the strip, so the strip is
\emph{almost a rectangle}: width $h$, height roughly $f(x)$
(Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-strip}(a)). Its area is approximately
\[
  A(x+h)-A(x) \;\approx\; f(x)\cdot h .
\]
Divide both sides by $h$:
\[
  \frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h} \;\approx\; f(x).
\]
The left side is exactly the difference quotient whose limit defines $A'(x)$. As
$h\To 0$ the strip gets thinner, the ``almost a rectangle'' becomes ``a
rectangle'' in the limit, the approximation tightens to equality, and we land on
\[
  A'(x) = f(x).
\]

\begin{happeningbox}
Writing $A'(x)=f(x)$ means reading off the
\textbf{instantaneous rate at which area is piling up.} The height of the curve
at $x$ \emph{is} the speed at which the running total grows as the wall
sweeps past $x$.

Think of $f$ as a flow rate (liters per second pouring into a tank) and
$A(x)$ as the total liters in the tank after time $x$. ``How fast is the tank
filling right now?'' is answered by the current flow rate. That sentence is the
Fundamental Theorem, Part 1, in plain language: \emph{the rate of accumulation is
the integrand itself}. A thin strip is the water added during a short stretch of
time: its height is the flow rate, its width $h$ is the length of the time
stretch, and the product $f(x)\,h$ is the amount added during it.
\end{happeningbox}

\subsection{Making it a real proof}

The strip picture is not yet a proof, because of the ``$\approx$''. Across the
strip the height is not exactly $f(x)$; the curve changes a little. We need to
control that change and show that it vanishes in the limit. The tool is a squeeze
between a shortest and a tallest rectangle, and it works for any continuous $f$.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1]\label{ch21:thm-ftc1}
Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$. Define $A:[a,b]\To\R$ by
\[
  A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,\dd t .
\]
Then $A$ is differentiable on $(a,b)$, and also at the endpoints in the one-sided
sense (at $a$ only $h>0$ is allowed in the difference quotient, at $b$ only
$h<0$), and
\[
  A'(x) = f(x) \qquad\text{for every } x\in[a,b] .
\]
In words: the accumulation function is an antiderivative of $f$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{intuitionbox}
The idea of the proof is to trap the increment $A(x+h)-A(x)$ -- the area of the
strip -- between the area of the shortest rectangle that fits under it and the
area of the tallest rectangle that covers it. Both rectangles have width $h$;
their heights are the minimum and maximum of $f$ on the little interval. As
$h\To 0$ that little interval collapses onto the point $x$, so both the minimum
and the maximum of $f$ are squeezed toward $f(x)$ (this is where continuity is
essential). The strip's average height has nowhere to go but $f(x)$.
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{proof}
Fix $x$ and take $h>0$ small enough that $x+h$ stays in $[a,b]$. Using the
splitting rule for integrals (area from $a$ to $x+h$ equals area from $a$ to $x$
plus area from $x$ to $x+h$),
\[
  A(x+h)-A(x)
  = \int_a^{x+h} f - \int_a^{x} f
  = \int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,\dd t .
\]
So the increment of $A$ is exactly the area of the strip -- no approximation yet,
this is an identity.

Because $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[x,x+h]$, the Extreme Value
Theorem (Chapter~8: any continuous function on a closed interval reaches a
lowest and a highest value there) hands us a point where $f$
attains its minimum value $m_h$ and a point where it attains its maximum value
$M_h$ on that interval. Every value of $f$ on $[x,x+h]$ lies between $m_h$ and
$M_h$, so the strip's area is trapped between the two rectangles of width $h$:
\[
  m_h\, h \;\le\; \int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,\dd t \;\le\; M_h\, h .
\]
Divide through by $h>0$:
\[
  m_h \;\le\; \frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h} \;\le\; M_h .
\]
Now let $h\To 0^+$. The interval $[x,x+h]$ shrinks to the single point $x$. The
points where $m_h$ and $M_h$ are attained lie within $h$ of $x$, so by continuity
of $f$ both $m_h$ and $M_h$ converge to $f(x)$. The middle term is squeezed
between two quantities that both approach $f(x)$, so by the Squeeze Theorem
\[
  \lim_{h\To 0^+}\frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h} = f(x).
\]
The case $h<0$ works the same way, with signs kept straight. Now the interval
is $[x+h,x]$, whose length is $-h=\abs{h}>0$, and
\[
  A(x+h)-A(x) = -\int_{x+h}^{x} f(t)\,\dd t .
\]
With $m_h$ and $M_h$ now the minimum and maximum of $f$ on $[x+h,x]$,
\[
  m_h\,(-h) \;\le\; \int_{x+h}^{x} f(t)\,\dd t \;\le\; M_h\,(-h).
\]
Dividing by the positive number $-h$, and using
$\int_{x+h}^{x} f(t)\,\dd t = -\bigl(A(x+h)-A(x)\bigr)$, gives the same squeeze
as before:
\[
  m_h \;\le\; \frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h} \;\le\; M_h .
\]
Letting $h\To 0^-$ squeezes the left-hand limit to $f(x)$ as well. The two
one-sided limits agree, so the two-sided derivative $A'(x)$ exists and equals
$f(x)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
Why does the proof go through the \emph{min} and \emph{max} on the little
interval rather than using $f(x)$ directly? Because we are not allowed to assume the
strip is a perfect rectangle -- that is the very thing we are trying to justify.
The min and max rectangles are bounds we can prove: the true strip area sits
between them. Continuity then
guarantees the trap is tight, because over a shrinking interval the highest and
lowest values of a continuous function cannot stay apart -- they both must
collapse to the value at the point. Continuity is exactly the hypothesis that
makes ``the curve barely changes across a thin strip'' a theorem instead of a
hope.
\end{whybox}

\begin{remark}
The same squeeze can be packaged as the \emph{Mean Value Theorem for Integrals}:
for continuous $f$ there is a point $c\in[x,x+h]$ with
$\int_x^{x+h} f = f(c)\cdot h$. (The average height of the strip is a value that
$f$ takes somewhere on the strip; see Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-mvt-int}.) Then
$\frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h}=f(c)$, and as $h\To 0$ the point $c$ is forced toward $x$,
so $f(c)\To f(x)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{trapbox}
A frequent confusion: students see $\int_a^x f(t)\,\dd t$ and try to ``plug in''
or differentiate with respect to $t$. There is no $t$ to differentiate with
respect to -- $t$ has been integrated away. The function $A$ depends on its
\emph{upper limit} $x$. The statement $A'(x)=f(x)$ means: differentiate with
respect to the upper limit, and you recover the integrand evaluated there.

Also avoid writing $A(x)=\int_a^x f(x)\,\dd x$ with $x$ in both places. One $x$
is a fixed endpoint and the other runs over $[a,x]$, so the expression is
ambiguous. Always rename the inside variable.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{The chain-rule version (variable in the limits)}

Part 1 plus the chain rule lets you differentiate any accumulation whose limits
are themselves functions. Suppose
\[
  G(x) = \int_a^{u(x)} f(t)\,\dd t .
\]
Write $G(x)=A(u(x))$ where $A$ is the accumulation function from before, so
$A'=f$. The chain rule gives
\[
  G'(x) = A'(u(x))\cdot u'(x) = f\big(u(x)\big)\, u'(x).
\]
If the variable sits in the lower limit instead, flip the sign (swapping limits
negates the integral). When both limits move, split at the fixed point $a$:
$\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)} f = \int_a^{v(x)} f - \int_a^{u(x)} f$. Differentiating each
piece by the rule above gives the general formula
\[
  \ddx{\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)} f(t)\,\dd t}
  = f\big(v(x)\big)\,v'(x) - f\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x).
\]
Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-chain} shows what the two terms mean. When $x$ increases a
little, the right wall $v(x)$ moves right by about $v'(x)$ times the change in $x$,
adding a strip of height about $f(v(x))$; the left wall $u(x)$ also moves,
removing a strip of height about $f(u(x))$.

\begin{example}\label{ch21:ex-chain}
Differentiate $\displaystyle G(x)=\int_2^{x^3}\sin(t^2)\,\dd t$.

\textbf{Solution.} Here $f(t)=\sin(t^2)$ and the upper limit is $u(x)=x^3$, so
$u'(x)=3x^2$. By the chain-rule version,
\[
  G'(x) = \sin\big((x^3)^2\big)\cdot 3x^2 = 3x^2\sin(x^6).
\]
We never found a formula for $G$ itself, and there is no \emph{elementary} one:
no formula built from finitely many powers, roots, exponentials, logarithms,
trigonometric functions and their inverses gives an antiderivative of
$\sin(t^2)$. Yet we differentiated $G$ exactly. Part 1 gives the derivative of an
integral without evaluating the integral first.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ch21:ex-chain2}
Differentiate $\displaystyle H(x)=\int_{x}^{x^2}\frac{1}{1+\ln t}\,\dd t$ for
$x>1$.

\textbf{Solution.} Both limits move: $u(x)=x$, $v(x)=x^2$. With
$f(t)=\frac{1}{1+\ln t}$,
\begin{align*}
  H'(x) &= \frac{1}{1+\ln(x^2)}\cdot 2x \;-\; \frac{1}{1+\ln x}\cdot 1\\
        &= \frac{2x}{1+2\ln x} - \frac{1}{1+\ln x}
        && \text{(since } \ln(x^2)=2\ln x\text{)}.
\end{align*}
\end{example}

\section{Part 1 says: every continuous function has an antiderivative}

Theorem~\ref{ch21:thm-ftc1} also says that antiderivatives always exist.

\begin{corollary}[Existence of antiderivatives]\label{ch21:cor-exist}
Every function continuous on an interval has an antiderivative on that interval --
namely its own accumulation function $A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,\dd t$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{aestheticbox}
In practice, ``find an antiderivative of $f$'' usually means ``find a formula
whose derivative is $f$.'' For many ordinary functions, such as $e^{-x^2}$,
$\frac{\sin x}{x}$ and $\sqrt{1+x^3}$, \emph{no elementary formula exists} (in the
sense of Example~\ref{ch21:ex-chain}). This is not a gap in our technique: the
theory begun by Joseph Liouville in the 1830s shows that these antiderivatives
cannot be written with elementary functions at all.

Part 1 shows that the antiderivative still \emph{exists} as a well-defined
function; we define it by an integral instead of a formula. For example,
$\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,\dd t$ is the area under the bell-shaped curve $e^{-t^2}$ from
$0$ to $x$. Multiplied by $2/\sqrt{\pi}$, it is the \emph{error function}
$\operatorname{erf}(x)$, used throughout probability and statistics. It is a
legitimate function, and by Part~1 its derivative is $e^{-x^2}$ (times
$2/\sqrt{\pi}$ for $\operatorname{erf}$).
\end{aestheticbox}

\section{Part 2: evaluating an integral with any antiderivative}

Part 1 tells us \emph{a} particular antiderivative -- the area function $A$ --
satisfies $A'=f$. Part 2 turns this into a method of computation: to find the
area, you may use \emph{any} antiderivative you can find, and the
answer is one subtraction.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2]\label{ch21:thm-ftc2}
Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and let $F$ be \emph{any} antiderivative of $f$
on $[a,b]$, meaning $F'=f$ throughout. Then
\[
  \int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x \;=\; F(b)-F(a).
\]
\end{theorem}

The standard shorthand for the right side is $\eval{F(x)}{a}^{b}=F(b)-F(a)$, read
``$F$ evaluated from $a$ to $b$.''

\begin{intuitionbox}
Why should the area equal $F(b)-F(a)$ for \emph{any} antiderivative, not just the
area function? Because antiderivatives of the same function can differ only by a
constant (Corollary~\ref{ch15:cor-plusC}, from the Mean Value Theorem: two
functions with the same derivative on an interval stay a constant vertical
distance apart). The area function $A$
and your chosen $F$ differ by a fixed constant $C$. When you compute the
difference $F(b)-F(a)$, the constant cancels: $(A(b)+C)-(A(a)+C)=A(b)-A(a)$. And
$A(b)-A(a)$ is the total area from $a$ to $b$ (Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-shift}).
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{proof}[Proof via Part 1]
Let $A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,\dd t$ be the area function. By Part 1, $A'=f$. By
assumption $F'=f$ as well. Two functions with the same derivative on an interval
differ by a constant (Corollary~\ref{ch15:cor-plusC}), so there is a
constant $C$ with
\[
  F(x) = A(x) + C \qquad\text{for all } x\in[a,b].
\]
Evaluate the difference:
\[
  F(b)-F(a) = \big(A(b)+C\big)-\big(A(a)+C\big) = A(b)-A(a).
\]
But $A(a)=\int_a^a f = 0$ (an interval of zero width sweeps out no area) and
$A(b)=\int_a^b f$. Therefore $F(b)-F(a)=\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$.
\end{proof}

A second proof does not use Part 1. It builds the total change in $F$ out of
small changes, each one controlled by the Mean Value Theorem (MVT), and it shows
directly why only the endpoint values $F(a)$ and $F(b)$ survive. Its key step is
a \emph{telescoping} sum: a sum in which each term cancels part of the next, so
that, like a collapsing telescope, only the first and last pieces remain. For
example, $(F_1-F_0)+(F_2-F_1)+(F_3-F_2)=F_3-F_0$.

\begin{proof}[Proof via telescoping and the Mean Value Theorem]
Partition $[a,b]$ into pieces $a=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_n=b$, and write
$\Delta x_k = x_k-x_{k-1}$ for the width of the $k$-th piece. The total change in $F$
across $[a,b]$ telescopes: each interior value is added once and subtracted
once, so only the endpoints survive:
\[
  F(b)-F(a) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(F(x_k)-F(x_{k-1})\big).
\]
Apply the Mean Value Theorem to $F$ on each little interval $[x_{k-1},x_k]$:
there is a sample point $c_k$ inside with
\[
  F(x_k)-F(x_{k-1}) = F'(c_k)\,(x_k-x_{k-1}) = f(c_k)\,\Delta x_k .
\]
(We used $F'=f$.) Substituting,
\[
  F(b)-F(a) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(c_k)\,\Delta x_k .
\]
The right side is a Riemann sum for $f$, with the MVT choosing the
sample points $c_k$ for us. As the partition is refined and the mesh goes to $0$, the
left side does not move at all (it is the fixed number $F(b)-F(a)$), while the
right side converges to $\int_a^b f$ by the definition of the integral. (Because
$f$ is continuous, this limit exists and does not depend on which partitions or
sample points are used.) Therefore
$F(b)-F(a)=\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$.
\end{proof}

\begin{whybox}
The second proof shows how Part 2 works: \textbf{a total change is
a sum of little changes, and a little change in $F$ is, by the MVT, exactly a
height of $f$ times a width.} Stacking those little $f(c_k)\,\Delta x_k$ bricks
\emph{is} building the area. Telescoping makes those bricks reassemble
into the endpoint difference $F(b)-F(a)$: every interior wall is shared by
two bricks and cancels, leaving only the outer walls $a$ and $b$
(Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-telescoping}). An antiderivative is a running total of the
increments, so the net total is the final reading minus the initial reading.
\end{whybox}

\begin{happeningbox}
Part 2 has a plain bank-account reading. Let $F(t)$ be your
account balance at time $t$ and $f(t)=F'(t)$ the instantaneous rate money flows
in (deposits positive, withdrawals negative). Then $\int_a^b f(t)\,\dd t$ adds up
all the little flows over the period -- the net amount that moved -- and that must
equal the change in balance, $F(b)-F(a)$. You did not need to track every
transaction; you only needed the balance at the start and the balance at the end.
The Fundamental Theorem is the statement that \emph{net accumulated rate equals
net change in total}. In this form it sounds obvious; the proofs above show
that this banking fact and the area fact are the same theorem.
\end{happeningbox}

\section{Why differentiation and integration are inverse}

We can now say exactly what ``inverse operations'' means here. There are two
round trips, one for each order.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Integrate, then differentiate} (Part 1): start with $f$, accumulate it
into $A(x)=\int_a^x f$, then differentiate. You get $f$ back.
\[
  \ddx{\int_a^x f(t)\,\dd t} = f(x).
\]

\textbf{Differentiate, then integrate} (Part 2): start with $F$, differentiate
into $F'=f$, then accumulate. You recover the net change in $F$.
\[
  \int_a^b F'(x)\,\dd x = F(b)-F(a).
\]

Each operation undoes the other, with one exception: going
$F\To F'\To \int F'$ recovers $F$ only \emph{up to its value at the starting
point} (you get the change $F(b)-F(a)$, not $F$ itself). That missing piece
is the constant of integration, and it is the only thing the two operations
do not cancel.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
This is the pattern of any operation and its inverse, like squaring and taking
square roots, except that here the operations act on whole \emph{functions}
instead of numbers. Write $D$ for differentiation. Integrating and then applying
$D$ is the \emph{identity} operation: it gives back exactly the function you
started with. Applying $D$ and then integrating is almost the identity: it gives
back $F$ only up to a constant. The constant is lost because $D$ sends every
constant function to zero, so once you have differentiated, nothing records which
constant was there. In linear algebra, the set of inputs that an operation sends
to zero is called its \emph{kernel}; the kernel of $D$ is the set of constant
functions. The same bookkeeping returns in differential equations, where the
single $+C$ grows into a whole family of solutions.
\end{connectionbox}

\section{Antiderivatives and the $+C$}

An \emph{antiderivative} of $f$ is any function $F$ with $F'=f$. The
\emph{indefinite integral} collects them all:
\[
  \int f(x)\,\dd x = F(x) + C ,
\]
where $F$ is one antiderivative and $C$ ranges over all real constants. The
notation deliberately drops the limits to signal ``a family of functions,'' as
opposed to the definite integral $\int_a^b f$, which is ``a single number.''

\begin{whybox}
Why is the family \emph{exactly} $F(x)+C$ -- why no other antiderivatives lurking
elsewhere? Suppose $G'=f$ too. Then $(G-F)'=f-f=0$ on the interval. A function
with zero derivative on an interval is constant (Mean Value Theorem again: if it
ever changed, some secant slope would be nonzero, forcing a nonzero derivative
somewhere). So $G-F=C$, i.e. $G=F+C$. The constant $+C$ gives the
\emph{complete} description of all antiderivatives on an interval. Geometrically,
all antiderivatives are vertical shifts of one another: same shape, same slope at
every $x$, slid up or down (Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-plusC}).
\end{whybox}

\begin{trapbox}
Two $+C$ pitfalls.

\emph{Forgetting it on an indefinite integral.} $\int 2x\,\dd x = x^2$ is
\textbf{incomplete}; the answer is $x^2+C$. You have named one antiderivative when
the question asked for all of them.

\emph{Writing it on a definite integral.} $\int_0^3 2x\,\dd x = 9$, a number --
no $C$. If you carry the constant through, it cancels in $F(3)-F(0)$ anyway, so
it is harmless but pointless. The $+C$ belongs to the indefinite world of
function-families, not the definite world of numbers.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{walkbox}
On your walk, picture the family of all antiderivatives as a deck of identical
curves stacked vertically. A definite integral asks you to read the \emph{rise}
between two $x$-values -- and rises are unaffected by which card of the deck you
read, because shifting the whole deck shifts both endpoints equally. Now ask
yourself: what \emph{one} extra piece of information would pin down a single card
out of the deck? (Answer: the value of $F$ at one point. In differential
equations this extra fact is called an \emph{initial condition}.)
\end{walkbox}

\section{Worked evaluations}

Evaluating a definite integral with Part 2 takes two steps: (1) find any
antiderivative $F$ of the integrand; (2) compute $F(b)-F(a)$.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Pause and think:} What happens if we integrate backward, with $b < a$?
The formula still gives $\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x = F(b)-F(a)$, and this equals
$-\bigl(F(a)-F(b)\bigr) = -\int_b^a f(x)\,\dd x$. So the recipe handles reversed
limits automatically, in agreement with the rule $\int_b^a f = -\int_a^b f$ from
Chapter~20: running right to left flips the sign of the rise.
\end{recoverybox}

\begin{example}\label{ch21:ex-poly}
$\displaystyle \int_1^3 (x^2 - 2x + 5)\,\dd x.$

\textbf{Antiderivative.} Reverse the power rule term by term:
$F(x)=\tfrac13 x^3 - x^2 + 5x$. (Check: $F'(x)=x^2-2x+5$.)

\textbf{Evaluate.}
\begin{align*}
  \eval{\tfrac13 x^3 - x^2 + 5x}{1}^{3}
  &= \Big(9 - 9 + 15\Big) - \Big(\tfrac13 - 1 + 5\Big)\\
  &= 15 - \tfrac{13}{3} = \frac{32}{3}.
\end{align*}
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ch21:ex-sin}
$\displaystyle \int_0^{\pi} \sin x\,\dd x.$

An antiderivative of $\sin x$ is $-\cos x$ (since $\ddx{-\cos x}=\sin x$). Thus
\begin{align*}
  \eval{-\cos x}{0}^{\pi} &= (-\cos\pi) - (-\cos 0)\\
  &= \big(-(-1)\big) - \big(-(1)\big) = 1 - (-1) = 2.
\end{align*}
The area under one hump of the sine curve is exactly $2$, a value that would be
hard to guess from Riemann rectangles. Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-sin} puts the two
sides of Part 2 next to each other: the area on the left equals the rise of the
antiderivative $-\cos x$ on the right.

\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ch21:ex-recip}
$\displaystyle \int_1^{e} \frac{1}{x}\,\dd x.$

An antiderivative of $1/x$ (for $x>0$) is $\ln x$. So
\[
  \eval{\ln x}{1}^{e} = \ln e - \ln 1 = 1 - 0 = 1.
\]
The area under the hyperbola $y=1/x$ from $1$ to $e$ is $1$
(Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-recip}). This gives another description of $e$: it is the
number $b>1$ for which the area under $1/x$ from $1$ to $b$ equals $1$.

\begin{example}\label{ch21:ex-exp}
$\displaystyle \int_0^{\ln 2} e^{x}\,\dd x.$

Since $e^x$ is its own antiderivative,
\[
  \eval{e^x}{0}^{\ln 2} = e^{\ln 2} - e^{0} = 2 - 1 = 1.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ch21:ex-sqrt}
$\displaystyle \int_0^{4} \sqrt{x}\,\dd x.$

Write $\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}$. Reverse the power rule: raise the exponent by one to
$\tfrac32$ and divide, giving $F(x)=\tfrac{2}{3}x^{3/2}$. Then
\[
  \eval{\tfrac23 x^{3/2}}{0}^{4} = \tfrac23\cdot 4^{3/2} - 0
  = \tfrac23\cdot 8 = \frac{16}{3}.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Signed area, and why you sometimes split]\label{ch21:ex-signed}
$\displaystyle \int_0^{2\pi}\cos x\,\dd x$ versus the \emph{geometric} area
between $\cos x$ and the axis over the same interval.

The definite integral, using antiderivative $\sin x$:
\[
  \eval{\sin x}{0}^{2\pi} = \sin(2\pi)-\sin(0) = 0 .
\]
Zero -- the positive area of the first hump exactly cancels the negative area of
the trough (Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-signed}). If instead you want the \emph{total
geometric area} (everything counted positive), you must split at the sign
changes:
\[
  \int_0^{\pi/2}\!\cos x\,\dd x - \int_{\pi/2}^{3\pi/2}\!\cos x\,\dd x
  + \int_{3\pi/2}^{2\pi}\!\cos x\,\dd x
  = 1 + 2 + 1 = 4 .
\]

\begin{trapbox}
The most common Part-2 mistake is integrating across a point where the
integrand \emph{blows up} (grows without bound). Consider
$\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1}{x^2}\,\dd x$. Using $F(x)=-\tfrac1x$ without checking gives
$F(1)-F(-1)=-1-(1)=-2$, a \emph{negative} answer for the area under a strictly
positive function, which is impossible. The catch: $1/x^2$
is not continuous on $[-1,1]$; it is undefined at $0$ and grows without bound
near it, and $F$ jumps there (Figure~\ref{ch21:fig-blowup}). Part 2 requires $f$
continuous on the whole interval (and $F$ differentiable throughout). This is an
\emph{improper} integral, here because the integrand is unbounded on the
interval (Chapter~25 treats these), and here the area is infinite, so the integral
\emph{diverges}: it has no finite value. \textbf{Always confirm the integrand is
continuous on $[a,b]$ before applying Part 2.}
\end{trapbox}

\section{A short history of the bridge}

\begin{historybox}
Pieces of both halves were known long before anyone saw they fit together.
Archimedes (3rd century BCE, Syracuse) computed areas and volumes by his
``method of exhaustion'' (sandwiching a curved region between inscribed and
circumscribed polygons), which amounts to Riemann sums evaluated with great
geometric skill, one shape at a time, with no general method. In the early
1600s, Pierre de Fermat, Bonaventura Cavalieri (using ``indivisibles,'' treating
a two-dimensional area as an infinite stack of one-dimensional lines), and John
Wallis found area formulas for $x^n$. On the other side, tangent-line methods
(the seeds of differentiation) were developed by Fermat, Ren\'e Descartes, and
especially Isaac Barrow, Newton's teacher and predecessor as Lucasian Professor
of Mathematics at Cambridge.

Barrow, in his 1670 \emph{Lectiones Geometricae} (specifically Lecture~X,
Proposition~11), came close to the general theorem. Using pure Euclidean
geometry (figures and ratios in the style of Euclid) without algebraic formulas, Barrow proved that if a curve is
constructed by accumulating the ordinates (the vertical heights $y$ of points on
a base curve), its tangent at any point is determined directly by the height of
the original curve. In modern terms, Barrow proved that differentiation inverts
accumulation. But he worked only with geometric diagrams, with no algebraic
equations or function notation, and treated the result as one geometric theorem
among dozens, not as a general method for computing areas.

It took \emph{Isaac Newton} (around 1666)
and \emph{Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz} (in 1675) to recognize the Fundamental
Theorem as the link that turns area problems into antiderivative problems. Newton,
motivated by physics and
celestial orbits, framed the calculus around ``fluxions'' (his term for the
instantaneous rates of change of flowing quantities) and ``fluents'' (the flowing
quantities themselves, such as accumulated position). For example, position is
the fluent whose fluxion is velocity. Leibniz, looking for a general symbolic
language, designed the notation $\dd x$ and the elongated S,
$\int$ (for Latin \emph{summa}, sum), precisely so that $\dd$ and $\int$
would visibly undo each other on the page: $\int \dd F = F$ and
$\dd(\int f\,\dd x) = f\,\dd x$. Together they turned a set of separate geometric
results into a general method of calculation.

Two disputes followed. First was the bitter Newton--Leibniz priority
dispute: accusations of plagiarism, rival national academies, and decades of bad
blood that divided British and Continental mathematics for a century, though
modern historians confirm they discovered the bridge independently. Second was
the foundational crisis: both men computed with ``infinitesimals'' (quantities
treated as smaller than any positive real number yet strictly nonzero when
dividing to find slopes, only to be discarded as zero at the end). In 1734, the
philosopher Bishop George Berkeley published \emph{The Analyst}, criticizing this
shift between $\dd x \neq 0$ and $\dd x = 0$ as logically inconsistent. The objection
was fair: neither Newton nor Leibniz had a rigorous definition of an infinitesimal. It took Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1820s) with limits, Bernhard Riemann (1850s)
with partitioned sums, and Karl Weierstrass (1850s--60s) with $\eps$--$\delta$
definitions to put the theorem on a rigorous foundation.

The theorem became a foundation for celestial mechanics, differential
equations, fluid dynamics, and physics. In several-variable calculus
(Chapter~35) it grows into Green's theorem, the divergence theorem and Stokes'
theorem. Each says that the total of a derivative over a region equals a total
over the region's boundary, just as $\int_a^b F'(x)\,\dd x = F(b)-F(a)$ involves
only the two boundary points $a$ and $b$.
\end{historybox}

\section{The two parts, side by side}

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Part 1 (the area-so-far has slope $f$):}
\[
  \frac{\dd}{\dd x}\int_a^x f(t)\,\dd t = f(x).
\]
\emph{Differentiating an accumulation returns the integrand.} This is the
existence half: it builds an antiderivative out of $f$, and it tells you the rate
at which area accumulates.

\textbf{Part 2 (evaluate by an antiderivative):}
\[
  \int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x = F(b)-F(a), \qquad F'=f.
\]
\emph{A definite integral is a net change in any antiderivative.} This is the
computational half: it replaces a limit of sums by a subtraction of two values.

They are two readings of one fact: accumulating and rate-measuring are
inverse processes. Every technique in the chapters ahead (substitution, parts,
partial fractions) is a method for the \emph{first} step of Part 2:
\textbf{finding an antiderivative}. Once you have $F$, the theorem does the rest.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Physics: position from velocity.} If $v(t)$ is velocity, then total
displacement over $[t_0,t_1]$ is $\int_{t_0}^{t_1} v(t)\,\dd t = s(t_1)-s(t_0)$,
because position $s$ is an antiderivative of $v$. The Fundamental Theorem is the
statement that ``displacement is the accumulation of velocity'' -- the area under
the speedometer trace is the distance traveled (when the velocity stays
positive). Likewise \emph{impulse} (the total push a force delivers over time) is
the integral of force over time, and \emph{work} (the energy a force transfers
while moving an object) is the integral of force over distance. In each case a
single antiderivative replaces an infinite tally of moment-by-moment
contributions.

\textbf{Probability.} A \emph{probability density} $p(x)$ is a function whose area
over an interval gives the probability that a random quantity lands in that
interval. Its \emph{cumulative distribution function}
$F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x p(t)\,\dd t$ gives the probability that the quantity is at
most $x$ (the lower limit $-\infty$ means the area is taken from the far left;
Chapter~25 makes this precise). By Part 1, $F'=p$: the density is the rate at
which probability accumulates.
\end{applicationbox}

\section{Reach of the Fundamental Theorem and the Liouville Barrier}

\begin{connectionbox}
The Fundamental Theorem reduces every definite integral, whether it measures work,
impulse, arc length, surface area or a probability, to one task: \emph{to integrate,
antidifferentiate}, then evaluate $F(b) - F(a)$.

But differentiation and antidifferentiation are not symmetric. Differentiating an
elementary formula always succeeds by following the rules of Chapters 11 and 12.
Antidifferentiating can fail. To get an exact value from Part~2 you need a
formula for $F$, and, as noted earlier in this chapter, Liouville's work shows that
for many simple elementary functions, differentiable as often as you like, no
elementary antiderivative exists. We will call this obstacle
\emph{Liouville's barrier}. Standard examples:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  &\int e^{-x^2}\,\dd x \quad (\text{the Gaussian bell curve}), \qquad
  &&\int \frac{\sin x}{x}\,\dd x \quad (\text{the sine integral}), \\[4pt]
  &\int \frac{e^x}{x}\,\dd x \quad (\text{the exponential integral}), \qquad
  &&\int \sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2 x}\,\dd x \quad (\text{the elliptic integral}),
\end{aligned}
\]
where in the last one $k$ is a constant with $0<k<1$.
For all of these functions, Part~1 guarantees that the accumulation function
$A(x) = \int_0^x f(t)\,\dd t$ exists and satisfies $A'(x) = f(x)$ (for
$\frac{\sin x}{x}$ and $\frac{e^x}{x}$, start at a point other than $0$, where
they are undefined). But Part~2 cannot give an exact formula for
$\int_a^b f(x)\,\dd x$, because no elementary formula for $A(x)$ exists.

This sets the plan for the rest of our study of integration:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{More ways to find antiderivatives (Chapters 22--24):} When an elementary antiderivative
  exists, how do we find it systematically? Chapter~22 develops \emph{integration by
  substitution} (the chain rule run in reverse). Chapter~23
  introduces \emph{integration by parts} (the product rule run in reverse, for
  products such as $x e^x$). Chapter~24 develops \emph{partial fractions and
  trigonometric integrals}, which handle every rational function (ratio of polynomials).
  \item \emph{When formulas fail: numerical integration (Chapter 27).} When an integrand hits
  Liouville's barrier, or when we only have measured data and no formula, we
  return to Riemann-style sums. Numerical integration (also called
  \emph{quadrature}) methods such as the trapezoidal rule (which replaces the
  curve by straight line segments) and Simpson's rule
  (which fits parabolas to the curve) approximate areas to high precision without
  an antiderivative.
\end{itemize}
\end{connectionbox}
