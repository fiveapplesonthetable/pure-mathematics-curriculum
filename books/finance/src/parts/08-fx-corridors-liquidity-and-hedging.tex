\label{ch:fx-corridors}
\chapterline

In Chapter~\ref{ch:mobile-money-ledgers}, every debit and credit inside a journal batch shared a single currency unit. As soon as a customer in London sends money to a relative in Nairobi, or an importer in Lagos pays a factory in Shenzhen, two structural questions confront the engineer designing the payment system:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Operational physics:} If international bank wires take two to three business days to clear, how do modern cross-border payment apps deliver local funds to the recipient's phone wallet in twelve seconds on a Sunday night?
  \item \emph{Ledger conservation:} In double-entry accounting, debits must equal credits. How can a journal entry balance when the customer pays in $1{,}000$ British pounds (\texttt{GBP}) and the recipient receives $162{,}400$ Kenyan shillings (\texttt{KES})?
\end{enumerate}

Both questions share one underlying principle. Domestic money never physically crosses a national border. A British pound deposit exists solely as a liability on a UK bank's ledger (ultimately backed by reserves at the Bank of England), and a Kenyan shilling deposit exists solely on a Kenyan bank or trust ledger (backed at the Central Bank of Kenya). Cross-border payment engineering is the art of coordinating matched local ledger movements on two sides of a border while tracking exchange-rate inventory and revaluation risk between them.

\section{Correspondent Ledgers, Bills of Exchange, and Messaging Rails}

Before writing a line of foreign-exchange (FX) code, strip away the illusion that international payment networks transport coins through undersea cables. Every cross-border payment architecture in history has operated by maintaining reciprocal credit balances in two jurisdictions and transmitting authenticated instructions to adjust them.

\begin{historybox}
In fourteenth-century Europe, transporting silver groats or gold florins from wool fairs in Bruges and London to banking houses in Florence meant paying armed guards and risking seizure on Alpine passes. Italian merchant-bankers -- notably the Datini and Medici partnerships -- eliminated physical coin shipment using \emph{cambium per litteras} (exchange by letters, or \textbf{bills of exchange}). A merchant depositing English sterling with the Medici branch in London received a signed paper draft instructing the Medici partner in Florence to pay an equivalent sum in gold florins to a named counterparty sixty days later. FX quotes embedded both the currency conversion ratio and the time-value discount, settling trade by local ledger offsets.

In the twentieth century, sovereign monetary arrangements shifted from the 1944 \textbf{Bretton Woods} fixed-peg regime (which anchored participating currencies to the US dollar and the dollar to gold at \$35 per troy ounce) to the post-1971 regime of \textbf{floating fiat exchange rates}, in which currency prices move continuously second by second on wholesale interbank markets.

In 1973, global banks formed \textbf{SWIFT} (Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication) to replace error-prone telex messages with standardized cryptographic payment instructions. Engineers new to banking often assume ``sending a SWIFT transfer'' moves money through SWIFT. In fact, \textbf{SWIFT sends messages, not money}. Value moves only because Bank~A holds a pre-established foreign-currency account on the books of Correspondent Bank~B in the destination country. From Bank~A's perspective, its account held at Bank~B is called a \textbf{Nostro} account (Latin \emph{nostro}, ``ours''); from Bank~B's perspective, that exact same account is a \textbf{Vostro} account (\emph{vostro}, ``yours''). A cross-border wire is nothing more than a chain of Nostro/Vostro ledger debits and credits triggered by authenticated SWIFT messages.
\end{historybox}

\section{Spot FX Mechanics Without Jargon Traps}

\subsection{Base Currency, Quote Currency, and Dimensional Algebra}

An exchange rate is the price of one unit of a first currency expressed in units of a second currency. Market convention writes every pair as \texttt{BASE/QUOTE}:

\begin{definition}[Base and Quote Currency]
A spot quote $\texttt{AAA/BBB} = S$ means that \textbf{$1$ unit of base currency \texttt{AAA} exchanges for $S$ units of quote (or counter) currency \texttt{BBB}}:
\[
  1\text{ AAA} = S\text{ BBB}, \qquad \text{so } S \text{ has physical units } \frac{\text{BBB}}{\text{AAA}}.
\]
Converting an amount $A_{\text{AAA}}$ from base to quote \emph{multiplies} by $S$; converting an amount $A_{\text{BBB}}$ from quote back to base \emph{divides} by $S$:
\[
  A_{\text{BBB}} = A_{\text{AAA}} \times S, \qquad A_{\text{AAA}} = \frac{A_{\text{BBB}}}{S}.
\]
\end{definition}

Look at two concrete pairs side by side:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\texttt{USD/KES} = 129.50$ means $1\text{ USD} = 129.50\text{ KES}$. Here \texttt{USD} is the base currency. Converting \$1,000 into shillings multiplies: $1{,}000 \times 129.50 = 129{,}500\text{ KES}$.
  \item $\texttt{GBP/USD} = 1.2800$ means $1\text{ GBP} = 1.2800\text{ USD}$. By historical interbank hierarchy (\texttt{EUR}, \texttt{GBP}, \texttt{AUD}, \texttt{NZD} quote ahead of \texttt{USD}), \texttt{GBP} is the base currency and \texttt{USD} is the quote currency. Converting \$1,000 into pounds \emph{divides}: $1{,}000 / 1.2800 = 781.25\text{ GBP}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Inverting Multiplication and Division in FX Code.} One of the costliest production bugs in payment engineering occurs when a developer stores an untyped float called \texttt{usd\_rate} and assumes converting from \texttt{USD} to any foreign currency always multiplies by \texttt{usd\_rate}. On \texttt{USD/KES = 129.50} that assumption happens to work; when the company launches the UK corridor and loads \texttt{GBP/USD = 1.2800} from a market-data feed, multiplying \$1,000 by $1.2800$ pays out $1{,}280.00\text{ GBP}$ (worth \$1,638.40) instead of $781.25\text{ GBP}$ -- an instant $63.8\%$ principal loss on every transaction until the hotfix deploys.

Represent every FX rate in code as an immutable value object carrying explicit \texttt{base\_currency}, \texttt{quote\_currency}, and rational/fixed-point \texttt{rate}, and require the conversion method to assert dimensional unit cancellation before returning an output amount.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Bid, Mid, Ask, and Embedded Retail Spreads}

In any liquid FX market, a wholesale market maker does not quote one number; it quotes a two-sided market for the \textbf{base} currency:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bid ($S_{\text{bid}}$):} The price (in quote currency) at which the dealer \emph{buys} $1$ unit of base currency from you.
  \item \textbf{Ask / Offer ($S_{\text{ask}}$):} The price (in quote currency) at which the dealer \emph{sells} $1$ unit of base currency to you, always satisfying $S_{\text{bid}} < S_{\text{ask}}$.
  \item \textbf{Mid-Market Rate ($S_{\text{mid}}$):} The midpoint benchmark between wholesale bid and ask:
  \[
    S_{\text{mid}} = \frac{S_{\text{bid}} + S_{\text{ask}}}{2}.
  \]
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[Wholesale Spread and Retail Customer FX Margin in Basis Points]
Given wholesale quotes $S_{\text{bid}} < S_{\text{mid}} < S_{\text{ask}}$ and a retail rate $S_{\text{cust}}$ quoted to an end customer:
\[
  \text{Wholesale Bid--Ask Spread (bps)} = 10{,}000 \times \frac{S_{\text{ask}} - S_{\text{bid}}}{S_{\text{mid}}},
\]
\[
  \text{Retail FX Margin vs Mid (bps)} = 10{,}000 \times \frac{\abs{S_{\text{mid}} - S_{\text{cust}}}}{S_{\text{mid}}}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Decoding a ``Zero Transfer Fee'' Remittance Quote]
Suppose the wholesale interbank market for \texttt{USD/KES} quotes $S_{\text{bid}} = 129.35$ and $S_{\text{ask}} = 129.65$, giving a mid-market rate of $S_{\text{mid}} = 129.50\text{ KES}$ per \texttt{USD} (a wholesale spread of $0.30 / 129.50 \approx 23.2\text{ bps}$).

A consumer remittance app advertises \emph{``Zero Transfer Fee''} on US-to-Kenya sends while quoting the sender $S_{\text{cust}} = 126.90\text{ KES}$ per \texttt{USD}. When a user sends \$1,000.00:
\begin{itemize}
  \item At wholesale mid-market ($129.50$), \$1,000.00 corresponds to $129{,}500.00\text{ KES}$.
  \item At the retail quote ($126.90$), the recipient receives $126{,}900.00\text{ KES}$.
  \item The embedded retail FX markup is $2{,}600.00\text{ KES}$ (worth $2{,}600 / 129.50 = 20.08\text{ USD}$, or \$20.08 at mid-market):
  \[
    \text{Retail FX Margin} = 10{,}000 \times \frac{129.50 - 126.90}{129.50} = 10{,}000 \times \frac{2.60}{129.50} \approx 200.77\text{ bps} \approx 201\text{ bps } (2.01\%).
  \]
\end{itemize}
Charging \$0 upfront while widening the exchange rate by $201\text{ bps}$ collects \$20.08 on a \$1,000 transfer -- economically identical to quoting mid-market and charging a $2.01\%$ explicit fee.
\end{example}

\subsection{Cross-Rates Through a Vehicle Hub Currency}

Few banks make tight direct wholesale markets between two non-dollar currencies such as \texttt{GBP/KES} or \texttt{CAD/NGN}. Instead, liquidity routes through \texttt{USD} as a \textbf{vehicle currency} (\texttt{GBP} $\to$ \texttt{USD} $\to$ \texttt{KES}). When a customer sells $1\text{ GBP}$ to receive \texttt{KES}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item You sell $1\text{ GBP}$ into wholesale \texttt{GBP/USD} at the dealer's \emph{bid} $S_{\text{GBP/USD}}^{\text{bid}}$, yielding $S_{\text{GBP/USD}}^{\text{bid}}$ dollars.
  \item You sell those dollars into wholesale \texttt{USD/KES} at the dealer's \emph{bid} $S_{\text{USD/KES}}^{\text{bid}}$, yielding:
  \[
    S_{\text{GBP/KES}}^{\text{bid}} = S_{\text{GBP/USD}}^{\text{bid}} \times S_{\text{USD/KES}}^{\text{bid}}.
  \]
\end{enumerate}
Symmetrically, buying $1\text{ GBP}$ using \texttt{KES} crosses both dealer \emph{ask} sides: $S_{\text{GBP/KES}}^{\text{ask}} = S_{\text{GBP/USD}}^{\text{ask}} \times S_{\text{USD/KES}}^{\text{ask}}$. Cross-rate spreads therefore add approximately across both legs.

\section{Pre-Funded Liquidity Pools and the Working Capital Float Formula}

Now return to our first structural question: how does a remittance or cross-border B2B platform deliver local funds in under thirty seconds when wholesale international bank wires require \texttt{T+2} days?

\subsection{Decoupling User Execution from Wholesale Settlement}

Instant cross-border payment operators split the problem into two decoupled loops running on completely different clocks:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Synchronous Local Execution Loop ($< 30\text{ seconds}$):} The PSP maintains pre-funded domestic liquidity pools on both ends of the corridor -- for example, a UK safeguarding bank pool in \texttt{GBP} (at a clearing bank such as ClearBank or Modulr) and a Kenyan disbursement float pool in \texttt{KES} (held in M-Pesa B2C trust and local bank rails). When a sender in London pays $200\text{ GBP}$ via UK Faster Payments, the PSP verifies local receipt into its UK pool and immediately instructs its Kenyan pool to disburse $32{,}480\text{ KES}$ to the recipient. No money crossed the UK--Kenya border; one domestic balance rose in London while pre-existing corporate/safeguarded inventory fell in Nairobi.
  \item \textbf{The Asynchronous Treasury Replenishment Loop ($\tau\text{ days}$):} Left alone, an asymmetric corridor (where $95\%$ of volume flows from diaspora senders in London to recipients in Nairobi) accumulates excess \texttt{GBP} in London while draining the \texttt{KES} pool in Nairobi toward zero. Behind the scenes, the treasury team converts accumulated \texttt{GBP} into \texttt{USD} (or a dollar stablecoin) and settles wholesale value into Kenya to refill the \texttt{KES} disbursement pool before it runs dry.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Deriving the Working Capital Float Requirement}

How much capital must the operator lock up inside destination payout pools so instant disbursements never bounce with ``insufficient float''?

\begin{proposition}[Corridor Working Capital Float Bound]
Suppose a destination corridor experiences an average net daily outflow of $D$ (measured in USD equivalent per day), the end-to-end replenishment cycle from origin collection to cleared destination payout float takes $\tau$ days (including collection settlement lag and weekend or bank-holiday closures), and peak-day demand bursts require a safety multiplier $k \ge 1$ (typically $1.25 \le k \le 1.60$ around month-end payroll dates). Then the minimum trapped liquidity float $W$ required to avoid corridor stock-outs satisfies
\[
  W \ge k \cdot D \cdot \tau.
\]
\end{proposition}

Look at the leverage hidden inside the single parameter $\tau$. Suppose your UK-to-Kenya and US-to-Kenya corridors disburse $D = 500{,}000\text{ USD}$ per day with a payday buffer factor $k = 1.40$:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Traditional Correspondent Wire Cycle ($\tau = 3.5\text{ days}$):} Origin card/ACH collections settle \texttt{T+1}, SWIFT correspondent FX wires take \texttt{T+2}, and banking cutoffs from Friday 16:00 through Monday morning create a $64$-hour weekend dead zone during which mobile-money users keep sending remittances nonstop. With $\tau = 3.5\text{ days}$:
  \[
    W_{\text{wire}} = 1.40 \times \text{USD } 500{,}000 \times 3.5 = \text{USD } 2{,}450{,}000.
  \]
  You must immobilize \$2.45M of equity or debt working capital just to keep one \$500k/day corridor liquid.
  \item \textbf{24/7 Stablecoin and Local OTC Treasury Cycle ($\tau = 0.15\text{ days} \approx 3.6\text{ hours}$):} When settled fiat converts via an automated on-ramp into a dollar stablecoin (\texttt{USDT} or \texttt{USDC}), moves on-chain in under a minute even on Saturdays and bank holidays, and swaps via a local liquidity orchestrator into \texttt{KES} float within a few hours, the cross-border transit cycle shrinks to $\tau = 0.15\text{ days}$ (or $\tau = 0.50\text{ days}$ if origin instant-rail settlement batches twice daily):
  \[
    W_{\text{fast}} = 1.40 \times \text{USD } 500{,}000 \times 0.15 = \text{USD } 105{,}000 \quad (\text{or USD } 350{,}000 \text{ at } \tau = 0.50).
  \]
\end{enumerate}

Shrinking $\tau$ from $3.5\text{ days}$ to $0.15\text{--}0.50\text{ days}$ reduces trapped working capital by \textbf{$85.7\%$ to $95.7\%$}. Suppose the corridor earns $35\text{ bps}$ of net contribution margin on \$500,000/day (\$1,750/day, or roughly \$638,750/year). On \$2.45M of trapped capital, pre-opex Return on Capital Employed is $26.1\%$; on \$350k of capital, the exact same payment flow earns $182.5\%$ annualized return on liquidity -- or lets the startup support seven times the transaction volume without raising dilutive equity or expensive warehouse debt.

\section{Multi-Currency Double-Entry Accounting}

Now let us solve the second structural question: how does a double-entry database stay strictly balanced when a single business event touches \texttt{GBP} and \texttt{KES}?

\subsection{Currency Position Sub-Ledgers and Functional Reporting Currency}

Adding \texttt{1000 GBP} to \texttt{-162432 KES} produces nonsense because currency codes are incompatible physical dimensions. Standard multi-currency bank accounting (codified in IAS~21 and ASC~830) enforces conservation on two simultaneous planes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Native Currency Sub-Ledger Plane:} Every currency $c \in \{\text{USD}, \text{GBP}, \text{KES}, \text{NGN}, \dots\}$ is a closed double-entry universe. Cross-currency conversions never debit \texttt{GBP} and credit \texttt{KES} directly; instead, each currency leg posts against a dedicated bridge account called \textbf{\texttt{FX Position -- <CCY>}}. Thus, for every currency $c$ individually:
  \[
    \sum_{p \in \text{Batch}} \text{signed\_minor}(p, c) = 0.
  \]
  \item \textbf{Functional Reporting Currency Plane (\texttt{USD}):} The company designates one primary economic currency -- say \texttt{USD} -- as its \textbf{functional currency}. Every ledger posting carries both its native integer minor units and its \texttt{USD} functional equivalent translated at the wholesale spot mid-market rate at the moment of recognition (with \textbf{\texttt{FX Position Equivalent -- USD}} mirroring the native position accounts).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}[Multi-Currency Remittance Entry in Native and USD Functional Views]
Suppose wholesale mid-market rates are $\texttt{GBP/USD} = 1.2800$ and $\texttt{USD/KES} = 129.50$ (implying a wholesale mid cross-rate of $1.2800 \times 129.50 = 165.76\text{ KES/GBP}$). A UK sender pays $1{,}000.00\text{ GBP}$ at a ``zero upfront fee'' retail quote of $162.432\text{ KES/GBP}$, so the Kenyan recipient receives $162{,}432.00\text{ KES}$.

Translate both legs into functional \texttt{USD} at wholesale mid-market:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Inflow value of $1{,}000.00\text{ GBP}$ at $1.2800$: \$1,280.00.
  \item Wholesale mid value of $162{,}432.00\text{ KES}$ disbursed at $129.50$: $162{,}432.00 / 129.50 = \text{USD } 1{,}254.30$.
  \item Earned \textbf{Customer FX Spread Revenue}: $\text{USD } 1{,}280.00 - \text{USD } 1{,}254.30 = \text{USD } 25.70$ ($200.78\text{ bps}$).
\end{itemize}

Look at how the journal batch balances in \texttt{GBP}, in \texttt{KES}, and in functional \texttt{USD} simultaneously:

Verify what the position accounts tell treasury at a glance right after this trade: the company is now \textbf{long $1{,}000.00\text{ GBP}$} (worth $+$\$1,280.00) and \textbf{short $162{,}432.00\text{ KES}$} relative to its starting inventory (costing $-$\$1,254.30 to replace at current mid-market), with $+$\$25.70 locked in if treasury hedges immediately at $129.50$.

\subsection{Separating Commercial FX Revenue, Realized FX Slippage, and Unrealized Revaluation}

Confusing commercial spread with market movement hides treasury losses inside top-line revenue. A clean multi-currency chart of accounts separates FX into three distinct income-statement lines:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Customer FX Spread Revenue (Operating Revenue):} The commercial markup between the wholesale mid-market rate at trade execution and the rate quoted to the customer (\$25.70 in the example above). This belongs to the product's gross take rate.
  \item \textbf{Realized FX Gain / Loss (Treasury Execution Slippage):} Suppose treasury waits ten hours before selling its accumulated \texttt{GBP} to replenish the \texttt{KES} pool. During those ten hours, the shilling strengthens from $129.50$ to $128.20\text{ KES/USD}$. Buying $162{,}432.00\text{ KES}$ now costs $162{,}432.00 / 128.20 = \text{USD } 1{,}267.02$ instead of the \$1,254.30 booked at trade time. When treasury executes the replenishment trade and closes out the open \texttt{FX Position}, the extra \$12.72 cost posts to \texttt{Realized FX Loss} -- measuring exactly how much commercial margin treasury gave back by delaying execution.
  \item \textbf{Unrealized FX Revaluation Gain / Loss (Period-End Mark-to-Market):} At every daily or month-end close, accounting rules require revaluing all foreign-currency monetary assets and liabilities (\texttt{KES} float, \texttt{NGN} float, \texttt{GBP} cash, foreign payables) to the closing spot rate $S_{\text{close}}$, posting the delta to \texttt{Unrealized FX Revaluation Gain/Loss}.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{How Unhedged Local Float Wipes Out Thin Payment Margins.} Suppose your cross-border mobile money platform processes \$2,500,000 in monthly GTV at a solid $40\text{ bps}$ net contribution margin, generating \$10,000 of hard-won operating profit for the month. To avoid frequent treasury top-ups, the team leaves \$200,000 worth of unhedged corporate local-currency float sitting in \texttt{NGN} and \texttt{KES} disbursement accounts.

In the final week of the month, the local currency devalues by $5\%$ against \texttt{USD}. Month-end mark-to-market revaluation records an immediate unrealized loss of
\[
  0.05 \times \text{USD } 200{,}000 = \text{USD } 10{,}000.
\]
One $5\%$ currency move on an oversized unhedged float pool just erased $100\%$ of the month's $40\text{ bps}$ transaction profits across $50{,}000$ customer payments. Note the distinction: customer wallet balances backed 1-for-1 in the \emph{same} local currency ($F_{\text{KES}} = L_{\text{KES}}$) naturally offset on revaluation because both the asset and the liability shrink equally in \texttt{USD}; it is \emph{corporate} pre-funded float (owned by shareholders in \texttt{USD} equity) that bears full devaluation exposure.
\end{trapbox}

\subsection{Practical Treasury Risk Rules for Payment Engineers}

Four automated controls keep FX slippage and devaluation risk bounded without building a proprietary trading desk:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Natural two-way corridor netting:} Match inbound remittances (\texttt{USD -> KES}) against outbound merchant/importer collections (\texttt{KES -> USD}) internally at mid-market first, hedging only the net residual imbalance in the external wholesale market.
  \item \textbf{Hold working capital in hard currency / dollar stablecoins until the last mile:} Keep treasury reserves in \texttt{USD} (or regulated \texttt{USDT}/\texttt{USDC} treasury accounts) and convert into volatile emerging-market currencies in frequent micro-batches sized to the next $4\text{--}12\text{ hours}$ of payout velocity.
  \item \textbf{Automated net-open-position (NOP) limits:} Monitor $\abs{\text{FX Position}_c}$ continuously in real time; whenever unhedged exposure in any currency exceeds a hard threshold (for example, \$25,000 equivalent), trigger an automated API RFQ (Request for Quote) hedge swap.
  \item \textbf{Short quote TTLs and volatility circuit breakers:} Stamp every locked customer FX quote with a cryptographically signed expiry (\texttt{30--120 seconds}) and automatically widen retail spread buffers when wholesale feed variance spikes.
\end{itemize}

In Chapter~\ref{ch:btc-usdt-settlement}, we wire all of these concepts together -- walking step by step through how real operators combine fiat collection processors, Bitcoin or \texttt{USDT} treasury rails, and destination orchestrators like HoneyCoin to settle cross-border books cleanly.

\begin{exercise}[Explicit Transfer Fee vs Embedded FX Spread]
Wholesale mid-market is $\texttt{USD/KES} = 129.50$. Competitor~A charges a flat \$4.99 upfront fee and quotes $129.10\text{ KES/USD}$. Competitor~B advertises ``\$0 Fee'' and quotes $126.50\text{ KES/USD}$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For a sender spending exactly \$100, \$500, and \$2,000 total out of pocket (including any upfront fee), calculate how many Kenyan shillings reach the recipient on Competitor~A vs Competitor~B, and compute the total effective cost in basis points relative to mid-market for each ticket size.
  \item Solve algebraically for the exact out-of-pocket USD amount $M^*$ at which both apps deliver the exact same KES payout to the recipient.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Synthetic Cross-Rate Bid/Ask Construction]
An interbank liquidity feed quotes $\texttt{GBP/USD} = 1.2790\text{ / }1.2800$ (bid / ask) and $\texttt{USD/NGN} = 1{,}590.00\text{ / }1{,}602.00$ (bid / ask).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Construct the synthetic wholesale \texttt{GBP/NGN} bid rate, ask rate, mid-market rate, and full bid--ask spread in basis points.
  \item Which side of each underlying pair do you cross when a London sender pays \texttt{GBP} to disburse \texttt{NGN} in Lagos, and which side do you cross when a Nigerian business pays \texttt{NGN} to settle a \texttt{GBP} tuition invoice in Manchester?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Weekend Dead-Zone Float Sizing and Return on Capital]
A US-to-Ghana remittance corridor disburses an average of \$280,000/day Monday through Thursday and \$420,000/day on Friday, Saturday, and Sunday. Under traditional correspondent banking, wires initiated after Friday 15:00 UTC do not credit local \texttt{GHS} payout float until Tuesday 11:00 UTC ($92\text{ hours}$ later).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item With a peak-demand safety buffer $k = 1.25$, compute the minimum pre-funded \texttt{GHS} working capital float $W_{\text{wire}}$ required on Friday at 15:00 UTC to survive until Tuesday morning without rejecting payouts.
  \item Repeat the calculation if treasury replenishes via a 24/7 stablecoin-to-\texttt{GHS} OTC rail with end-to-end settlement latency of $4\text{ hours}$ ($\tau = 1/6\text{ day}$) even on weekends. By what percentage does required working capital drop?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Multi-Currency Position Sub-Ledger and Revaluation]
Your company's functional currency is \texttt{USD}. At 10:00 UTC, $\texttt{EUR/USD} = 1.0800$ (mid) and $\texttt{USD/KES} = 130.00$ (mid). A customer in Berlin sends $2{,}000.00\text{ EUR}$ at a retail cross-rate of $137.50\text{ KES/EUR}$ ($275{,}000.00\text{ KES}$ disbursed immediately from pre-funded \texttt{KES} float).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Write the balanced native (\texttt{EUR}, \texttt{KES}) and functional (\texttt{USD}) journal entries at 10:00 UTC using \texttt{FX Position} accounts, isolating exact \texttt{Customer FX Spread Revenue} in \texttt{USD}.
  \item At 17:00 UTC, treasury sells the $2{,}000.00\text{ EUR}$ for \texttt{USD} at $1.0785$ and buys $275{,}000.00\text{ KES}$ at $129.10\text{ KES/USD}$. Compute the exact \texttt{Realized FX Gain/Loss} and net cash retained in \texttt{USD}.
  \item Suppose separately that the company holds $26{,}000{,}000\text{ KES}$ of corporate payout float booked at $130.00\text{ KES/USD}$, and at month-end close \texttt{USD/KES} moves to $135.20$. Write the month-end \texttt{Unrealized FX Revaluation} journal entry in \texttt{USD}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
