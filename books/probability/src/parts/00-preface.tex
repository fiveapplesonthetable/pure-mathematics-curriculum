\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

There is a particular moment, familiar to anyone who has learned a piece
of mathematics well, when a formula stops being a string of symbols and becomes a
\emph{picture}. Before that moment you can plug numbers in and get the right
answer; after it, you can close your eyes and \emph{see} why the answer had to
come out that way. This book is written entirely in service of that second kind
of knowing.

Probability is rich in such moments because almost every result in the
subject connects directly to a concrete physical or conceptual experiment:
flipping a coin, drawing a card, waiting for a bus, or letting a process unfold.
The mathematics is a careful bookkeeping of \emph{what tends to happen when chance is in charge}. Our
approach is to keep that concrete activity in view at all times. You will watch
the dice before we write down a \emph{distribution} (the rule that lists every
possible outcome together with its chance of occurring).

\section*{What this book is for}
\label{ch00:what-for}

Most probability courses, and many fine textbooks, are organized around results
to be proved and formulas to be applied. You meet a counting formula, then a
distribution built from it, then the formulas for its average and its spread
(how widely the values vary from the center),
and you are asked to trust that this is all going somewhere. Often it is, but
the trust is doing a lot of the work, and trust is exactly what evaporates under
exam pressure or three years later when you need the idea.

If you
\emph{understand} where a formula comes from, you will rarely need to memorize
it, and you will be able to rebuild it from scratch when you have forgotten the
details. Understanding lasts
longer than memory. It also transfers. Take the \emph{law of large numbers}:
the fact that the average of many repeated trials settles down to the true
probability. If you see why it works, you can spot something wrong in a viral statistic,
a medical trial summary, or a trading strategy tested only on past prices.

\begin{focusbox}
The single organizing principle of this book: \textbf{intuition first, then
history or mechanism, then a real proof.} We never lead with the theorem. We
lead with the situation that makes the theorem feel inevitable, and only then do
we make it precise. If at any point a derivation feels like it appeared out of
nowhere, that is a bug in our writing, not a gap in your ability.
\end{focusbox}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: intuition before formulas.} Suppose you toss three fair coins (equally likely to land heads or tails) at once. Which is more common: all three showing the same face (all heads or all tails), or a mixed result with two of one face and one of the other? Before writing down any list, picture the toss: there are only two ways for all three to agree, but how many ways are there to pick the odd coin out in a mixed toss? (Three choices for which coin disagrees, times two choices for its face, gives six mixed outcomes against two unanimous ones, so the mixed result is three times as likely. Figure~\ref{ch00:three-coins} lists all eight outcomes.)
\end{recoverybox}

This is not a ``probability without tears'' book that hides the mathematics.
The proofs are here, and they are real. But a proof can be rigorous without
being dry, and it can be complete without becoming an unmotivated sequence of
symbols. Where a
standard text writes ``it follows that,'' we try to write the sentence that
tells you \emph{why} it follows.

\section*{How to read this book}
\label{ch00:how-to-read}

Read it with a pencil, and read it slowly. Probability punishes skimming more
than almost any other branch of mathematics, because so many of its errors are
\emph{plausible}. A wrong answer in calculus usually looks wrong; a wrong answer
in probability often looks completely reasonable until you simulate it (write a program to run the experiment many times) and watch
it fail. (This is why the subject is stuffed with paradoxes, results that
contradict common sense, and why we devote so much space to them.)

A few concrete suggestions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{When you meet a new claim, guess first.} Before reading our
    explanation of, say, the birthday problem (the chance that people in a room share a birthday), write down your gut estimate.
    Being wrong on purpose, and then seeing exactly where your intuition
    misfired, is the fastest way to upgrade that intuition permanently.
  \item \textbf{Treat every box as a different ``voice'' in a conversation.}
    Each answers a specific question you might be asking at that moment.
  \item \textbf{Do not skip the figures.} They carry part of the argument.
  \item \textbf{Simulate.} The surest cure for a
    shaky probabilistic intuition is twenty lines of code and ten thousand
    trials.
  \item \textbf{Do the exercises.} Every chapter ends with a set spanning
    computation, concept, and at least one proof.
\end{itemize}

\begin{marginnotebox}
You do not have to read in order, but the early chapters build on one
another. Chapters 1--9 form the foundation: sample spaces (the list
of all possible outcomes), conditioning (updating probabilities when new clues
arrive), random variables (numbers attached to chance outcomes), and expectation and
variance (the center of gravity and the spread of those numbers). After that the book
branches: continuous distributions and limit theorems, which describe what happens
when many random pieces are combined (Chapters
10--22); processes that unfold in time (Chapters 23--29); inference, which means
drawing conclusions from data, and modern
applications (Chapters 30--35); and a final part that makes the foundations
fully rigorous using measure theory
(the mathematics of assigning sizes to sets)
(Chapters 36--38). Two appendices collect the calculus, sums, and linear
algebra you may want to refresh.
\end{marginnotebox}

\section*{The visual language: a guide to the boxes}
\label{ch00:visual-language}

Throughout the book, certain remarks are pulled out into colored
boxes. Different questions deserve different answers. Once you learn the color
code you can follow the main thread or dip into the exact aside you want.
Here is the vocabulary, with a short example of each.

\begin{intuitionbox}
\textbf{Intuition} (green). The mental picture before the mathematics. Example:
``Variance measures how far a random quantity (a numerical value that depends on chance) typically lands from its average
-- it is the spread of the cloud, not its center.''
\end{intuitionbox}

\begin{whybox}
\textbf{Why it works} (blue). The reason a proof or formula works, stated as
plainly as we can manage. Example: write $\E[X]$ for the \emph{expectation} of a
random quantity $X$, its probability-weighted average. Then
$\E[X+Y]=\E[X]+\E[Y]$ for any two random quantities $X$ and $Y$, because
adding first and then averaging gives the same result as averaging first and
then adding. This holds even when $X$ and $Y$ are not \emph{independent}
(independent means that knowing one tells you nothing about the other).
\end{whybox}

\begin{happeningbox}
\textbf{What is really happening} (deep green). Zooms out from a calculation to
identify the general mechanism. Example: the \emph{central limit theorem} says
that adding up many small independent random pieces produces the same
bell-shaped distribution, whatever the pieces are. It is a fact about how
adding and averaging smooth out detail, not something special to coins or dice.
\end{happeningbox}

\noindent
\textbf{History} (yellow). Where an idea came from, and the human problem that
forced it into existence. Example: probability as mathematics began at
a gambling table, when the Chevalier de M\'er\'e (a French nobleman) asked Pascal
why a betting rule that had worked for him failed when rolling
pairs of dice. (The full History box for this preface comes after the
roadmap of the book, below.)

\begin{paradoxbox}
\textbf{Paradox} (red). A result that contradicts the answer almost everyone
gives -- stated sharply, then resolved. Example: switching doors on a game show
doubles your chance of winning, even though, after a door is opened, it
\emph{feels} like a coin flip. We will see exactly why the feeling is wrong.
\end{paradoxbox}

\begin{applicationbox}
\textbf{Application} (magenta). The idea at work in the world: machine
learning (using data to train algorithms to make predictions), A/B testing (showing two versions of a web page to randomly chosen
halves of the visitors and comparing the results), finance, genetics, signal
processing (analyzing and modifying signals like sound or radio waves). Example: Bayes' rule, the formula for updating a probability when
new evidence arrives, is used both to interpret a medical test result and to
decide whether an email is spam.
\end{applicationbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
\textbf{Connection} (teal). A bridge to the rest of mathematics, often pointing
to the companion \emph{Calculus From the Ground Up} and \emph{Linear Algebra
From the Ground Up}. Example: a probability density (a curve whose area represents
probability) is an integrand, the function inside an integral, and an
expectation is computed by integrating against it, so everything you know about
integration becomes a tool for computing averages.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{trapbox}
\textbf{Common trap} (red). A specific, tempting mistake -- placed exactly where
you are most likely to make it. Example: confusing $\P(A \given B)$ (the
probability of $A$ given $B$) with $\P(B \given A)$ (the probability of $B$
given $A$), the error behind many courtroom and medical blunders.
\end{trapbox}

\begin{walkbox}
\textbf{Walking question} (orange). A question to chew on while you walk away
from the page; we usually answer it shortly after. Example: ``If you keep
flipping a fair coin, must the number of heads and tails eventually equal out?''
(The answer is more surprising than it sounds.)
\end{walkbox}

\begin{labbox}
\textbf{Work it yourself} (purple). A short, hands-on task -- often a
simulation -- where you produce the insight rather than read it.
\end{labbox}

\begin{aestheticbox}
\textbf{Why this matters} (cyan). The longer-range payoff, so that no result
feels like an isolated trophy.
\end{aestheticbox}

There are a few more, \textsf{Focus}, \textsf{Formal}, \textsf{Reading note},
and \textsf{Pause}, whose names say what they do. You have already met
\textsf{Focus} and \textsf{Reading note} above. You do not need to memorize
the colors; you will learn them through use.

\section*{The arc: from a coin flip to measure theory}
\label{ch00:arc}

Where the book ends explains how it begins.

We begin (Chapter 1) with the simplest object there is: a single fair
coin. From it we extract the basic ideas of the subject:
outcomes, events (collections of outcomes, such as ``the die shows an even
number''), and the idea that probability is a kind of \emph{mass} (a total weight of $1$) spread over
the possibilities. Counting (Chapter 2) lets us handle situations with many equally
likely outcomes, and counting alone takes us surprisingly far. Chapter 3 states
the basic rules that every probability obeys. Then comes the
central skill of probabilistic reasoning: \emph{conditioning} (Chapters 4--5).
Learning to update a probability in the light of new information is, more than
any formula, a crucial skill.

With those foundations we introduce \emph{random variables} (Chapter 6) --
numbers attached to chance outcomes -- and the two summaries that compress a
whole distribution into a few telling numbers: \emph{expectation} (Chapter 8,
the balance point of the probabilities) and \emph{variance} (Chapter 9, the
typical squared distance from that balance point). From there probability goes
continuous (Chapters 10--12), where it becomes area under a curve and the bell
curve makes its entrance.

The middle of the book is about \emph{how randomness adds up}. When you combine
many random pieces (summing them, averaging them, letting time run), predictable
patterns appear. This is the territory of the law of large
numbers (averages settle onto the true probability) and the central limit
theorem (sums of many small independent pieces form a bell curve), treated in
Chapters 19--22. They are two of the most widely used facts in applied mathematics.
Figure~\ref{ch00:lln-preview} is a small preview: as we average more and more
coin flips, the running average is pulled, as if by gravity, toward the true
probability $1/2$.

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: how averages settle.} Suppose you toss a fair coin 100 times and obtain 60 heads -- a running proportion of $0.60$. In the next 100 tosses, you get an exactly balanced 50 heads. What is your new overall proportion of heads? Did the coin ``make up'' for the early heads with extra tails, or did the 100 new flips dilute the early deviation? (Check:
\[
  \frac{60 + 50}{200} = \frac{110}{200} = 0.55.
\]
The surplus of 10 heads remains, but its percentage impact was sliced in half. The law of large numbers operates by dilution, not compensation.)
\end{recoverybox}

After the limit theorems we let time enter the picture and study
\emph{processes} in Chapters 23--29. First come Markov chains (systems where the
next step depends only on where you stand now, not on past history) and the
Poisson process (random events arriving at a steady average rate, like raindrops
on a pavement). Next come random walks (a particle that steps left or right
according to successive coin flips), martingales (the mathematical description
of a fair game, where your expected fortune tomorrow equals your fortune today),
and Brownian motion (the jittery path of a particle pushed around by countless
tiny random collisions).
Then we turn the whole apparatus around and use it for \emph{inference} (Chapters
30--35): given observed data, what can we legitimately conclude about the
underlying process that generated it? This is where probability meets statistics,
machine learning, and data science (extracting knowledge from large sets of data).

Finally, in the capstone chapters (36--38), we return to the very beginning --
``what is a probability, really?'' -- and answer it properly, with measure
theory (the branch of mathematics that assigns rigorous sizes, areas, and volumes
to sets). This foundation makes everything earlier fully precise. We
have placed it last on purpose. Kolmogorov's axioms, the short list of basic
rules for probability that Andrey Kolmogorov set down in 1933, make sense only
\emph{after} you have felt, across thirty-five
chapters, the places where naive intuition needs a guardrail.

\end{aestheticbox}

\begin{historybox}
That spiral mirrors the history of probability, which spent its first two
centuries as a collection of gamblers' rules of thumb before finding a rigorous
mathematical foundation.

The subject began in Renaissance gambling halls. In 1564, the Italian physician,
astrologer, and compulsive gambler Gerolamo Cardano wrote \emph{Liber de Ludo
Aleae} (The Book on Games of Chance), the first systematic attempt to calculate
odds using sample spaces of equally likely die rolls. The modern mathematical
treatment began in the summer of 1654, in an exchange of letters between Blaise Pascal
and Pierre de Fermat. A French nobleman and gambler, Antoine Gombaud (the
Chevalier de M\'er\'e), had approached Pascal with two puzzles. First, de M\'er\'e
noticed that betting on at least one $6$ in $4$ rolls of a fair die was profitable,
yet betting on at least one double-$6$ in $24$ rolls of two dice was a steady
loser, contradicting his naive rule of proportions (since $24/36 = 4/6$). In
fact the first bet wins with probability
\[
  1 - \Bigl(\frac{5}{6}\Bigr)^4 \approx 0.518,
\]
and the second with only
\[
  1 - \Bigl(\frac{35}{36}\Bigr)^{24} \approx 0.491.
\]
Second,
he presented the old \emph{problem of points}: if two players agree to play a game
of chance over several rounds and are interrupted before either has won, how should
the prize money be split fairly based on the score so far? Pascal and Fermat solved
it by counting all the ways the unfinished game could have ended, which gave
the first mathematical treatment of expected value.

Over the next century, Christiaan Huygens published the first textbook on the
subject (1657), Jakob Bernoulli proved the first law of large numbers (published
1713, showing that observed frequencies settle onto the true probabilities), and Pierre-Simon
Laplace summed up the field in his 1812 treatise \emph{Th\'eorie
analytique des probabilit\'es}. Laplace defined probability classically: the
ratio of favorable outcomes to the total number of ``equally likely'' possibilities.
Yet this definition harbored a fatal circularity: what does ``equally likely''
mean, if not ``possessing equal probability''?

When mathematicians pushed probability from finite card games into infinite and
continuous spaces, the classical definition collapsed entirely. In 1889, Joseph
Bertrand demonstrated his famous chord paradox: if you pick a ``random chord''
across a circle, what is the probability that its length exceeds the side of an
inscribed equilateral triangle? Depending on whether you choose the chord's
endpoints uniformly on the circumference, choose its midpoint uniformly along a
radius, or choose its midpoint uniformly across the circle's area, the answer comes
out to $1/3$, $1/2$, or $1/4$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch00-bertrand}). Each method sounded equally reasonable; each produced
a different number. Without a rigorous definition of what a random choice meant,
probability risked becoming mere guesswork.

In 1900, David Hilbert presented twenty-three open problems in Paris. The sixth
asked for axioms for physics, and he named probability explicitly: it was to be
put on the same rigorous footing as geometry, built from a few stated basic
rules (axioms). A fierce controversy erupted in the 1920s.
The Austrian mathematician Richard von Mises insisted that probability was an
empirical science of repeatable physical experiments, defining probability as the
limiting relative frequency in an infinite sequence of trials (a ``collective'')
that resists any gambling system. But mathematicians could not construct such
sequences without encountering severe logical paradoxes. Meanwhile, logicians
and philosophers argued that probability was purely subjective -- a measure of
an observer's rational degree of belief.

In 1933, the thirty-year-old Soviet mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov broke the
deadlock with his short book \emph{Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung}
(Foundations of the Theory of Probability). Kolmogorov realized
that probability did not require a new philosophy of randomness. It was
already a branch of \emph{measure theory}, the theory of lengths, areas, and
volumes of general sets developed by \'Emile Borel and Henri Lebesgue.
A sample space is a set $\Omega$ (the Greek capital omega) containing all
possible outcomes; an event is a subset of $\Omega$ that can be given a size
(a \emph{measurable} subset); and the probability $\P$ assigns each event a
size that is never negative, gives all of $\Omega$ size $1$, and obeys the
rule that the probabilities of disjoint events (events that cannot happen
together) add.

Kolmogorov's axioms put the tools of modern analysis to work. Limit theorems,
martingales, Markov processes, Brownian motion, and the calculus of random
processes now had a rigorous foundation. Yet some foundational questions
remain open. Kolmogorov gave us the rules of probability, but not a single
interpretation of what a probability means. What is the probability of a
one-off event that can never be repeated, such as a nuclear conflict in a given
decade, or life evolving on another planet? In quantum mechanics, the
probabilities of combined experiments do not always follow the classical rules,
and physicists use a modified ``quantum probability''. And in computer science,
people still debate what makes a single sequence of bits truly random. We begin
where Pascal began, with the coin, but the road leads into modern mathematics.
\end{historybox}

\section*{Simulate, then trust, then verify}
\label{ch00:simulate}

If we could press one habit on you, it would be this: \textbf{when in doubt,
simulate.} To \emph{simulate} is to have a computer run a chance experiment
many times, using random numbers, and record what happens. A computer that
flips ten million coins in a second is a
reliable probability tutor. It has no intuition to fool and no formula
to misremember; it runs the experiment and reports what happened.

\begin{labbox}
Here, in a few lines of Python, is the entire Figure~\ref{ch00:lln-preview}
experiment: flip a fair coin and report the running fraction of heads after each
flip. Here \texttt{random.random()} returns a random number between $0$ and
$1$, so it is below $0.5$ half the time; we count that as heads. We will write
code in this spirit throughout the book.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import random
heads = 0
for n in range(1, 201):
    heads += (random.random() < 0.5)   # one fair flip
    print(n, heads / n)                # running fraction of heads
\end{lstlisting}
Run it. Watch the printed fraction swing wildly at first, then creep toward
$0.5$ and refuse to leave its neighborhood. (Push the loop to two hundred
thousand flips and it will hug $0.5$ even more tightly.) You have just witnessed
the law of large numbers with your own eyes, before we have proved a single
thing about it.
\end{labbox}

Simulation plays three distinct roles in this book, and keeping them
separate in your mind pays off.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Discovery.} When you do not yet have a guess, simulate to form
    one. The numbers will suggest a pattern; your job is then to explain it.
  \item \textbf{Verification.} When you have derived a formula, simulate to
    check it. If the theory and the simulation disagree, one of them is wrong,
    and finding out which is instructive.
  \item \textbf{Computation.} For problems too hard to solve exactly (and modern
    applications are full of them), simulation \emph{is} the answer. Chapter 35,
    on Monte Carlo methods (computing answers by repeated random simulation),
    rests entirely on this idea, a standard computational tool of modern science.
\end{enumerate}

Look at the order in our section title: simulate, \emph{then trust}, then
verify. There is a temptation, once you have a fast simulator, to stop
thinking and measure everything. Resist it. A simulation can tell you
\emph{that} something is true to high confidence; only a proof tells you
\emph{why}, and only the why generalizes. A measured probability of $0.4999$
across ten million flips is persuasive, but it is the \emph{argument} that the
true value is exactly $1/2$ -- by symmetry, with no appeal to any experiment at
all -- that you can carry into every other symmetric situation you ever meet.

\begin{happeningbox}
Simulation and proof complement each other by examining the same
problem in two ways. The simulation is your laboratory: it shows you what happens. The
proof is your theory: it explains why it had to happen. A good
probabilist moves between them, guessing with the laboratory, securing
with the theory, and trusting neither blindly. That habit, more than any list
of distributions, is what this book is trying to teach.
\end{happeningbox}

\begin{paradoxbox}
Untrained
probabilistic intuition is unreliable for everyone; the human brain did not
evolve to estimate chances. Consider: in a room
of 23 people, the chance that two share a birthday is already better than
even. Almost everyone guesses the number must be in the hundreds. Rather than
trusting raw intuition, we
train intuition on calculations and simulations, then check it by proof. By the end of this book your intuition will
be a far better instrument, precisely because you will have spent so much time
catching it in the act of being wrong.
\end{paradoxbox}

Here is the arithmetic behind the birthday claim.

\begin{example}[The birthday problem, worked once, slowly]
With $n$ people in a room and $365$ equally likely birthdays (ignoring leap
years), it is far easier
to compute the chance that \emph{nobody} shares a birthday and subtract from one.
Line the people up. The first person's birthday is free. The second must avoid
one taken day, so it is ``safe'' with probability $364/365$. The third must avoid
two, giving $363/365$, and so on. Multiplying these step-by-step chances
together (each one assumes the people before have all landed on different days)
gives
\[
  \P(\text{all distinct})
  = \frac{365}{365}\cdot\frac{364}{365}\cdots\frac{365-n+1}{365}
  = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\Bigl(1-\frac{k}{365}\Bigr),
\]
where $\P(\cdot)$ means ``the probability of'' and the symbol $\prod$ means
``multiply together the terms for $k=0,1,\dots,n-1$''.
The probability of \emph{at least one match} is then $1$ minus this product.
Plugging in $n=23$ gives $0.5073$, just over a coin flip. The reason it feels
impossible is that we instinctively count the $23$ people, when what matters is
the number of \emph{pairs} of people. That number is written $\binom{23}{2}$
(read ``23 choose 2'') and equals
\[
  \binom{23}{2} = \frac{23\cdot 22}{2} = 253,
\]
and $253$ chances to collide is a great many. Figure~\ref{ch00:birthday} shows
the whole curve.
\end{example}

\begin{recoverybox}
\textbf{Mental check: the pair explosion.} In a room with 5 people, every person shakes hands with every other person exactly once. How many handshakes take place? Now imagine a 6th person enters the room: how many \emph{new} handshakes do they make? Pairs outgrow people: 5 people make $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ pairs, 6 make $15$, and 10 make $45$ (Figure~\ref{ch00:handshakes}). Why does the count of pairs
\[
  \binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}
\]
grow roughly like the square of the crowd, $n^2/2$, rather than in step with $n$?
\end{recoverybox}

\section*{A note on rigor}
\label{ch00:rigor}

Two commitments guide how we present the mathematics. First, we state conditions
honestly: when a result requires assumptions, we say so, and when an argument is
only a plausibility sketch, we flag it and point to where the full
proof lives (often in Chapters 36--38). Second, we never let formality obscure
the idea. A definition must capture an idea you already half-see.

\begin{connectionbox}
This book leans on calculus (for probabilities as areas under curves and
averages computed as integrals) and on linear algebra (for tables that record
how many random quantities vary together, and for Markov chains). We have
written it to be self-contained, with the
appendices gathering what you need, but where a deeper treatment helps, we point
to the companion \emph{Calculus From the Ground Up} and \emph{Linear Algebra
From the Ground Up}, which share this book's philosophy and its box-colored
visual language. The three are meant to be read as a family.
\end{connectionbox}

\section*{Acknowledgments and an invitation}
\label{ch00:acknowledgments}

Every probability book stands on the shoulders of a few great ones, and the
debts of this one are easy to name. From Blitzstein and Hwang we learned that a
rigorous probability text can also be warm and story-driven. From Ross
we inherited the breadth of worked examples and applications. From Feller, the
conviction that the deep results deserve deep treatment. From Wasserman, the
insistence that all of this
must ultimately help us reason from data. 
Probability is the mathematics of
uncertainty. Learning it does not make the future predictable, but it makes the
future \emph{intelligible}: chance turns out to follow laws of its own, as firm
as gravity.

We claimed that adding and averaging ``smooth out detail.'' Here is what
that means. Take the total of a handful of dice. A single die
is flat: every face is equally likely. Add two dice and the distribution becomes a
tent, peaked at a total of $7$. Add five and it is already a recognizable bell. Nothing
about the die changed; the \emph{shape} came purely from adding independent
copies together. Figure~\ref{ch00:clt} shows this. To compare totals that live
on different ranges ($1$--$6$, $2$--$12$, $5$--$30$), each total is shifted so
its average sits at $0$ and squeezed so its typical spread is $1$; the heights are
then scaled so that each curve encloses the same area. This convergence is the
central limit theorem in miniature.

\section*{A few warm-up questions}
\label{ch00:warmup}

Here are five questions to carry into Chapter~1. Guess an answer for each.

\begin{exercise}[Guess before you learn]
\label{ch00:ex-guess}
For each, commit to an answer \emph{before} reading on in the book.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A fair coin is flipped $10$ times. Which exact sequence is more likely:
    \texttt{HHHHHHHHHH} or \texttt{HTHHTHTTHH}? (Careful.)
  \item In the same $10$ flips, are you more likely to get exactly $5$ heads, or
    some number of heads that is \emph{not} $5$?
  \item A test for a rare disease is ``$99\%$ accurate.'' You test positive. Off
    the top of your head, what is the chance you actually have the disease:
    closer to $99\%$, or closer to $10\%$?
  \item You wait at a bus stop where buses arrive ``on average every $10$
    minutes,'' at random times. You show up at a random moment. Do you expect to
    wait about $5$ minutes, or longer?
  \item Roll a fair die repeatedly until you see a $6$. On average, how many
    rolls will that take?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{walkbox}
Take question 1 on your next walk. Most people feel the second sequence is
``more likely'' because it looks random. But we asked for one \emph{specific}
sequence, and every specific sequence of $10$ flips has the same probability
$(1/2)^{10} = 1/1024$. The illusion comes from comparing ``ten heads'' against
``the huge \emph{family} of jumbled-looking sequences'' without noticing.
Keeping that distinction straight (a single outcome versus a class of
outcomes) is a large part of probability. Question 2 is its mirror image, and
your answers should disagree.
\end{walkbox}

\begin{exercise}[The arithmetic of collisions]
\label{ch00:ex-collisions}
Let $n$ be the number of people in a room.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Compute the exact number of pairs of people for $n = 10, 23, 50,$ and $100$ using
    \[
      \binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}.
    \]
  \item Write down the product formula for $\P(\text{all distinct birthdays})$ with $n=3$ people and evaluate it with a calculator. What is the chance that at least two of the three people share a birthday?
  \item Explain in your own words why having $253$ distinct pairs of people in a room of $23$ people makes a collision so much more probable than a naive guess of $23/365 \approx 6.3\%$ would suggest.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Writing your first simulation loop]
\label{ch00:ex-monte-carlo}
The text asserts that a fair coin flipped $200$ times has a running average that settles toward $0.5$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Using the Python snippet from Section~\ref{ch00:simulate} (or in your favorite programming language), simulate $1{,}000$ flips of a fair coin. Record the final fraction of heads.
  \item Repeat this $1{,}000$-flip experiment $20$ times. Do the resulting fractions scatter around $0.5$? What was the lowest and highest fraction observed?
  \item Now change the coin's bias so that heads appears with probability $p = 0.70$. Does the running fraction still settle, and to what value? How does this illustrate the \emph{frequentist} interpretation of probability, which defines the probability of an event as the long-run fraction of trials in which it happens?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[The four stages in your own words]
\label{ch00:ex-four-stages}
Looking back at the roadmap of Figure~\ref{ch00:book-roadmap}, explain why the book begins with discrete coins and dice before introducing continuous curves, and why measure theory appears as a capstone rather than at the front door. What would happen if a textbook began on page 1 with the formal set-theoretic machinery of measure theory, without first building intuition from dice?
\end{exercise}

Turn the page. In Chapter~1, \emph{What Probability Really Is}, we begin with a
single coin toss and ask what the number $1/2$ is
saying about the world.

\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{The compact version of everything above.} Build intuition first.
Distrust your untrained gut, especially when it feels most certain. Simulate to
discover and to check, but prove to understand and to generalize. Read the
boxes, draw the pictures, do the exercises. Everything in the book, from
the central limit theorem to Brownian motion to machine learning, is built on
the principles introduced with a single fair coin.
\end{focusbox}
